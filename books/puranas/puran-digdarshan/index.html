Chapter 1 · Introduction to the Purāṇas

Printed pp. 50–54

1 · The word “Purāṇa”

A name can reveal the qualities or defects of the thing it names. Since ancient times, people have tried to choose names that express a thing’s character as fully as possible. Authors and poets therefore give careful thought to the titles of their works, hoping that a short name will convey the work’s subject. The names of many Sanskrit works do just this: Veda, Upaniṣad, Smṛti, and Purāṇa each announce something of their subject. Later works such as the Raghuvaṃśa, Kumārasaṃbhava, and Mudrārākṣasa follow the same practice.

The author extends this principle to the names of epic characters. He presents Rāma as one who dwells in the hearts of living beings, and Rāvaṇa as one who makes them weep. Yudhiṣṭhira is praised for remaining steady through the Mahābhārata war and its moral crises. Duryodhana is described as a warrior drawn to unjust conflict; Bhīma as formidable; and Arjuna as a hero whose qualities win others over. Duḥśāsana, the author says, is aptly named for the man who tried to disgrace Draupadī in the assembly. These examples, he argues, show how names were chosen to match character.

He contrasts that older ideal with people whose names may not fit them: a “Vidyāsāgara” (ocean of learning), “Vidyādhara” (bearer of knowledge), or “Vedaprakāśa” (light of the Veda) may be ignorant, while a man called “Bahādura Siṃha” (brave lion) may be frightened by the sound of mice. The point, he says, is that ancient authors were especially careful when naming their works. He therefore begins by examining the derivation of the word Purāṇa.

The author says the word is formed from the indeclinable purā (“formerly”), the root ṇī (“to obtain”), and the affix ḍa. He also gives an alternative derivation from purā with the sense “what existed before.” He cites Pāṇini’s Aṣṭādhyāyī 4.3.22, 2.1.49, 4.3.105, 2.1.46, and 8.4.2 in support, then turns to Yāska’s Nirukta.

पुराणं कस्मात्? पुरा नवम् भवति।

purāṇaṃ kasmāt? purā navam bhavati.

“Why is it called Purāṇa? Because what was new in former times is now old.” In ordinary terms, something ancient is called purāṇa. The author lists pratna, purātana, and cirantana as related words for “ancient” or “old.”

He next cites the traditional definition:

पुराणं पञ्चलक्षणम्।

purāṇaṃ pañcalakṣaṇam.

“A Purāṇa has five defining characteristics.” The five are creation (sarga), secondary creation (pratisarga), genealogies (vaṃśa), the eras of the Manus (manvantara), and the accounts of notable persons (vaṃśānucarita).

The author argues that this definition fits the Purāṇas available in his day. He maintains that no other body of literature gives the same broad, connected account of creation and the world. He points to descriptions of the sun, moon, planets, constellations, and stars, and concludes that the Purāṇas preserve knowledge reaching back before the appearance of human beings. This is the argument he develops in the opening section.

2 · The eighteen Mahāpurāṇas

From the beginning of creation to the time of dissolution, the author says, the Purāṇas give an ordered history of the universe and a detailed account of its essential transformations, past and future. He describes them as scientific yet accessible, and as living, speaking representations of Ārya culture. He writes that even their opponents, amazed by their scope, have been compelled to call them “real encyclopedias.” He then gives the eighteen names:

ब्राह्मं पाद्मं वैष्णवं च शैवं लैङ्गं सगारुडम् ।
नारदीयं भागवतमाग्नेयं स्कान्दसंज्ञितम् ॥
भविष्यं ब्रह्मवैवर्तं मार्कण्डेयं सवामनम् ।
वाराहं मात्स्यं कौर्मं च ब्रह्माण्डाख्यमिति त्रिषट् ॥

brāhmaṁ pādmaṁ vaiṣṇavaṁ ca śaivaṁ laiṅgaṁ sa-gāruḍam
nāradīyaṁ bhāgavatam āgneyaṁ skānda-saṁjñitam
bhaviṣyaṁ brahma-vaivartaṁ mārkaṇḍeyaṁ sa-vāmanam
vārāhaṁ mātsyaṁ kaurmaṁ ca brahmāṇḍākhyam iti tri-ṣaṭ

“The Brahma, Padma, Viṣṇu and Śiva; the Liṅga together with the Garuḍa; the Nārada, Bhāgavata, Agni and Skanda; the Bhaviṣya, Brahmavaivarta, Mārkaṇḍeya and Vāmana; the Varāha, Matsya, Kūrma, and the one called Brahmāṇḍa—thus, three times six: eighteen.”

In order, the eighteen are the Brahma, Padma, Viṣṇu, Śiva, Liṅga, Garuḍa, Nārada, Bhāgavata, Agni, Skanda, Bhaviṣya, Brahmavaivarta, Mārkaṇḍeya, Vāmana, Varāha, Matsya, Kūrma, and Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇas.
