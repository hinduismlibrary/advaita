ऋग्वेदो यजुर्वेदः सामवेदोऽथर्ववेद इति वेदाश्चत्वारः । शिक्षा व्याकरणं निरुक्तं छन्दो ज्यौतिषं कल्प इति वेदाङ्गानि षट् । पुराणन्यायमीमांसा धर्मशास्त्राणि चेति चत्वार्युपाङ्गानि । अत्रोपपुराणानामपि पुराणेऽन्तर्भाव:, वैशेषिकशास्त्रस्य न्याये, वेदान्तशास्त्रस्य मीमांसायाम्, महाभारतरामायणयोः सांख्यपातञ्जलपाशुपतवैष्णवादीनां च धर्मशास्त्रे; एवं मिलित्वा चतुर्दश विद्याः । तथा चोक्तं याज्ञवल्क्येन-- 'पुराणन्यायमीमांसाधर्मशास्त्राङ्गमिश्रिताः । वेदाः स्थानानि विद्यानां धर्मस्य च चतुर्दश' इति । एता एव चतुर्भिरुपवेदैः सहिता अष्टादश विद्या भवन्ति । आयुर्वेदो धनुर्वेदो गान्धर्ववेदोऽर्थशास्त्रं चेति चत्वार उपवेदाः । सर्वेषां चास्तिकानामेतावन्त्येव शास्त्रप्रस्थानानि; अन्येषामप्येकदेशिनामेतेष्वेवान्तर्भावात् । ननु-- नास्तिकानामपि प्रस्थानान्तराणि सन्ति; तान्येतेष्वनन्तर्भावात्पृथग्गणयितुमुचितानि । तथा हि--शून्यवादेनैकं प्रस्थानं माध्यमिकानाम् । क्षणिकविज्ञानमात्रवादेनान्यद्योगाचाराणाम् । ज्ञानाकारानुमेयक्षणिकबाह्यार्थवादेनापरं सौत्रान्तिकानाम् । प्रत्यक्षसलक्षणक्षणिकबाह्यार्थवादेनापरं वैभाषिकाणाम् । एवं सौगतानां प्रस्थानचतुष्टयम् । तथा देहात्मवादेनैकं प्रस्थानं चार्वाकाणाम् । एवं देहातिरिक्तदेहपरिमाणात्मवादेन द्वितीयं प्रस्थानं दिगम्बराणाम् । एवं मिलित्वा नास्तिकानां षट् प्रस्थानानि । तानि कस्मान्नोच्यन्ते ?- सत्यम्; वेदबाह्यत्वात्तेषां म्लेच्छादिप्रस्थानवत्परम्परयापि पुरुषार्थानुपयोगित्वादुपेक्षणीयत्वमेव । इह च साक्षाद्वा परम्परया वा पुमर्थोपयोगिनां वेदोपकरणानामेव प्रस्थानानां भेदो दर्शितः । ततो न न्यूनत्वशङ्कावकाशः ॥  अथ संक्षेपेणैषां प्रस्थानानां स्वरूपभेदे हेतुः प्रयोजनभेद उच्यते बालानां व्युत्पत्तये--

There are four Vedas: Ṛg, Yajur, Sāma, and Atharva. The six Vedāṅgas are phonetics, grammar, etymology, meter, astronomy, and ritual procedure. The four auxiliary branches are Purāṇa, logic, Mīmāṃsā, and Dharmaśāstra. The minor Purāṇas are included within the Purāṇas, Vaiśeṣika within logic, Vedānta within Mīmāṃsā, and the Mahābhārata, Rāmāyaṇa, Sāṅkhya, Pātañjala, Pāśupata, Vaiṣṇava, and related teachings within Dharmaśāstra; together these are fourteen disciplines. Yājñavalkya says that the Vedas, with Purāṇa, logic, Mīmāṃsā, Dharmaśāstra, and the Vedāṅgas, are the fourteen seats of knowledge and dharma. With four Upavedas they make eighteen fields: Ayurveda, Dhanurveda, Gāndharvaveda, and Arthaśāstra. These are the scriptural approaches of all who accept the Veda; adherents of partial positions are included among them. One may object that those who reject the Veda have other approaches and should be separately counted. The Buddhists have four: Mādhyamika emptiness; Yogācāra momentary cognition alone; Sautrāntika inference of a momentary external object having the form of cognition; and Vaibhāṣika direct perception of a momentary external object with its own characteristic. The Cārvākas hold that the body is the self; the Digambaras hold that the self, distinct from the body, is measured like the body. Thus the non-Vedic have six approaches. Why are these omitted? Because they stand outside the Veda and are judged here, like the approaches of outsiders, not to serve the human goal even indirectly. This account describes the approaches that serve the human goal directly or indirectly and are aids to the Veda; therefore there is no cause to suspect incompleteness. Now the differences in nature and purpose of these systems are briefly explained for the understanding of beginners.

Section 3 · The Veda and its mantras

3

तत्र धर्मब्रह्मप्रतिपादकमपौरुषेयं प्रमाणवाक्यं वेदः । स च मन्त्रब्राह्मणात्मकः । तत्र मन्त्रा अनुष्ठानकारकभूतद्रव्यदेवताप्रकाशकाः । तेऽपि त्रिविधा ऋग्यजुःसामभेदात् । तत्र पादबद्धगायत्र्यादिच्छन्दोविशिष्टा ॠचः 'अग्निमीळे पुरोहितम्' इत्याद्याः । ता एव गीतिविशिष्टाः सामानि । तदुभयविलक्षणानि यजूंषि । 'अग्नीदग्नीन्विहर' इत्यादिसंबोधनरूपा निगदमन्त्रा अपि यजुरन्तर्भूता एव । तदेवं निरूपिता मन्त्राः ॥

The Veda is an authorless sentence of valid knowledge that teaches dharma and Brahman. It consists of mantras and Brāhmaṇas. The mantras reveal the substances and deities that are factors in ritual performance. They are threefold as Ṛc, Yajus, and Sāman. Ṛcs, such as “I praise Agni, the household priest,” are marked by meters such as Gāyatrī and arranged in metrical lines. Those same verses, when set to melody, are Sāmans. Yajuses differ from both. Addressed prose mantras such as “O Agnīd, arrange the fires” are also included among Yajuses. Thus the mantras have been described.

The Brāhmaṇa portion is threefold: injunction, explanatory passage, and what differs from both. The Bhāṭṭas call injunction verbal prompting; the Prābhākaras call it an enjoining; logicians and others call it the means to what is desired. Injunction is fourfold—origination, eligibility, application, and performance. An origination injunction, such as “An eight-pot cake for Agni is to be prepared,” teaches only the form of a rite. An eligibility injunction, such as “One desiring heaven should sacrifice with the new- and full-moon rites,” teaches the connection of a result with undertaking the sacrifice and its required acts. An application injunction, such as “He should sacrifice with rice grains” and “He offers the firewood,” teaches an auxiliary's relation to a rite. A performance injunction, formed by combining the preceding three, teaches the unity of performing a principal rite together with all its parts; some hold it is enjoined by revelation, others that it is derived. Ritual action itself is twofold: subordinate action and result-directed action. Subordinate action is prescribed with reference to the factors of a sacrifice. It is fourfold as origination, acquisition, transformation, and refinement. In “In spring a Brāhmaṇa should establish the fires” and “He shapes the sacrificial post,” the fires and post arise with particular refinements through establishing or shaping. In “Vedic study is to be undertaken” and “He milks the cow for its milk,” what already exists—the Veda or milk—is obtained through studying or milking. In “He presses the Soma,” “He pounds the rice,” and “He melts the ghee,” the materials are transformed by pressing, pounding, and melting. In “He sprinkles the rice” and “The wife looks at it,” materials are refined by sprinkling, looking, and similar acts. These four are auxiliaries. Result-directed ritual action, prescribed with reference to sacrificial factors, is twofold: auxiliary and principal. An auxiliary serves another purpose; the principal serves no other purpose. Auxiliaries are also twofold: those that help by direct contact and those that help from a distance. The first sustains the principal rite's proper form; the second helps its result. An injunction with all auxiliaries complete is a prakṛti; one with some lacking is a vikṛti; a dārvi-homa, distinct from both, is a third case. Other cases are to be inferred similarly. Thus the injunction section is described. A sentence subordinate to an injunction and marked by praise or blame is arthavāda. It is threefold: guṇavāda, anuvāda, and bhūtārthavāda. Guṇavāda conveys a meaning contradicted by another means of knowledge, as in “The sun is the sacrificial post.” Anuvāda conveys what another means has already established, as in “Fire is a remedy for cold.” Bhūtārthavāda conveys a meaning neither contradicted nor already established by another means, as in “Indra raised the thunderbolt against Vṛtra.” It is said: “When there is contradiction, it is guṇavāda; what is established is anuvāda; what remains after these is held to be bhūtārthavāda.” Though all three kinds of arthavāda praise an injunction, bhūtārthavāda is authoritative about its own meaning as well, according to the reasoning of the Devatā topic. Validity is revealing what is unknown and not sublated. That does not apply to guṇavāda and anuvāda, respectively because their subject is contradicted or already known. The ordinary validity of bhūtārthavāda is not lost even when its own meaning is not its primary purport. A Vedānta statement differs from injunction and arthavāda. Though it reveals something unknown, it does not prescribe action and so is not an injunction. It is self-valid concerning Brahman, the supreme human goal whose nature is knowledge and highest bliss; its purport is established by six interpretive indicators, such as opening and conclusion. It makes all injunctions serve it through purification of the inner instrument, and it is subordinate to no other purpose; therefore it is not arthavāda. Thus a Vedānta statement is distinct from both. Sometimes it is called an injunction merely because it reveals the unknown; elsewhere it is treated as bhūtārthavāda because it is an authoritative sentence without an injunction word. There is no fault in this usage. Thus the three kinds of Brāhmaṇa have been described. The Veda, consisting of ritual and Brahman sections, is a means to dharma, prosperity, desire, and liberation.

Because Ṛg mantras have distinctive meters arranged in metrical feet, ignorance of them is censured, and particular rites are prescribed according to particular meters, knowledge of meter is sought. The sage Piṅgala composed an eight-chapter treatise on prosody to teach it. Three chapters set out the seven Vedic meters—Gāyatrī, Uṣṇih, Anuṣṭubh, Bṛhatī, Paṅkti, Triṣṭubh, and Jagatī—with their subdivisions. Five chapters beginning “now the worldly” also describe ordinary meters useful in Purāṇas and histories, as occasion arises, just as grammar treats ordinary words.

Mīmāṃsā is twofold: ritual Mīmāṃsā and inquiry into the embodied Self, Brahma-Mīmāṃsā. The twelve-chapter ritual Mīmāṃsā, beginning “Now, therefore, inquiry into dharma” and ending “and because it is seen in the Anvāhārya offering,” was composed by Jaimini. Its chapters treat in order: the means of knowing dharma; distinctions and non-distinctions of dharma; principal and subsidiary; differences in application according to whether a rite serves the sacrifice or the person; sequence based on Vedic statement, meaning, and recitation; eligibility; general extension; specific extension; adaptation; exclusion; coordinated application; and contextual application. Jaimini also composed the four-chapter Saṃkarṣaṇa section. Though known as the Devatā section, it belongs to ritual Mīmāṃsā because it teaches ritual action called worship. The four-chapter Brahma-Mīmāṃsā, beginning “Now, therefore, inquiry into Brahman” and ending “There is no return, because of scripture,” was composed by Bādarāyaṇa. It presents reasoning for listening, which leads to direct realization of the identity of the individual self and Brahman. Its first chapter establishes that all Vedānta statements have as their purport, directly or indirectly, nondual Brahman, identical with the innermost Self. Its first section examines statements with clear indications of Brahman; the second, unclear indications concerning Brahman as an object of meditation; the third, mostly unclear indications concerning Brahman to be known. The fourth examines words such as “unmanifest” and “unborn,” which might seem to refer chiefly to another subject. The second chapter resolves apparent conflict with remembered teachings and reasoning; this is non-contradiction. Its first section resolves conflict with Sāṅkhya, Yoga, Kaṇāda, and related teachings; the second shows defects in those views, since inquiry establishes its own view and refutes the opposing view. The third resolves apparent conflict among Vedic statements about the creation of the great elements and, later, about the individual self. The fourth resolves conflict among statements about the senses and their objects. The third chapter teaches the means: its first section cultivates dispassion by describing the self's departure to and return from other worlds; its second analyzes the meanings of “you” and “that”; its third gathers parallel statements about unqualified Brahman and discusses combining or not combining qualities across Vedic branches; its fourth sets out external means such as life-stage duties and sacrifices, and internal means such as tranquility, restraint, listening, and contemplation. The fourth chapter determines the distinct results of meditation on qualified and unqualified Brahman. Its first section teaches liberation while living through repeated listening and realization of unqualified Brahman, with no further taint of merit or demerit. Its second considers departure at death; the third describes the northern path of the deceased knower of qualified Brahman; the fourth describes disembodied liberation for the knower of unqualified Brahman and residence in Brahma's world for the knower of qualified Brahman. This is the crown of all śāstras, and all other śāstras are subordinate to it. Seekers of liberation should honor it in the manner taught by Śrī Śaṅkara Bhagavatpāda. This is the secret.

Its purpose is dispassion toward sense-objects: even enjoying them according to the treatise ends only in suffering. Medical science aims at knowledge of disease, its causes, cessation, and the means of cessation.

एवं धनुर्वेदः पादचतुष्टयात्मको विश्वामित्रप्रणीतः । तत्र प्रथमो दीक्षापादः, द्वितीयः संग्रहपादः, तृतीयः सिद्धिपादः, चतुर्थः प्रयोगपादः । तत्र प्रथमे पादे धनुर्लक्षणमधिकारिनिरूपणं च कृतम् । अत्र धनुःशब्दश्चापे रूढोऽपि धनुर्विधायुधे प्रवर्तते । तच्चतुर्विधं मुक्तममुक्तं मुक्तामुक्तं यन्त्रमुक्तं चेति । मुक्तं चक्रादि । अमुक्तं खड्गादि । मुक्तामुक्तं शल्यावान्तरभेदादि । यन्त्रमुक्तं शरादि । तत्र मुक्तमस्त्रमुच्यते । अमुक्तं शस्त्रमित्युच्यते । तदपि ब्राह्मवैष्णवपाशुपतप्राजापत्याग्नेयादिभेदादनेकविधम् । एवं साधिदैवतेषु समन्त्रकेषु चतुर्विधायुधेषु येषामाधिकारः क्षत्रियकुमाराणां तदनुयायिनां च, ते सर्वे चतुर्विधाः पदातिरथगजतुरगारूढा इति । दीक्षाभिषेकशकुनमङ्गलकरणादिकं च सर्वमपि प्रथमे पादे निरूपितम् । सर्वेषां शस्त्रविशेषाणामाचार्यस्य च लक्षणपूर्वकं संग्रहणप्रकारो दर्शितो द्वितीयपादे । गुरुसंप्रदायसिद्धानां शस्त्रविशेषाणां पुनः पुनरभ्यासो मन्त्रदेवतासिद्धिकरणमपि निरूपितं तृतीयपादे । एवं देवतार्चनाभ्यासादिभिः सिद्धानामस्त्रविशेषाणां प्रयोगश्चतुर्थपादे निरूपितः । क्षत्रियाणां स्वधर्मयुद्धाचरणं दुष्टस्य दण्डश्चोरादिभ्यः प्रजापालनं च धनुर्वेदस्य प्रयोजनम् । एवं च ब्रह्मप्रजापत्यादिक्रमेण विश्वामित्रप्रणीतं धनुर्वेदशास्त्रम् ॥

Dhanurveda, composed by Viśvāmitra, has four sections: consecration, collection, accomplishment, and application. The first describes the bow and the qualified practitioner. Though dhanus commonly means bow, here it means weapons generally. Weapons are fourfold: released, unreleased, both released and unreleased, and mechanically released. A discus is released; a sword unreleased; a dart or spear of mixed type; an arrow mechanically released. Released weapons are called astras, unreleased weapons śastras. They are of many kinds, such as Brahma, Vaiṣṇava, Pāśupata, Prājāpatya, and Āgneya. Warrior princes and their followers are qualified to use the fourfold, mantra-endowed weapons; they are infantry, chariot, elephant, and horse riders. The first section also explains consecration, anointing, omens, and auspicious acts. The second explains collecting weapons and identifying their teachers. The third explains repeated practice of weapons mastered in a teacher's lineage and mastery of their mantras and deities. The fourth explains deploying weapons perfected through worship and practice. Dhanurveda's purpose is for warriors to perform their duty in battle, punish wrongdoers, and protect people from thieves and others. Thus Viśvāmitra taught this Dhanurveda through the lineage beginning with Brahmā and Prajāpati.

Its purpose is knowledge that distinguishes Prakṛti from Puruṣa.

The Yoga system was composed by Patañjali in four sections, beginning “Now, the teaching of Yoga.” The first explains samādhi as restraint of the mind's movements and its means, practice and dispassion. The second teaches eight limbs for attaining samādhi even with a distracted mind: restraint, observances, posture, breath control, withdrawal, concentration, meditation, and samādhi. The third treats yogic powers; the fourth, liberation as isolation. Its purpose is steady contemplation through restraining thoughts different from the intended object. The Pāśupata scripture of Paśupati was composed in five chapters to free the soul from Paśupati's bonds, beginning “Now we shall explain the method of Pāśupata yoga.” It explains the individual as the bound creature and effect, the Lord as cause, yoga as settling the mind in Paśupati, and observances such as bathing in ashes three times daily. Its purpose is liberation called the end of suffering. These are called effect, cause, yoga, observance, and the end of suffering.

Section 16 · Pāñcarātra

16

एवं वैष्णवं नारदादिभिः कृतं पञ्चरात्रम् । तत्र वासुदेवसंकर्षणप्रद्युम्नानिरुद्धाश्चत्वारः पदार्था निरूपिताः । भगवान्वासुदेवः सर्वकारणं परमेश्वरः । तस्मादुत्पद्यते संकर्षणाख्यो जीवः । तस्मान्मनः प्रद्युम्नः । तस्मादनिरुद्धोऽहंकारः । सर्वे चैते भगवतो वासुदेवस्यैवांशभूतास्तदभिन्ना एवेति भगवतो वासुदेवस्य मनोवाक्कायवृत्तिभिराराधनं कृत्वा कृतकृत्यो भवतीत्यादि च निरूपितम् ॥

The Vaiṣṇava Pāñcarātra was composed by Nārada and others. It explains four principles: Vāsudeva, Saṅkarṣaṇa, Pradyumna, and Aniruddha. Lord Vāsudeva is the supreme Lord and cause of all. From him arises the individual self called Saṅkarṣaṇa, from that the mind called Pradyumna, and from that the ego called Aniruddha. All are portions of Vāsudeva alone and not different from him. Worshipping Vāsudeva through mind, speech, and body makes one fulfilled.

Section 17 · Three accounts of causation and the conclusion

17

तदेवं दर्शितः प्रस्थानभेदः । सर्वेषां च संक्षेपेण त्रिविध एव प्रस्थानभेदः-- तत्रारम्भवाद एकः; परिणामवादो द्वितीयः; विवर्तवादस्तृतीय: । पार्थिवाप्यतैजसवायवी याश्चतुर्विधाः परमाणवो द्व्यणुकादिक्रमेण ब्रह्माण्डपर्यन्तं जगदारभन्ते, असदेव कार्यं कारकव्यापारादुत्पद्यते इति प्रथमस्तार्किकाणां मीमांसकानां च । सत्त्वरजस्तमोगुणात्मकं प्रधानमेव महदहंकारादिक्रमेण जगदाकारेण परिणमते, पूर्वमपि सूक्ष्मरूपेण सदेव कार्यं कारणव्यापारेणाभिव्यज्यते इति द्वितीयः पक्षः सांख्ययोगपातञ्जलपाशुपतानाम् । ब्रह्मणः परिणामो जगदिति वैष्णवानाम् । स्वप्रकाशपरमानन्दाद्वितीयं ब्रह्म स्वमायावशान्मिथ्यैव जगदाकारेण कल्पत इति तृतीयः पक्षो ब्रह्मवादिनाम् । सर्वेषां प्रस्थानकर्तॄणां मुनीनां विवर्तवादपर्यवसानेनाद्वितीये परमेश्वरे प्रतिपाद्ये तात्पर्यम् । न हि ते मुनयो भ्रान्ताः, सर्वज्ञत्वात्तेषाम् । किं तु बहिर्विषयप्रवणानामापाततः पुरुषार्थे प्रवेशो न संभवतीति नास्तिक्यवारणाय तैः प्रकारभेदाः प्रदर्शिताः । तत्र तेषां तात्पर्यमबुद्ध्वा वेदविरुद्धेऽप्यर्थे तात्पर्यमुत्प्रेक्षमाणास्तन्मतमेवोपादेयत्वेन गृह्णन्तो जना नानापथजुषो भवन्तीति सर्वमनवद्यम् ॥

Thus the divisions of the approaches have been shown. In brief, all approaches reduce to three accounts: origination, transformation, and apparent manifestation. The first, held by logicians and Mīmāṃsakas, says four kinds of atoms—earthy, watery, fiery, and airy—begin the world up to the cosmic egg through successive pairs and combinations; the previously nonexistent effect arises through its causes. The second, held by Sāṅkhya, Yoga, Pātañjala, and Pāśupata systems, says Pradhāna, made of sattva, rajas, and tamas, transforms into the world through Mahat, ego, and the rest; the effect already exists subtly and is manifested through its cause. Vaiṣṇavas say the world is a transformation of Brahman. The third view, held by teachers of Brahman, says nondual Brahman, self-luminous and supreme bliss, appears falsely as the world through its own māyā. All sages who founded these systems ultimately teach the nondual supreme Lord through apparent manifestation. They are not confused, being omniscient. Since those inclined to external objects cannot immediately enter the human goal, the sages displayed different formulations to prevent denial of truth. People who misunderstand their intent and imagine they meant things opposed to the Veda, adopting such views as final, follow many paths. Thus the whole account is consistent.
