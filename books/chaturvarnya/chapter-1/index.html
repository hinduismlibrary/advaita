Birth-based varṇa

By the lexicon statement, “varṇa [is used] for a twice-born person and the like, and for white and the like,” the word varṇa is established in the sense of a jāti beginning with the twice-born. The word jāti, however, is indeed rooted in birth. For example, jātis such as lionhood are indeed rooted in birth.

The Brāhmaṇa was His mouth; the Rājanya was made His arms. His thighs were that which was the Vaiśya; from His feet the Śūdra was born.

By this mantra, the arrangement of the varṇas by birth is explicitly explained. Thus: the Brāhmaṇa was the mouth of this Lord whose form is the Great Cosmic Puruṣa; the meaning is, [he was] born from the mouth. The Rājanya was His arms, made [so] because of being born from the arms. The two thighs of this Puruṣa were the form of the Vaiśya. From the feet of this Lord the Śūdra was born.

Now, it may be objected here: because of the expression of co-reference in “the Brāhmaṇa [was] the mouth,” the Brāhmaṇa’s being the mouth of Him may be merely figurative. The same relation should likewise be understood for the Rājanya and the others. This is not so. From the concluding statement, “From His feet the Śūdra was born,” just as the Śūdra’s birth from the feet [is understood], the Brāhmaṇa and the others too must be understood as born from the mouth. Nor should it be said that co-reference is thereby inexplicable: it is established as in “clay-pot” and “gold-earring,” where there is co-reference by being produced from that. Therefore, the great sage Śaunaka, by saying “because it was produced from that,” stated that the Brāhmaṇas and the others take the form of the mouth and the rest precisely through being produced. The Taittirīya Śruti too, by its statement of creation, such as “He fashioned the Trivṛt from the mouth,” has stated the birth-origin of Brāhmaṇa-ness and the rest.

For the increase of the worlds, from the mouth, arms, thighs, and feet, he brought forth the Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, and Śūdra. Here Kullūka Bhaṭṭa [says]: “For the abundance of the worlds beginning with Bhū, he created the Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, and Śūdra from the mouth, arms, thighs, and feet, in that order. The Brāhmaṇa and the rest were created from the mouth and the rest.” Since the jāti status of Brāhmaṇa-ness and the rest is established for those alone who are born from the mouth and the rest, the well-known conclusion here too fits: jāti is by birth alone. For in the world it is well known that the jāti of lions and the rest is by birth alone. In that way, just as lions and the rest, by virtue of being jāti, are not rooted in qualities, actions, and the like, so too are the Brāhmaṇas and the rest.

The fourfold varṇa was created by Me according to the division of guṇa and karma. Know Me, though its maker, as the immutable non-agent.

Now, [it may be objected]: “By the Gītā’s statement, ‘The fourfold varṇa was created by Me according to the division of guṇa and karma,’ it is understood that the fourfold varṇa was created by guṇas and karmas alone.” No, because this arises from ignorance of the intended meaning. Thus: just as sticks and the like are instrumental in the production of a pot, guṇas and karmas and the like are said to be instrumental in the creation of the fourfold varṇa. Just as, when a pot has arisen, its continued state is not by sticks and the like, but the sticks and the like that existed before the pot’s production are causes of the pot; in the same way, when bodies of Brāhmaṇas and the rest are attained by birth in the wombs of Brāhmaṇas and the rest, guṇas and karmas are causes [of that attainment]. Therefore, only those guṇas and karmas by which the Lord made the bodies of Brāhmaṇas and the rest are stated here to be instrumental causes of the fourfold varṇa. And this is indeed accepted by followers of Sanātana Dharma, since every effect everywhere has guṇa and karma as its root. Therefore the Lord said, “The fourfold varṇa [was created] by Me,” as [were] the sky, Moon, Sun, and the rest. Just as the sky and the rest cannot be created by all, so too the Brāhmaṇas and the rest. But proponents of a fourfold-varṇa arrangement rooted in guṇa and karma desire the determination of Brāhmaṇa-ness and the rest by the guṇas and karmas belonging to those respective bodies after the arising of those respective bodies; that is not established by this statement.

Viśvāmitra account

Nor should it be said that Viśvāmitra, Vasiṣṭha, and others, whose distinction by another jāti is well known from scripture, underwent a change of jāti through the power of their own austerities, and that this must therefore be based on qualities, actions, and the like.

Rather, they were originally Brāhmaṇas and the like. Through the power of their austerities, the obstructing coverings were removed, and their Brāhmaṇa status and the like became manifest.

According to Vālmīki’s account of Viśvāmitra, the exchange of the Brāhmaṇa oblation prepared by the great sage Ṛcīka made it the material cause of Viśvāmitra’s body. Though he was a Brāhmaṇa, austerity and the like were used only to remove the defect of birth in a Kṣatriya womb.

As for Vasiṣṭha, he was indeed a Brāhmaṇa because he was a son of Brahmā. After Nimi’s curse, he was born from the seed of Mitra and Varuṇa, stirred on account of Urvaśī and deposited in a pot. This was merely divine birth and did not remove his Brāhmaṇa status. The matter concerning Devāpi will be stated later.

Śaṅkara’s view

The revered Commentator too stated the origins of the Brāhmaṇas and the others according to the gradations of the differences in the qualities beginning with sattva, intensified by actions.

The fourfold varṇa was created by Me according to the division of qualities and actions. Know Me, though its creator, as the immutable non-agent.

“The fourfold varṇa” means the four varṇas. “Created by Me” means produced by Me, the Lord, in accordance with the śruti, “The Brāhmaṇa was His mouth,” and so on. “According to the division of qualities and actions” means according to the division of qualities and the division of actions. The qualities are sattva, rajas, and tamas. Among these, the actions of one in whom sattva predominates are calmness, self-restraint, austerity, and the like. The actions of a Kṣatriya in whom rajas predominates, with sattva subordinate, are valor, vigor, and the like. The actions of a Vaiśya in whom rajas predominates, with tamas subordinate, are agriculture and the like. Service alone is the action of a Śūdra in whom tamas predominates, with rajas subordinate. Thus, the meaning is: “The fourfold varṇa was created by Me according to the division of qualities and actions.” And this fourfold varṇa does not exist in other worlds; hence the specification “in the human world.” This is the commentary of Śaṅkara there.

Here, the qualities beginning with calmness and self-restraint mentioned in the Gītā are stated to be natural to the Brāhmaṇas and the others; but that Brāhmaṇa-hood and the like are based on them is not within the purport of the Lord’s words. Therefore, just as a lion born from a lioness, endowed with qualities such as valor and fierceness, is a primary lion, so among the Brāhmaṇas and the others, those born from Brāhmaṇas and the others and endowed with qualities appropriate to each varṇa, such as calmness and self-restraint, are the primary Brāhmaṇas and the others. Brāhmaṇas and the others who lack the natural qualities and are dependent on birth alone are Brāhmaṇas by birth alone; but those who lack such birth and are endowed with qualities beginning with calmness and self-restraint are Brāhmaṇas and the others only in a secondary sense, by virtue of qualities. Just as a lion lacking qualities such as valor is a lion by kind, while Devadatta and the like, lacking lion birth but endowed with valor and the like, are lions in a secondary sense, so too it is to be understood here.

As the Mahābhāṣyakāra said: “Austerity, sacred learning, and birth make a Brāhmaṇa. He who is deficient in austerity and sacred learning is a Brāhmaṇa by birth alone.”

Purāṇic agreement

Here some say that no accounts are heard in the Purāṇas of any Brāhmaṇas being born from the mouth of the Supreme Lord. Rather, in the Bhāgavata and other Purāṇas, by statements such as “Nārada was born from a limb,” the birth of those particular Brāhmaṇas from Prajāpati’s thumb and other limbs is heard. That is trivial, because in the Mahābhārata and other texts the birth of hundreds of pairs of Brāhmaṇas and the others from the mouth and the other limbs is described. Even if this is so of the original Brāhmaṇas, in other cases what is intended is precisely their origin from the powers of the mouth and the other limbs.

“The Lord created one hundred foremost Brāhmaṇas from His mouth alone; from His arms one hundred Kṣatriyas, and from His thighs one hundred Vaiśyas. From His feet too Keśava created one hundred Śūdras, O best of the Bharatas.”

Here, in statements such as “The Brāhmaṇa was His mouth,” the words “mouth” and the like refer only to the respective primary powers. Thus Brāhmaṇas arise from the power of the mouth, and Kṣatriyas from the power of the arms. Although these beings and trees and the like originate by the power of resolve and are not directly womb-born, since birth from the power of a womb is nowhere absent, the womb-born status of all is established. By the word “womb” is understood the power of Prakṛti, which serves as the material cause.

Therefore, the whole universe is of the nature of Śiva and Śakti. Even where there is no union of Śiva and Śakti in the form of a womb and liṅga, their presence as the all-pervading nature of Śiva and Śakti is undeniable even in a sprout and the like.

Jāti through boons and curses

And where, through the boon or curse of great sages, a lion attains divinity or the state of a boar, there, by the process stated above, through the operation of Prakṛti according to differences among classes of powers, the material causes constituting the former body are dissolved, and the material causes that initiate another jāti are assembled. In that case, a change of jāti in that very birth is indeed an exception.

Jāti through cessation of admixture

In some Smṛtis, a change of jāti is also taught in another way. For example, a Śūdra girl, when married by a Brāhmaṇa, may bear a daughter; she too, when married by a Brāhmaṇa, may bear a daughter. In this way, in the seventh generation that girl is regarded as a Brāhmaṇī. In these distinctions, all the statements of the Śāstra are reconciled.

Figurative Śūdratva

What someone has claimed—that “by birth one becomes a Śūdra”—and declared, that everyone is a Śūdra by birth, is also trivial, because no authoritative statement of that kind is found. Rather, opposite statements of Smṛti are found, such as “He is to be known as a Brāhmaṇa by birth” and “A Brāhmaṇa by birth is superior.”

In some places, the intended meaning is only “like a Śūdra.” “One devoid of literature, music, and the arts is a beast indeed, without tail and horns.” Here, a person devoid of literature, music, and the arts does not actually become an animal; the intended meaning is only “like an animal.”

“The Brāhmaṇa who, in particular, does not worship the Sandhyā becomes a Śūdra while still living; when dead, he is born as a dog.” (Dakṣa Smṛti 2.32)

“One who eats the food of a Śūdra continuously for one month becomes a Śūdra in this birth; when dead, he is born as a dog.” (Aṅgiras Smṛti 6.7)

“A twice-born man who, without studying the Veda, applies himself to other pursuits quickly attains Śūdra-hood while still living, together with his descendants.” (Manu Smṛti 2.168)

Similarly, in statements such as “by conduct one becomes a Śūdra,” Śūdra-hood too is to be understood only in a secondary sense. Likewise, in statements such as “the sons of Kṣatriyas became Brāhmaṇas” and “two Vaiśyas attained Brāhmaṇahood,” only likeness to a Brāhmaṇa is to be understood, as with a lion used in a secondary sense. These statements do not bring about the attainment of Brāhmaṇa-hood, Śūdra-hood, and the like, since jāti is eternal and its alteration is impossible as long as the body lasts.

The śruti “On the eleventh day the father should give the name” prescribes on that very eleventh day the giving to Brāhmaṇas and the others of names appropriate to their respective varṇas. At that time, however, Brāhmaṇa-hood and the like cannot be determined in accordance with qualities and actions. Therefore, the Śāstras prescribe the respective rites while designating Brāhmaṇas and the others as those respective varṇas: “One should initiate a Brāhmaṇa at eight years and teach him, a Rājanya at eleven years, and a Vaiśya at twelve years. A Brāhmaṇa should establish the fires in spring, a Rājanya in summer, and a Vaiśya in autumn. A king who desires sovereign rule should sacrifice with the Rājasūya.”

In these and similar statements, the rites are prescribed with reference to varṇas established by birth. They are not stated in this way: “One who wishes to become a Brāhmaṇa should do this; one who does this is a Brāhmaṇa.” No prescription of Brāhmaṇa-hood and the like through the respective actions is heard in that manner.

Just as daughters and the like are established by birth, and the actions appropriate to daughters and the like are prescribed in the world for those established by birth, it is not said, “She who acts in this way becomes a daughter; she who acts in this way becomes a wife.” If that were so, others too could become wives by performing the actions of a wife. The same is to be understood here.

Brāhmaṇa-hood applied to knowledge of Brahman and the like, however, is entirely different from that, because it depends upon jāti and is not itself of the nature of a jāti.

Distinct birth-wombs of Brāhmaṇas and others

Now, [someone may say]: “Just as lions, dogs, and the like differ in jāti according to differences of womb, this is not so with the wombs of Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, and the others.” That is not so, because the Chāndogya teaches differences in the birth-wombs of Brāhmaṇas and the others: “Those here whose conduct is pleasing attain a pleasing womb—either the womb of a Brāhmaṇa, the womb of a Kṣatriya, or the womb of a Vaiśya. Those here whose conduct is foul attain a foul womb; they enter the womb of a dog, the womb of a pig, or the womb of an outcaste.” (Chāndogya Upaniṣad 5.10)

Here the wombs of Brāhmaṇas and the others are described just as the wombs of dogs, pigs, and the like. Therefore, just as there is a difference of jāti between dogs and pigs, there is likewise a difference of jāti among Brāhmaṇas and the others. How could there be a difference of jāti in the absence of distinction? A difference of jāti among Brāhmaṇas and the others, as different kinds of human beings, is established just as among different kinds of horses and the like. “Having become a horse, he carried the gods; having become a stallion, he carried the Gandharvas.”

Moreover, distinction is not seen only in visible form; if it were so, differences in scent and the like could not be accounted for. Although mango trees do not differ in appearance, differences in their fruits and the like are perceived. Even when the differences among many mango fruits are not perceived by appearance, their differences are understood through taste and the like. In the same way, although they do not differ with respect to being human and the like, the distinction of Brāhmaṇas and the others is established through differences in happiness, capacity to delight, and the like.

Therefore, even where Brāhmaṇa-hood is taught with reference to a father and the like, Brāhmaṇa-hood in the father, son, and the others is understood as directly stated. By this, the formation of feminine words such as “Brāhmaṇī” by the feminine suffix after a jāti word is established. In the grammatical rule concerning “Brāhmaṇa” in a non-jāti sense, that form is prescribed when it does not denote a jāti; when it does denote a jāti, “Brāhmaṇa” itself is used.

Therefore: “Even a twice-born of bad conduct is worthy of honor, but not a Śūdra whose senses are controlled. Who, abandoning a wicked cow, would milk a well-behaved donkey?” (Parāśara 8.32)

“Whether unlearned or learned, the Brāhmaṇa is a great divinity; whether ritually prepared or not, fire is a great divinity.” So says Manu.

With reference to the excellence of the birth-womb, if there is a distinction of varṇa by birth, then the difference in naming is consistent: “A Brāhmaṇa’s name should be auspicious; a Kṣatriya’s should be endowed with strength; a Vaiśya’s should be associated with wealth; and a Śūdra’s should be repulsive.”

Guṇa-and-karma doctrine

It is said: “All these words apply to a collection of qualities: Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, Śūdra” (Pāṇini 2.2.6). According to Patañjali’s explanation, the words beginning with Brāhmaṇa are expressions of qualities alone, not words denoting jāti. Accordingly, the arrangement of varṇas rests on a collection of qualities and actions such as austerity and sacred learning. Therefore, the mantra “The Brāhmaṇa was His mouth” should properly be explained in accordance with this. The mouth of Brahmā, whose form is the universe, was the person who wished to become a Brāhmaṇa, distinguished by the qualities that cause Brāhmaṇa-hood, abundant in sattva, endowed with learning of the Veda and the like, and devoted to the Supreme Lord. He was foremost and supreme, occupying the place of the mouth. Just as the mouth, being foremost in knowledge, is superior to the other limbs, so too he is called the mouth because he is foremost in knowledge and superior through qualities such as calmness and the rest.

“Now, whoever, knowing this imperishable, departs from this world becomes a Brāhmaṇa.” In śruti passages such as this, the designation “Brāhmaṇa” depends on knowledge.

The Rājanya, distinguished by valor and the like, abundant in rajas with sattva subordinate, was produced by the arms. He was made to occupy the place of the arms because, like the arms, he is chiefly characterized by strength. Likewise, the Vaiśya, occupying the place of the thighs of this Puruṣa, takes their form; he is distinguished by actions such as farming and trade and is chiefly concerned with accumulating wealth. He was made the thighs, abundant in rajas with tamas subordinate. By derivation: “He who repeatedly enters here and there for the sake of work is a Viṭ; his offspring is a Vaiśya.”

The Śūdra, stationed in the place of the feet, is characterized by service, crafts, skill, and labor; he is born with tamas predominant and rajas subordinate. Thus it is imagined that the Brāhmaṇas and the others, endowed with those qualities, exist as the limbs beginning with the mouth of Prajāpati, whose form is the cosmic body. That is not so. The statement “The Brāhmaṇa was His mouth” also teaches Brāhmaṇa-hood and the like as arising from the mouth and the other limbs, as is confirmed by the concluding words, “The Śūdra was born from His feet.” The ablative case cannot be changed into a nominative meaning merely in accordance with the context, since that would oppose the primary meaning and require a figurative meaning.

Is the mouthhood of Brāhmaṇas and the others primary or secondary? If it is a relation of co-reference, is it primary or secondary? Is it figurative, as in “The lion is Devadatta”? Or is it by denial, as in “This pillar is a man”? Is the co-reference primary, as in “That is Devadatta,” or is it only a relation of effect and cause, as in “The pot is clay”? Since the ablative alone, by removing doubt, expresses the intended meaning, it is superior. Just as a pot is called clay because it is made of clay, so too the designation “mouth” is explained through predominance. Therefore the creation account that follows—“The Moon was born from the mind, the Sun was born from the eye”—is consistent. Manu too clearly states that Brāhmaṇas and the others are born from those limbs in “those born from the mouth, arms, thighs, and feet.”

Moreover, if calmness and the like cause Brāhmaṇa-hood, then one already endowed with them would already have become a Brāhmaṇa. How, then, could the wish to become one arise in him? If by “Brahman” only the supreme reality that supports all is intended, that too does not fit, because Brahman cannot be born as Brahman, and because it contradicts the śruti that one, being Brahman itself, attains Brahman. If it is said that one who does not know Brahman becomes Brahman through knowledge, that too does not fit, since this is accomplished by discernment and does not depend on Brāhmaṇa-hood. Hence the śruti says: “Among humans, gods, and seers, whoever knows that supreme Brahman becomes Brahman itself. Whoever among the gods awoke to it became that; so too among the seers and humans.”

Moreover, in the cosmic form of Brahmā, the gods alone occupy the position of the mouth, because they predominate through the abundance of sattva and are superior even to humans; humans do not, since they are mortal.

Two meanings of the word Brāhmaṇa

It is said: “Whoever, O Gārgī, departs from this world without knowing this imperishable is a wretched person; but whoever departs from this world knowing this imperishable is a Brāhmaṇa.” In śruti passages such as this, the designation “Brāhmaṇa” is based on knowledge. This does not negate Brāhmaṇa-hood as a jāti, because this śruti concerns the technical meaning of “Brāhmaṇa,” just as the grammatical terms “guṇa” and “vṛddhi” have technical meanings.

Therefore, śruti enjoins effort to attain Brāhmaṇa-hood in the form of steadfastness in Brahman even for one who is a Brāhmaṇa by jāti. “Having known this very Self, the Brāhmaṇas, after rising above desire for sons and desire for wealth, wander as mendicants.” “A Brāhmaṇa, having become indifferent to learning, should seek to remain as a child. Having become indifferent to childlikeness and learning, he should become a sage. Having become indifferent to silence and non-silence, then he is a Brāhmaṇa.”

Here, the first occurrence of “Brāhmaṇa” denotes jāti, while the second denotes one who knows Brahman, because a Brāhmaṇa alone is eligible for renunciation and the like. Here the knower of Brahman is not enjoined to pursue learning, childlikeness, and the like, since these have already been accomplished.

Therefore, the Vajrasūcī Upaniṣad’s teaching of Brāhmaṇa-hood characterized by knowledge of Brahman for someone other than a Brāhmaṇa by jāti does not deny jāti. It says there: “Many seers, such as Agastya, are described as having attained knowledge of Brahman without belonging to this jāti. Therefore, there is no Brāhmaṇa by jāti.” Since that is a cause of liberation, that alone is primary Brāhmaṇa-hood.

By this it is also explained that the Rājanya is predominant in strength because he is born from the arms, which are predominant in strength. Likewise, the Vaiśya and Śūdra are characterized by birth from the thighs and feet. Therefore, Smṛti texts clearly state their birth from the mouth and the other limbs.

“This is the duty of those born from the mouth: bearing the mark of Viṣṇu. This duty is not prescribed for those born from the arms and thighs.”

Manu and others state birth from those limbs in such passages as “those born from the mouth, arms, thighs, and feet.” Their commentators, including Kullūka Bhaṭṭa, likewise state birth from those parts. In every respect, Smṛti follows the meaning of Śruti, as the great poet Kālidāsa said. Therefore, the meaning stated in Smṛti is to be understood as the meaning of Śruti. Any mistaken views contrary to Manu should be rejected.

“Whatever Smṛtis stand outside the Veda, and whatever mistaken views there are, all should be known as fruitless, for those Smṛtis are said to abide in darkness.”

With regard to the statements “The Śūdra was born from His feet” and “[the Rājanya] was fashioned from the arms,” it is not proper to abandon their primary meanings and impose a figurative meaning, such as taking “was born” and “was fashioned” to mean “was imagined.” When the primary meaning is possible, adopting another meaning is unjustified.

Devāpi account

It is also said: “The seer, Ārṣṭiṣeṇa’s son, Devāpi, knowing the gods’ good will, sat as Hotṛ. He sent the heavenly waters from the upper ocean down to the lower, toward the fields. When Devāpi, appointed as priest for Śantanu and chosen as Hotṛ, prayed compassionately for rain, Bṛhaspati gave him a voice heard by the gods, a voice that brings rain.” (Ṛgveda 10.98.5–7)

From these mantras it is understood that Devāpi Kauravya, Śantanu’s brother, through austerity attained Brāhmaṇa-hood, with his Vedic knowledge made manifest, and became a priest. Yāska too interpreted the mantra in the same way. An account is told there: Devāpi Ārṣṭiṣeṇa and Śantanu Kauravya were brothers. Śantanu, the younger, had himself consecrated as king. Devāpi undertook austerity and the Brāhmaṇa’s way of life. Thereafter, for twelve years during Śantanu’s reign, rain did not fall. The Brāhmaṇas who knew the Veda told him: “You have acted unrighteously. You were consecrated after passing over your elder brother; therefore, the gods do not rain for you.” Śantanu then asked Devāpi to accept the kingdom. Devāpi said to him: “I shall be your priest and officiate for you.” This is his hymn of desire; this is its meaning. (Nirukta 2.10)

The summary meaning of the mantra is this: Devāpi, appointed as Śantanu’s priest and chosen as Hotṛ for the rite intended to bring rain, prayed compassionately for the king. Taking him to be one whose speech was heard by the gods and who prayed for rain, Bṛhaspati gave him speech that brings rain. By praising the gods with that speech, he obtained rain from them. This is also supported by the words: “Sit as Hotṛ, worship the gods at the proper time, and serve the gods with oblation, O Devāpi.” (Ṛgveda 10.98.4)

Thus it is said: “The Kauravya Ārṣṭiṣeṇa, firm in his vows, attained Brāhmaṇa-hood through great austerity, O Rāma, best of sages. The royal sage Sindhudvīpa and the great ascetic Devāpi attained Brāhmaṇa-hood; so too did the great sage Viśvāmitra, blessed, of fierce splendour and great austerity.” (Mahābhārata, Rājadharma section)

The account of Viśvāmitra likewise supports the arrangement of varṇas by qualities and actions. It is said: “Born of Gādhi, of great splendour, he blazed like a fire. By austerity he cast off Kṣatriyahood and attained the radiance of Brahman.” (Mahābhārata 6.16.28)

“Then the great ascetic Viśvāmitra attained Brāhmaṇa-hood. Though he too was a Kṣatriya, he became the founder of a line of Brāhmaṇas.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti section 4.8)

Likewise, the eighty-one sons of Ṛṣabhadeva attained Brāhmaṇa-hood through qualities and actions. It is said: “The younger sons, the eighty-one descendants, obeyed their father’s command. Great in bodily strength, great in Vedic learning, devoted to sacrifice and purified in their actions, they became Brāhmaṇas.” (Bhāgavata 5.4.12) “The eighty-one twice-born were promulgators of the ritual tradition.” (Bhāgavata 11.2.19)

Although these too were Kṣatriyas, they became Brāhmaṇas after abandoning Kṣatriya duties through austerity and the like. This does not contradict the birth-based arrangement of Brāhmaṇas and the other varṇas, since a change of jāti takes place between births, not within the same birth; this is the general rule.

Someone may say: “The mantra says that Devāpi, though a Kṣatriya, was Śantanu’s priest; and because he performed the work of a priest, though he was a Kṣatriya, he accepted Brāhmaṇa-hood in that very birth through Brāhmaṇa duties, austerity, and the like. Thus Brāhmaṇa-hood based on qualities and actions is established.” That is a trifling argument, because the initiation and eligibility for the rite are forbidden to one who is not a Brāhmaṇa.

Yāska explains the account in this way: Devāpi and Śantanu, sons of Ṛṣṭiṣeṇa, were Kauravya brothers. Śantanu, the younger, had himself consecrated as king. Devāpi undertook austerity. Thereafter rain did not fall for twelve years during Śantanu’s reign. The Brāhmaṇas who knew the Veda told him: “You acted unrighteously, passing over your elder brother and having yourself consecrated. Therefore, the gods do not rain for you.” Śantanu then entreated Devāpi to take the kingdom. Devāpi said: “I shall be your priest and officiate for you.” (Nirukta 2.10)

Ārṣṭiṣeṇa, son of Ṛṣṭiṣeṇa, was chosen as Hotṛ for the rite intended to bring rain for Śantanu and sat down to perform the Hotṛ function. Devāpi knew the favourable intention of the gods, turned toward granting water. When the gods had been propitiated, he sent the heavenly waters down from the upper ocean to the earthly ocean; this is the distinction between the two oceans. The waters sent forth nourished the crops. As priest and Hotṛ for the rite for rain, compassionating the king, he prayed that rain might fall.

If it is said that the mantra, Nirukta, and its commentary state that Devāpi was a Kṣatriya and also say that he served as a priest, the answer is no. The words “Ārṣṭiṣeṇa’s son” and the rest praise the deity and do not establish that a Kṣatriya attained Brāhmaṇa-hood through priestly work in the same birth. Therefore, Durgācārya, the commentator on the Nirukta, explicitly explains that the Devāpi appointed as Hotṛ in the rite for rain for Śantanu belonged to another kalpa.

Brāhmaṇa-hood through austerity in another birth

It was stated there, according to the principle “the relation between a word and its meaning is innate,” that the relations between words and meanings are natural and eternal. Since humans and others are impermanent, being subject to birth and death, the varṇas are understood as eternal classes, not as individual persons. Vedic words do not denote individuals; they denote eternal jātis. For example, the word “Indra” denotes the jāti of being Indra. Although individuals are impermanent, the jāti is eternal.

Or words may denote a station, as does “prāḍvivāka.” A prāḍvivāka is not a particular person but a particular office; whoever occupies that office is called by that term. Likewise, Vedic words such as “Devāpi” may refer to a particular jāti or to a particular station. Therefore, it is said that Devāpi became a Brāhmaṇa through austerity in another birth and performed priestly service. Thus the account is connected with a Devāpi of another kalpa. “Let him do this”—in this way the whole meaning is explained by Durgācārya.

The interpretation that “rarāṇa” means rejoicing and being pleased through praise, recitation, meditation, and the like is contrary to the Nirukta. Durgācārya derives the word from “rāti,” meaning “giving,” and explains “rarāṇa” as “giving.”

Just as Viśvāmitra, in another kalpa, attained the state of a supreme seer through austerity, became a seer of mantras such as the Gāyatrī, and performed priestly service at sacrifices, so too did this Devāpi.

Attainment of jāti through austerity

Alternatively, that statement has this very meaning: Brāhmaṇa-hood is attained through a special form of austerity. For example, the perfected great sage Agastya, by the power of his words, caused Nahuṣa to become a python. This transformation took place through the dissolution of the particles that formed Nahuṣa’s body and the gathering of the particles that formed a python’s body. In the same way, for those perfected through extraordinary austerity, Brāhmaṇa-hood can be produced by dissolving the particles that form a Kṣatriya’s body and assembling the particles that form a Brāhmaṇa’s body. Priesthood in such a case is certainly possible. This is indeed known. But it does not follow that every Kṣatriya, even one unable to transform a mouse, can make himself a Brāhmaṇa through actions alone.

Changes of form without an intervening birth, through the extraordinary power of austerity and the force of weapons and the like, are exceptions: Nahuṣa attained divinity and then the state of a python; Ahalyā, Menakā, and others attained the state of stone and the like. This too is an exception due to the power of austerity. The general rule is that the arrangement of varṇas is by birth alone. The attainment of Brāhmaṇa-hood by Devāpi, Viśvāmitra, and others, even without an intervening birth, is an exception brought about by the extraordinary power of austerity.

Therefore, for those perfected by great austerity, or for the Lord who can create, undo, or transform, the transformation of material nature through a resolve expressed as a boon or a curse is not very difficult. Through Viśvāmitra’s austerity Menakā was transformed; the sons of Vasiṣṭha met their end; Triśaṅku was sent to heaven; and a new heaven was created.

It is then not difficult to produce Brāhmaṇa-hood by dissolving the particles that form a Kṣatriya body, in which rajas predominates and sattva is subordinate, and assembling the particles that form a Brāhmaṇa body, in which sattva and the power of the mouth predominate. Likewise, through austerity and resolve, the particles that form bodies of the other varṇas can be dissolved, and those that form a Brāhmaṇa body assembled. Just as an earthen pot cannot become a golden pot without the dissolution of the elements that formed the earlier pot and the assembly of the elements that form the new one, so too this should be understood in the present case.

The Gītā’s agreement

For this reason, the Gītā’s statement, “The fourfold varṇa was created by Me according to the division of guṇa and karma,” accords with this very view. Birth in the respective wombs is explained according to the divisions of guṇa and karma. Śruti establishes the attainment of wombs through good or bad conduct. Therefore, the Supreme Lord, the giver of the fruits of action, said that He created the fourfold varṇa of Brāhmaṇas and the others in accordance with the division of guṇa and karma.

It is not possible for just anyone to create this. Jāti is by birth, and birth follows guṇa and karma. Who but a madman would say otherwise?

Moreover, just as a pot is made from its material causes, such as clay, and not from something distinct from its material cause after the pot has come into being, so too, when the bodies of Brāhmaṇas and the others arise from atoms endowed with the specific powers that initiate those bodies, later guṇas and karmas distinct from those causes cannot be considered instrumental in bringing about that birth.

Later guṇas and karmas can certainly cause birth in a womb that corresponds to them in another existence. It is well known that actions and the qualities corresponding to them initiate jāti, lifespan, and experience. As it is said, “When the fruit of merit or demerit is intense, it is experienced in this very life.” In some cases, they may initiate another jāti in that same life; that is an exception.

Someone may object: “If such qualities and actions are not now present, how can their existence in a previous birth be known?” That is not so. Even if they are unknown to others, the Lord who gives the fruits of action knows them. It is impossible to perceive every action that initiates birth. Even so, their existence is not in doubt, since it can be inferred from its fruit. Likewise, the possibility of attaining a Brāhmaṇa’s or another varṇa’s womb does not contradict their existence.

Thus, the guṇas and karmas are the causes of birth in the wombs of Brāhmaṇas and the others, and through them the arrangement of varṇas follows. If the varṇa arrangement is accepted as depending on the guṇas and karmas manifested in the body after birth, then, as these change from day to day and moment to moment, varṇa too would change continually. There would then be no stable arrangement.

Varṇa by birth, excellence through action

An objection may be raised: If the qualities and actions proper to a Brāhmaṇa are the cause of that body, then only qualities and actions proper to a Brāhmaṇa should always be present in it, and contrary ones should not arise. That is not so, because their efficacy is exhausted in bringing about birth in that womb. In a suitable environment, through the rites performed by parents and others, instruction by a qualified teacher, study, understanding, conduct, and teaching, the qualities and actions proper to a Brāhmaṇa become manifest in the body. As the śruti says: “One who has a mother, a father, and a teacher.” Otherwise, the prescriptions for rites performed by parents and others would be pointless.

Thus, even when one has been born in a Brāhmaṇa womb through former good qualities and actions, because of inadequate care, heedlessness, bad company, and an adverse environment, the qualities such as calmness and self-restraint proper to a Brāhmaṇa are suppressed. Contrary qualities and actions arise, and in the end birth in a dog’s or pig’s womb may follow. In the same way, among Śūdras, the qualities and actions appropriate to them, arising from rajas predominating with tamas subordinate, may be overcome. Actions contrary to their own varṇa arise, and qualities proper to a Brāhmaṇa become manifest. Through these, in due course, attainment of Brāhmaṇa-hood and the like in another birth may occur. Therefore the conclusion is: varṇa is by birth; excellence is through action. Thus distinctions such as highest, middling, and lowest also fit.

This refutes the objection that the division of varṇa must be determined by the division of qualities and actions present at the time, since otherwise contradiction or a division of the sentence would result. The former actions that bring about jāti, lifespan, and experience have already accomplished their purpose. Once the result has been attained, the means need not be posited again; a cause does not become the cause of its own cause. When Brāhmaṇa-hood and the rest have already been established by qualities and actions from a previous birth, to posit present qualities and actions as necessary for that same result is to assume what is already established. If qualities and actions cause birth, and birth is the cause of the fourfold varṇa, then qualities and actions are causes of the fourfold varṇa as causes of its cause, like family descent and parents.

In the statement “The fourfold varṇa was created by Me,” the division of qualities and actions is stated as the instrumental cause in the creation of the fourfold varṇa. Creation here means birth; it is nothing other than that. Thus it is clear that the Lord produces the Brāhmaṇas and the rest by granting birth in their respective wombs in accordance with the division of qualities and actions. To accept an arrangement of varṇa based on qualities and actions alone, independently of birth, contradicts the statement that it was created by the Lord.

It is objected: “Even among those born in these respective wombs, the qualities and actions proper to each are not found; they are seen elsewhere as well, and there are many departures from the rule. Therefore it is not established that the division of the four varṇas follows only from the division of qualities and actions existing in a previous birth.” That is not so. Qualities and actions from a previous birth are not found in this body precisely because they belonged to a previous birth: the cause has been exhausted by producing the body. Even when good and bad bodies arise from good and bad actions, actions contrary to the nature of each body may arise. If the actions that produced the body alone brought about every subsequent good and bad action, human effort would be reduced to pouring water upon ashes, and the injunctions and prohibitions of scripture would be pointless. A heedless person acts on what is prohibited, ignoring the scripture of prohibition; one who is vigilant and attentive to scripture abandons what is prohibited and does what is prescribed.

Thus it is sometimes seen that, through former actions, one attains a womb and prosperity in wealth and grain, yet falls through heedlessness. Another may attain a low condition through former actions and, though reviled by many, rise to greatness through austerity. Rāvaṇa too, though he had attained great lordship through former austerities, is remembered as having fallen through heedlessness. “You have attained the fruit of dharma; of that there is no doubt. You will swiftly reap the fruit of this adharma as well.” (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Sundara Kāṇḍa 7.51.22.)

The claim that creation is to be explained by qualities and actions manifest in the body is insubstantial, pointless, and contradictory. Were that so, the specific statement “The fourfold varṇa was created by Me” would be pointless, since the Lord is already the creator of all. Indeed, a distinction is recognized between what is created directly by the Lord and what is created by the Lord through beings who have assumed particular forms.

Moreover, the phrase “qualities and actions of a previous birth manifest in the present body” is pointless. If the intended meaning is that qualities and actions belonging to a previous birth are manifested in the present body, that is what should have been said; even then, it does not hold. Let the reasoning by a bad analogy be accepted: just as a pot made by a potter is called the Lord’s creation, so the fourfold varṇa made by someone else is called the Lord’s creation. Then one would have to abandon the force of the accepted means of knowledge and accept faith alone. The intermediate agent responsible here has not been specified. If it is society, society is nothing apart from an aggregate of individuals, and the aggregate is nothing apart from its individuals. Since individuals are limited in knowledge and lack the capacity to determine exceedingly subtle qualities and actions, the conclusion is indeterminacy. The foolish claim that some ignorant person or committee will determine the fourfold varṇa is mere prattle; no universally accepted assembly of learned and discerning scholars is established.

Defects in an arrangement of varṇa by action

Moreover, qualities and actions do not remain the same even within a single birth, because they change rapidly. Even one whose qualities and actions are good may, through heedlessness, become engaged in bad qualities and actions. Even one inclined toward bad qualities and actions may, through the company of the good and the force of human effort, attain excellence in qualities and actions. Examples of this are found in Vālmīki, Ajāmila, and others.

Because qualities change unevenly at every moment, these same distinctions are seen even through the divisions of the Kṛta, Tretā, Dvāpara, and Kali ages. Just as the intervals between successive parts of a day are experienced, gradations are experienced for each person every day. If varṇa were determined by differences in qualities and actions, then varṇa would change from day to day. In the morning, when a person endowed with sattva performs meditation on and worship of the Supreme Lord, he would become a Brāhmaṇa. When he takes up a staff to drive away dogs and the like, he would become a Kṣatriya. When he engages in the buying and selling of salt and other goods, agriculture, and the care of cattle, he would become a Vaiśya. When he undertakes sweeping and cleaning the house, he would become a Śūdra. Such a state cannot be called an orderly arrangement.

Further, under such a theory, a Brāhmaṇa endowed at some time with tamasic qualities and actions would become a Śūdra, approach a Brāhmaṇa woman endowed with sattvic qualities, and beget a caṇḍāla in her. Out of fidelity, a wife cannot restrain even a badly behaved husband. A son endowed with sattvic qualities would regard his father, endowed with tamasic qualities, as a Śūdra and make him perform service. Thus, in a varṇa arrangement governed by qualities and actions, there would be utter disorder.

Qualities and actions carried over from another birth can manifest in their own form in a subsequent birth. Because they perish moment by moment, however, their earlier forms are not seen; their fruits, whose results have begun, are manifested through the inscrutable power of destiny, and the existence of the causes is inferred from the force of the fruit. “That which is inconceivable is destiny; it is not destroyed even among beings. Clearly, this reversal has fallen upon me and her. Who, O Saumitri, can undertake to fight destiny, when no action of it apart from its result can be seen?” (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa 2.22.20–21.) Such are the statements of the Rāmāyaṇa. Even when accumulated, those qualities and actions do not themselves become manifest, because, as the unseen and the like, they are beyond perception, inference, and scriptural testimony.

The meaning of svabhāva

The very accumulation of impressions produced by the repeated practice of action and knowledge is what is designated by words such as nature and inherent disposition. A student who has practiced those actions or fields of knowledge, and fallen asleep, on waking again engages in them because of the impressions produced by practice. In the same way, a being who has practiced those actions or knowledge, upon dying and reaching another birth, engages there in them through that very former practice.

“By that very former practice he is carried on, even though helpless.” (Bhagavad Gītā 6.44.) “Even a man of knowledge acts according to his own nature; beings follow their nature. What will restraint accomplish?” (Bhagavad Gītā 3.33.) “Bound by your own action born of nature, O son of Kuntī, what through delusion you do not wish to do, you will do helplessly.” (Bhagavad Gītā 18.60.)

Elsewhere, birth and death are said to be like sleeping and waking. “When, through absorption in sense objects, a living being no longer remembers itself, that complete forgetfulness, whatever its cause, is called death. Birth, O bounteous one, is a person’s identification with all things as the self; acceptance of sense objects is said to be like a dream or a desire.” (Śrīmad Bhāgavata 11.22.38–39.)

Scope for human effort in the doctrine of svabhāva

An objection may be raised: If everyone acts under the control of nature, then human effort is denied, and the injunctions and prohibitions of scripture become pointless. That is not so. Human effort is possible because scripturally informed attraction and aversion, produced by good company and the study of scripture, remove natural attraction and aversion and break their hold as aids to nature. It is said: “Attraction and aversion are fixed in the senses and their objects. One should not come under their sway, for they are his enemies.” (Bhagavad Gītā 3.34.)

Although the actions that initiate a particular jāti, lifespan, and experience produce those fruits alone and do not directly initiate other actions, through impressions they nevertheless become causes of actions corresponding to them. Those very actions which initiate further actions through impressions are sometimes called nature or destiny. When heedlessness arises, even those actions, though auspicious, do not initiate auspicious actions; rather, censurable actions begin through bad company and the like. Through the power of human effort and good company, even those actions do not initiate inauspicious actions; instead, auspicious actions manifest.

For example, although earlier seeds are present in a field, they do not sprout; those newly placed there are the ones that emerge. In the same way, impressions produced by human effort, sacred study, and the like manifest and generate actions suited to them. Auspicious former actions that initiated the bodies of Brāhmaṇas and the others generate corresponding knowledge and actions when they are not overcome by contrary heedlessness and by knowledge and actions arising from bad company. Nourished by desires, actions, and understanding born of human effort, and by a suitable favorable environment, good company, and scriptural rites, those impressions bear fruit properly.

“By great sacrifices and by sacrifices, the body is made Brahmanical.” When this is absent, even if a Brāhmaṇa body has arisen through good qualities and actions, contrary environments, bad company, and the absence of scriptural rites may prevent the marks appropriate to Brāhmaṇa-hood from appearing in conduct; in the end, birth in a bad womb alone may follow. Likewise, one whose body has been produced by tamasic actions and who is born in a Śūdra body may, through good company, actions enjoined by scripture, and corresponding impressions, become worthy of honor like Vidura and attain a good destination at the end, even without Vedic study, performance of its meaning, or the standing appropriate to it.

The Lord’s action follows karma

Therefore, although the Supreme Lord is the inner controller of all, governs all, and causes all to act, because His action depends on karma there is no partiality, cruelty, or the like. According to the impressions left by former actions, each person acts in a particular way; the Supreme Lord impels that person and makes him act in that way. He does not act independently. As an authoritative statement declares.

Thus the accumulation of impressions from former actions itself constitutes nature. The actions prescribed for Brāhmaṇas and the others are divided according to the qualities arising from that nature. The Lord created the varṇas of Brāhmaṇas and the others with actions corresponding to them. Therefore actions such as calmness and self-restraint are prescribed for them in accordance with their qualities. A person born in a particular varṇa must perform the actions proper to it, not whatever he pleases.

“The actions of Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, Vaiśyas, and Śūdras, O scorcher of enemies, are divided according to the qualities born of their own nature.” (Bhagavad Gītā 18.41.) Here the actions of Brāhmaṇas and the others are said to be divided by the qualities born of their nature. Thus it is false to claim that only qualities born of nature are stated to cause the divisions of action. The divisions of varṇa are not set aside while prescribing divisions of action; rather, differences of action are prescribed without rejecting differences of varṇa.

Accordingly, in statements such as “A Brāhmaṇa should sacrifice” and “A king who desires sovereignty should sacrifice with the Rājasūya,” it is right that birth in the respective varṇa itself establishes eligibility for each rite. Therefore, Nīlakaṇṭha’s commentary too does not reject Brāhmaṇa-hood and the rest by birth; its explanation is generally to be understood in accordance with the explanation of Bhagavatpāda Śaṅkara.

Śaṅkara’s view of Gītā 18.41

Now, bringing the meaning of the entire Gītā to a conclusion and showing the means for attaining renunciation, Śaṅkara begins the section with “Brāhmaṇas and the rest.” “Service is prescribed for Śūdras; since they are of one birth, they are not entitled to Vedic study.” (Śaṅkara’s commentary.) Their actions are divided and not intermixed.

He states the reason: “by the qualities born of their own nature.” Nature is the Lord’s prakṛti, consisting of the three guṇas; it is the source of those qualities. Or, sattva alone is the source of a Brāhmaṇa’s nature, because of its peacefulness. For a Kṣatriya’s nature, rajas with sattva subordinate is the source, because of its rulership. For a Vaiśya’s nature, rajas with tamas subordinate is the source, because of its qualities of agriculture and the like. For a Śūdra’s nature, tamas with rajas subordinate is the source, because of its quality of service.

Nīlakaṇṭha’s explanation

Or, svabhāva means impressions from a previous birth. The qualities arising from those impressions, and not from birth alone, are like the birds’ ability to fly through the sky. Therefore, qualities that distinguish one jāti from another are not listed among calmness and the rest. Teaching and the like, which distinguish the three twice-born varṇas from Śūdras, or distinguish Brāhmaṇas from the other two, are not listed there. Rather, calmness and the rest are seen as qualities common to all jātis.

Valor and the like are seen even in Brāhmaṇas such as Droṇa, and calmness and the like even in Kṣatriyas such as Bharata; the reverse is seen as well. Therefore, in whatever varṇa calmness and the rest are found, even if the person is a Śūdra, he is to be known as a Brāhmaṇa by these marks. And where Śūdra qualities are seen even in a Brāhmaṇa, he is a Śūdra. As Yudhiṣṭhira said to Nahusha, who had become a serpent: “Where truth, giving, forbearance, good conduct, compassion, austerity, and purity are found, there he is remembered as a Brāhmaṇa, O lord of serpents. Where this conduct is seen, O serpent, that person is remembered as a Brāhmaṇa; where it is not found, he is designated a Śūdra.”

Manu, however, stated the duties of the jātis: “Teaching, studying, performing sacrifice, officiating at sacrifice, giving, and receiving gifts he assigned to Brāhmaṇas. Protecting the subjects, giving, sacrifice, study, and nonattachment to sense objects belong to a Kṣatriya, in brief. Protecting cattle, giving, sacrifice, study, trade, lending at interest, and agriculture belong to a Vaiśya. The Lord prescribed one duty alone for a Śūdra: service, without resentment, to these very varṇas.” (Manu Smṛti 1.88–91.)

Therefore, where calmness and the rest are seen in one who is not a Brāhmaṇa, or in a Brāhmaṇa, that person is intended as a Brāhmaṇa in this sense.

“A person devoted to his own work attains perfection.” (Bhagavad Gītā 18.45.) Here, however, the actions of one’s own varṇa are the teaching and the other duties stated by Manu, not calmness and self-restraint. For a person of knowledge and realization has no other perfection to attain. Calmness and self-restraint, therefore, are characteristics of a Brāhmaṇa who is established in Brahman.

The explanation of Nīlakaṇṭha given above, whether by conduct or by quality and by speaking of Brāhmaṇa-hood produced by qualities, does not deny jāti by birth or the duties corresponding to it. Therefore, in “a person devoted to his own work,” the duties of teaching and the like stated by Manu are understood as one’s own work. If Brāhmaṇa-hood and the other jātis were not established by birth, how could the duties prescribed by jāti be called the duties of Brāhmaṇas? And how could Pārtha say, “The eternal duties of jāti and family are destroyed”?

Bhagavatpāda Śaṅkara, however, takes calmness and the rest as intended in “a person devoted to his own work,” in accordance with the context. By properly performing these duties prescribed for the jātis, one attains heaven. Those who are devoted to their own work according to varṇa and āśrama experience the fruit of their actions after death and then, through what remains, obtain birth in a distinguished region, jāti, family, and condition, with appropriate dharma, lifespan, learning, wealth, happiness, and understanding. (Āpastamba Dharma Sūtra 2.2.2.3.)

“Only Brāhmaṇas are entitled to rise above worldly life; therefore Brāhmaṇas are mentioned.” (Śaṅkara’s commentary.) “Having known this Self, the Brāhmaṇas rise above the desire for sons and live the life of mendicants.” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, Kahola Brāhmaṇa.)

Likewise, the duty of a Kṣatriya to fight, even though it involves the killing of teachers, brothers, sons, and others and is exceedingly difficult, is not unrighteous when it is his own duty. “If you do not undertake this, then, abandoning your own duty and honor, you will incur sin.” (Bhagavad Gītā 2.33.) By this it is firmly stated that actions enjoined by the Veda for the duration of life, even actions involving the killing of animals and the like, are not unrighteous. Although this point is raised as an objection, it is not refuted by the statement “that is wrong”; the position that duties belong to those entitled to perform them remains intact.

“The fourfold varṇa was created by Me,” in accordance with the śruti, “The Brāhmaṇa was His mouth.” The qualities are sattva, rajas, and tamas. The actions of a Brāhmaṇa, in whom sattva predominates, are calmness, self-restraint, austerity, and the like. The actions of a Kṣatriya, in whom rajas predominates with sattva subordinate, are valor, vigor, and the like. The actions of a Vaiśya, in whom rajas predominates with tamas subordinate, are agriculture and the like. Service alone is the action of a Śūdra, in whom tamas predominates with rajas subordinate. Thus the fourfold varṇa was created according to the division of qualities and actions. These qualities and actions are the instrumental causes in the Lord’s creation of the fourfold varṇa; accordingly, each varṇa performs actions appropriate to its predominating quality.

“The actions of Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, Vaiśyas, and Śūdras, O scorcher of enemies, are divided according to the qualities born of their own nature.” (Bhagavad Gītā 18.41.) Here the actions of Brāhmaṇas and the rest are divided by the qualities arising from their own nature. It is therefore false to say that only qualities born of nature are stated to cause the divisions of action. Rather, the duties are prescribed by following the distinctions of varṇa; the distinctions of varṇa are not prescribed by following the divisions of action. It has already been stated that jāti itself establishes eligibility for particular rites, as in “A Brāhmaṇa should sacrifice” and “A king who desires sovereignty should sacrifice with the Rājasūya.” Nīlakaṇṭha’s explanation too does not reject Brāhmaṇa-hood and the rest by birth; his three explanations mostly follow the explanation of Bhagavatpāda Śaṅkara.

In the first explanation, “by the qualities born of nature” means: nature is the Lord’s prakṛti, consisting of the three guṇas; those guṇas have that as their source. The actions such as calmness and self-restraint are prescribed in accordance with the qualities arising from prakṛti, such as sattva.

In the second explanation, “nature” means the natures of the Brāhmaṇas and the others; the causes arising from those natures are the qualities such as sattva. The actions such as calmness are divided according to them. Sattva is the cause of a Brāhmaṇa’s nature. Rajas, with sattva subordinate, is the cause of a Kṣatriya’s nature; and so on for the four varṇas, whose natures are seen as peacefulness, rulership, dullness, and the like. Thus actions such as calmness are prescribed for Brāhmaṇas and the others in accordance with the sattva and other qualities stated as the causes of their respective natures.

In the third explanation, the word “nature” means the impressions produced by actions in a previous birth, which in the present birth are manifested as inclinations toward their own results. The qualities arising from those impressions are called “born of nature”; qualities cannot arise without a cause. Actions are divided by those qualities. Thus the actions such as calmness are prescribed in accordance with qualities produced by impressions from actions of previous births, which manifest in living beings as inclinations toward their respective results. The qualities and actions through which births occur in the wombs of Brāhmaṇas and the others generally become manifest in the present birth as their fruits ripen; those qualities are what the word “nature” denotes. The qualities of sattva and the others arising from them divide the actions of Brāhmaṇas and the rest.

It may be asked: “How can the actions of Brāhmaṇas and the others, divided and prescribed by scripture, be said to be divided by qualities such as sattva?” There is no fault in this. Scripture too divides the actions of Brāhmaṇas and the others with regard to their particular qualities such as sattva, and not independently of those qualities. Thus even actions divided by scripture are said to be divided by qualities.

The effect of environment

An objection may be raised: “At present the opposite is seen. In some Brāhmaṇas only dullness is seen, while among Śūdras calmness is seen.” That is not so. Such conditions occur only occasionally, owing to contrary company, environments, and actions suited to them. Thus, although valor is seen in Paraśurāma and Droṇa, they are not Kṣatriyas; although Bhīṣma and Yudhiṣṭhira possess calmness and the like, they are not Brāhmaṇas.

Likewise, although Arjuna displayed dispassion and the like at the time of battle, the Lord forbade the life of mendicancy, which would have led him into another’s duty, and set him to the Kṣatriya duty he did not desire. The qualities are subtle, difficult to discern, and beyond the reach of human understanding. Therefore, it is fitting that calmness and self-restraint and the like be prescribed by scripture alone. As the learned say: “An injunction applies to what is otherwise unattained.”

“Considering your own duty, you ought not to waver.” (Bhagavad Gītā 2.31.) “A Kṣatriya’s own duty is battle.” (Śaṅkara’s commentary.) What is already obtained through desire is not enjoined, nor does a duty become dharma merely because it is desired. “Better one’s own duty, though deficient, than another’s duty well performed.” (Bhagavad Gītā 3.35.) “Doing the action determined by one’s own nature, one does not incur sin.” (Bhagavad Gītā 18.47.) As poison is not harmful to a worm born in poison, so one does not incur sin by doing the action determined by one’s own nature. (Śaṅkara’s commentary.)

“One should not abandon one’s inborn action, O son of Kuntī, even if it is defective; for all undertakings are covered by fault, as fire is by smoke.” (Bhagavad Gītā 18.48.) “Inborn” means arisen together with birth. (Śaṅkara’s commentary.) Thus, for Brāhmaṇas and the others, the action that arises with birth and is rooted in jāti is the action to be performed. That alone is beneficial, for another’s duty is dangerous.

Further, even if someone declares himself a follower of Bhagavatpāda and established in Brahman, should he abandon that view and adopt the opinion of Nīlakaṇṭha, it has already been said that this will not accomplish that end. How can the meaning of words such as “born of nature” and “inborn,” as explained by the commentator, accord with the view that varṇa changes according to qualities and actions, since qualities and actions are liable to change? This is also clear from the distinct worldly results described in the Purāṇas for people of the different varṇas and āśramas.

“A person devoted to his own prescribed action attains perfection. Through the performance of his own action, when impurity has been removed, the qualified person attains the perfection marked by the fitness of body and senses for steadfast knowledge.” (Śaṅkara’s commentary on Bhagavad Gītā 18.45.) Since the actions of Brāhmaṇas and the others, such as calmness, are said to be prescribed by jāti, it is clearly established that Brāhmaṇas and the others are jātis.

Therefore, in the Purāṇas and in commentaries on the Veda, such as those of Sāyaṇa, varṇas are stated to be by birth. “The Brāhmaṇa arose from the mouth of the Puruṣa, O descendant of Kuru; because he came from the mouth, he became the chief among the varṇas, the Brāhmaṇa, the teacher. From His arms arose the Kṣatra, and the Kṣatriya followed it; born to protect the varṇas from injury. From the thighs of the Lord arose the people, who sustain the world; the Vaiśya arose from them, arranging the means of livelihood for humankind. From the feet of Bhagavān arose service in sacrifice for the accomplishment of dharma; the Śūdra was born in that service, by whose work Hari is pleased. These varṇas worship Hari, who leads to heaven, through their own duties, with faith, for the purification of the self, together with their respective practices.” (Śrīmad Bhāgavata Purāṇa 3.6.30–34.)

Uvaṭa says: “The Śūdras are understood to have been born from the feet, because they were produced from them.” Mahīdhara says: “The Brāhmaṇa is a person endowed with the jāti of Brāhmaṇa; he was the mouth of Prajāpati, which means that he was born from the mouth. The Rājanya is endowed with the jāti of Kṣatriya and was made from the arms, which means that he was produced from the arms. The Vaiśya took the form of Prajāpati’s thighs, which means that he was produced from the thighs. From His feet arose a man endowed with the jāti of Śūdra.”

Sāyaṇa too, in his commentary on the Ṛgvedic Puruṣa Sūkta, says: “The Brāhmaṇa, a person endowed with the jāti of Brāhmaṇa, was the mouth of Prajāpati, which means that he was born from the mouth. The Rājanya, endowed with the jāti of Kṣatriya, was made from the arms, which means that he was produced from the arms. The Vaiśya took the form of the thighs, which means that he was produced from the thighs; and from the feet arose a man endowed with the jāti of Śūdra.”

This very production of Brāhmaṇas and the others from the mouth and the rest is clearly recited in the seventh book of the Yajurveda, in the passage “He fashioned the Trivṛt from the mouth” and the like. Therefore both the question and its answer are to be understood in precisely this sense.

Patañjali’s view

Some say: “In the Mahābhāṣya, words such as Brāhmaṇa and the rest are used as words for qualities; therefore Brāhmaṇa-hood and the rest depend on qualities alone.” This springs from ignorance of the Mahābhāṣya, for it contradicts the statement, “Austerity, sacred learning, and birth make a Brāhmaṇa.”

Or it may be said that, in order to deceive the ignorant, austerity and sacred learning were mentioned, but birth was not. This too is answered. Under the rule on the negative particle nañ (Pāṇini 2.2.6), the question is raised: which member is primary in this compound? The Pradīpa explains that in ordinary usage a Kṣatriya and the like are denoted by the expression “non-Brāhmaṇa.” The discussion concerns the proper analysis of the formation. If the word Brāhmaṇa denotes a jāti, then one who lacks Brāhmaṇa-hood is a non-Brāhmaṇa, such as a Kṣatriya; in that expression, the other member is primary. The objections raised against taking either the first or the second member as primary are then answered. If the second member alone were primary, the instruction “Bring a non-Brāhmaṇa” would result in bringing any Brāhmaṇa. When it is objected, “If one says, ‘Bring the king’s man,’ why is just any man not brought?”, a distinguishing qualification is accepted there; in the same way, the negative particle qualifies the word Brāhmaṇa. Thus what is to be brought is the person specified as a non-Brāhmaṇa, not simply any Brāhmaṇa.

When asked, “What is that?”, the answer is, “The meaning of what is denied.” After setting out further questions and answers, it is said: “A word is used afterwards to make its meaning known. If that meaning has already been excluded, why is the word used?” To show the object of the negation, the meaning of Brāhmaṇa is therefore explained; the word Brāhmaṇa joined with the negative particle is used to state that its defining character is absent in a Kṣatriya and the rest. The qualities whose absence is natural in them are denied, while the presence of the other qualities is understood. In this connection the author of the Mahābhāṣya says: “These words apply to groups of qualities: Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, and Śūdra.”

According to the explanation in the Pradīpa, the word guṇa here refers to an attribute. Therefore birth, denoted by the word yoni, is also included in the group of qualities. The passage does not indicate a primary Brāhmaṇa-hood independent of birth. The Mahābhāṣya accordingly cites the teaching of the Smṛti: “Austerity, sacred learning, and birth make a Brāhmaṇa. One who is deficient in austerity and sacred learning is still a Brāhmaṇa by birth alone.” The Pradīpa explains: “Austerity means observances and other prescribed acts; sacred learning means study of the Veda and its ancillary disciplines; womb means birth from a Brāhmaṇa woman. Birth is what makes one a Brāhmaṇa and is the basis for the designation ‘Brāhmaṇa.’” Although the whole group of qualities may be absent, the word is used when one part of that group is present; hence the statement, “One who is deficient in austerity and sacred learning is still a Brāhmaṇa by birth alone.”

Some also take fair complexion, pure conduct, tawny coloring, and brown hair to be qualities of Brāhmaṇa-hood. The Pradīpa says that these are qualities commonly known and commonly observed. The Udyota explains that such qualities vary. Some people take visible traits, frequently seen in the world, to be qualities of the Brāhmaṇa group.

“Words used of a group are also used of its members,” says the Mahābhāṣya. For example, one speaks of the eastern Pāñcālas and the western Pāñcālas. In this way the word Brāhmaṇa, used of a group, is also applied to its members, both where birth is absent and where the qualities are absent. The Mahābhāṣya gives the examples: “This is a Brāhmaṇa who stands and urinates”; “This is a Brāhmaṇa who goes about eating.”

Following the Pradīpa, when the word Brāhmaṇa is used in reproach because austerity and sacred learning are absent, the birth remains; the word is applied to the member by treating him as belonging to the group. But when birth is absent, the word is used through uncertainty or bad information. Seeing someone fair, of pure conduct, tawny, and brown-haired, a person concludes, “This is a Brāhmaṇa.” Later he learns, “This is not a Brāhmaṇa.” In that case the word was used as praise, to suggest the presence of qualities such as study; once the absence of Brāhmaṇa birth is discovered, that meaning is withdrawn. The Mahābhāṣya also gives the case of a person who is wrongly directed: “Bring that Brāhmaṇa.” The messenger goes and sees someone, concludes, “This is a Brāhmaṇa,” and later learns that he is not. Here too the word was used because of the misleading instruction, and its birth-based meaning is then denied.

The Pradīpa explains that the absence of one part may be spoken of as the absence of the whole group, while the presence of another part may be spoken of as the presence of the group. It is like calling a collection absent when one part has been removed, or present because another part remains.

Even when birth as a Brāhmaṇa is present, the absence of austerity and sacred learning is censured; even when birth is as a Kṣatriya and the like, the presence of austerity and sacred learning is praised. Brāhmaṇa-hood is primary when both birth and those qualities are present. Just as lionhood is primary in a lion endowed with the qualities of courage and strength, while the word “lion” is used figuratively when the qualities are present without the birth, so one born in a Brāhmaṇa family and endowed with austerity and sacred learning is a Brāhmaṇa in the primary sense. One lacking them is a Brāhmaṇa by birth; a Kṣatriya endowed with them is called a Brāhmaṇa in a figurative and honorific sense.

A note on the phrase “because of birth”: without birth in a Brāhmaṇa lineage, Brāhmaṇa-hood does not arise. Austerity and sacred learning, however, bring about the excellence associated with Brāhmaṇa-hood, as in the Smṛti to be cited below: “He whose three [qualities] are pure …”

Baudhāyana’s Gṛhya-sūtra, in its first question and seventh chapter, sets out the stages: “One born from a Brāhmaṇa woman to a Brāhmaṇa is called ‘born’ before the upanayana. Having undergone the upanayana and studied some of the Veda, he is a Brāhmaṇa. Having studied one branch, he is a śrotriya; one who has studied the ancillary disciplines and recites them is an anūcāna; one who has studied the Kalpa is an ṛṣikalpa; one who studies and expounds the sūtras is a [learned Brāhmaṇa]; one who knows the four Vedas is a ṛṣi; beyond this he is a deva.” If the reading in the Smṛti cited in the commentary is “Brāhmaṇa by birth,” it means a Brāhmaṇa designated by birth.

Alternatively, the compound may mean “a Brāhmaṇa from birth alone.” Dakṣa says: “A child from birth is to be regarded as like an embryo until eight years of age, his form alone being manifest. In matters of eating, drinking, and what may or may not be spoken, there is no fault at that time so long as he has not undergone the upanayana. After the upanayana there is fault in doing what is censured; he is not yet fit for the observances, until he reaches sixteen years of age.” (Dakṣa Smṛti 1.)

Meaning of the word Rāja

“A king who desires sovereignty should sacrifice with the Rājasūya.” On this point the teachers of both Pūrvamīmāṃsā and Uttaramīmāṃsā agree: only a Kṣatriya is entitled to perform the Rājasūya; Brāhmaṇas and the others are not entitled, even when they are kings. Kṣatriyahood is by jāti, not by actions such as protecting subjects. In the Rājasūya procedure, the offerings and recitations are stated according to whether the sacrificer is a Brāhmaṇa, a Vaiśya, or a Rājanya. The question is whether these references merely identify the circumstances of those who are already entitled, or whether the rite is prescribed for all of them.

If rāja means one who performs the work of ruling, then the work of a king must first be determined. The objector says that Brāhmaṇas and others who protect the people and bear the royal insignia—a golden staff, a white parasol, and chowries—are called kings in Aryan usage, while the title is not applied in the same way to Kṣatriyas among non-Aryans. Therefore, the word should mean one who performs the work of sovereignty, and not a jāti alone; usage accepted among the learned, supported by scripture, should prevail over usage among those outside that tradition.

The reply considers words whose meanings differ between learned and ordinary speech. When there is doubt whether the meanings of words such as pika and nema should be determined from the Veda and the lexicons or from common usage, the meaning established in the relevant Vedic passage is accepted, since otherwise the meaning of the passage would be unsettled. Thus pika means “cuckoo,” nema means “half,” and tāmarasa means “lotus.” In these cases, as the meanings of pika and the like are fixed by usage, so the meaning of rāja is properly fixed by the usage that applies it to the Kṣatriya jāti. A Kṣatriya alone is therefore a king in the primary sense and alone has eligibility for the Rājasūya; Brāhmaṇas and Vaiśyas are not eligible for it.

It is objected that words such as yava and varāha also have different meanings in learned and non-learned speech. Their forms are the same, and their usages are ancient; therefore, they should be interpreted in either sense. The answer is that when a Vedic sentence supplies the reason for a meaning, that scriptural context determines it. In the passage about what is made of yava, the text says that while other plants wither, the yavas flourish. The accompanying verses describe the plants that lose their leaves in the summer and the yava plants standing in joy with their grain-bearing heads; they distinguish these from the priyangu plants that perish in one season and flourish in another. Likewise, the sentence about the varāha and the shoes is followed by the words, “The cows follow the varāha.” Cows follow and imitate a four-footed animal, not a shoe. Thus the Vedic context establishes the proper meanings of yava and varāha.

One may further object that this is not merely ordinary usage: Pāṇini’s rule, “After words denoting qualities and after Brāhmaṇa and the rest, also in the sense of action,” prescribes the suffix ṣyañ. From this, rāja plus its action yields rājyam, “kingship” or “the work of ruling.” The reply is that the grammatical derivation does not overrule the established meaning of rāja. If the stated conditions and the context show that the word is used in its familiar sense, it should not be made to mean something else. In the Rājasūya passage, the word denotes the Kṣatriya by jāti; the related word rājyam can denote the work associated with the king.

The usage of words among non-Aryans may have arisen at a particular time, and the usage among Aryans may also have arisen at a particular time, as with words such as gogauṇī and the like. Therefore, the word’s form alone does not decide between these usages. Where the Vedic context establishes a meaning, that meaning is authoritative; where the word is used in both ways, the established usage and the teaching of Pāṇini settle the question. The word rāja is thus primary for the Kṣatriya jāti and figurative for one who merely performs the work of ruling.

As the commentators explain, a word is to be understood in keeping with its connection in the sentence. If one abandons the expressed word, one must imagine an additional word; and when the relation between a cause and its result is separated by an intervening expression, that relation must also be supplied. “If one wishes for the result, then the Brāhmaṇa should sacrifice” is understood by supplying the wish for the result; the injunction is not thereby lost. In the prescribed sequence, the Bārhaspatya offering is placed between the Agneya and Aindra offerings, and the oblations are made and recited in the order stated. The relation between what is done and its result is understood from the sentence. As the Bhāṣya and the Kalpataru make clear, one injunction may be understood from the meaning and another may be stated directly.

The Parimala, after setting out proofs on both sides, concludes that in the Rājasūya sentence the word rāja must mean a Kṣatriya. A Kṣatriya jāti is a non-relative, undivided, and unrestricted basis for the word’s application; it is therefore proper, when there is no contrary proof, to take the meaning on which the word is directly established. Although the word rāja is found in both scriptural and ordinary usage, its power is established in Kṣatriyas. Their jāti provides a common defining feature that is not overextended to every person who protects someone.

Protection alone cannot be the defining cause of the word, for everyone protects something: people protect their children, spouses, wealth, or their own bodies. Nor is protection of a district sufficient. Even when a person appointed by a king holds an office and protects a district, the title rāja is not thereby applied to him. Nor is independent rule the defining cause, since a person may be consecrated as king over a particular country by an emperor and still not be a king in that sense.

The word rāja is also used without actual sovereignty, as in “king of beasts” and “king of birds,” where it indicates eminence alone. Pāṇini’s rule concerning compounds such as rājadaṃta also supports this usage. Therefore the meaning of rāja that is neither too narrow nor overextended is the Kṣatriya: among Kṣatriyas, protection and excellence are generally present, while occasional use of the word for another protector is a settled figurative usage. Śukra’s view

“The offspring in the three varṇas are joined with Brāhmaṇas.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 60.42.) Here offspring means descendants. Nīlakaṇṭha therefore says that because all the varṇas are born from Brāhmaṇas, even a Śūdra has a share in sacrifice. “Therefore the varṇas are mingled in orderly fashion with the jāti-varṇas; this is their transformation. Among them each has a Sāman, a Yajus, and a Ṛc, and one Brāhmaṇa is established.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 60.47.)

This is also consistent with the Kṣatriyas and the others being created as distinct jātis for the protection of the realm and other purposes; it does not establish the abolition of jāti. Though all arise from Brahmā, just as space, wind, and the other elements retain their distinct kinds, there is no contradiction in Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, and the others having distinct jātis though they have the same cause. Eligibility for sacrifice is inherited through tradition, not held independently by all, for this would contradict the direct śruti, “Therefore the Śūdra is not admitted to the sacrifice” (Taittirīya Saṃhitā 7.1.1.6), and the discussion concerning those outside the Śūdra class. For this reason Nīlakaṇṭha also speaks of a mental sacrifice.

Elevation of jāti

“From a Māhiṣya a Karaṇa is born, and a Ratha-kāra is born; mixed offspring are known as anuloma and pratiloma.” (Yājñavalkya Smṛti, Ācāra chapter.) In the tenth chapter of Manu, from verse 6 through verse 66, the many jātis beginning with Mūrdhāvasikta are likewise affirmed, as they are by other authors of Smṛti.

“Elevation of jāti is to be understood in the fifth or seventh generation; when actions are reversed, parity follows, and, as before, the order is from lower to higher.” (Yājñavalkya Smṛti, Ācāra 1.96.) Here the elevation of jāti—attainment of a higher jāti such as Brāhmaṇa—is understood in the fifth or seventh birth; the word “also” includes the sixth. This arrangement is fixed. A Niṣādī, born to a Brāhmaṇa by a Śūdra woman, is married to a Brāhmaṇa and bears a daughter; that daughter too is married to a Brāhmaṇa and bears a daughter. In this way, through the generations, a Brāhmaṇa is born in the seventh. An Ambaṣṭha, born to a Brāhmaṇa by a Vaiśya woman, by the same process gives rise to a Brāhmaṇa in the sixth generation. A Mūrdhāvasikta gives rise to one in the fifth. In the same way, an Ugra and a Māhiṣya, when married to Kṣatriyas, give rise respectively to a Kṣatriya in the sixth and fifth generations; a Karaṇa, married to a Vaiśya, gives rise to a Vaiśya in the fifth. The same is to be understood in other cases.

Moreover, when the occupations on which livelihood depends are reversed, Brāhmaṇas and the others attain a lower status. For example, if a Brāhmaṇa cannot live by his principal occupation, he may live by the work of a Kṣatriya as an alternative. If he cannot live by that either, he may live by the work of a Vaiśya; if still unable, by the work of a Śūdra. A Kṣatriya who cannot live by his own occupation may live by the work of a Vaiśya or a Śūdra; a Vaiśya who cannot live by his own occupation may live by that of a Śūdra. If, outside a time of distress, a person does not abandon that occupation, then in the seventh, sixth, or fifth generation he becomes equal to the jāti corresponding to it, in the order from higher to lower.

Thus a Brāhmaṇa who lives by a Śūdra’s occupation and does not abandon it gives rise to a Śūdra in the seventh generation. Living by a Vaiśya’s occupation gives rise to a Vaiśya in the sixth; living by a Kṣatriya’s occupation gives rise to a Kṣatriya in the fifth. In the same way, a Kṣatriya who lives by a Śūdra’s occupation gives rise to a Śūdra in the sixth, and one who lives by a Vaiśya’s occupation gives rise to a Vaiśya in the fifth. A Vaiśya who lives by a Śūdra’s occupation gives rise to a Śūdra in the fifth. As stated earlier, the lower and higher outcomes follow the reversal of occupations; the mixed jātis born by anuloma and pratiloma unions are described through the example of the Ratha-kāra.

Varṇa-saṅkara jātis

Now the jātis produced by the mixing of varṇas are set forth: the lower, the higher, and the order from lower to higher. Offspring produced among Mūrdhāvasiktas by Kṣatriyas, Vaiśyas, and Śūdras; among Ambaṣṭhas by Vaiśyas and Śūdras; and among Niṣādas by Śūdras are lower and born by pratiloma. Offspring produced by Brāhmaṇas among Mūrdhāvasiktas, Ambaṣṭhas, and Niṣādas; by Brāhmaṇas or Kṣatriyas among Māhiṣyas and Ugras; and by Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, or Vaiśyas among Karaṇas are higher and born by anuloma. The other cases are to be understood in the same way. This order of lower and higher is to be understood as stated earlier. “Where these varṇa-defilers are born, that realm is quickly destroyed together with its people.” (Manu Smṛti 10.61.)

Kauṭilya likewise advises preserving the order of varṇa and āśrama: “When boundaries and proper limits are established and the condition of varṇa and āśrama is maintained, the world, protected by the three Vedas, prospers and does not decline.” The mixing of varṇas and its consequences are explained at length.

The Brāhmaṇa’s status in the realm

“A Brāhmaṇa, when born on earth, is born as the highest, the lord of all beings, for the protection of the treasury of dharma.” “Whatever exists in the world is the Brāhmaṇa’s own; by the excellence of his birth, he is entitled to it all.” “The Brāhmaṇa eats his own food, wears his own garments, and gives his own wealth; through the Brāhmaṇa’s generosity the other people eat.” (Manu Smṛti 1.99–101.)

“One should regard a Brāhmaṇa of ten years and a king of a hundred years as father and son; of the two, the Brāhmaṇa is the father.” “Whether learned or unlearned, a Brāhmaṇa is a great divinity, just as fire, whether consecrated or not, is a great divinity.” (Manu Smṛti 2.135; 9.317.)

“Among beings, those that breathe are highest; among those that breathe, those endowed with understanding; among those endowed with understanding, human beings; among human beings, Brāhmaṇas; among Brāhmaṇas, the learned; among the learned, those with discernment; among those with discernment, the doers; among the doers, those who know Brahman.” “The very birth of a Brāhmaṇa is the eternal embodiment of dharma.” (Manu Smṛti 1.87, 1.98.)

“A man is declared purer above the navel; therefore the Self-born has declared his mouth to be the purest part of him. Because he was born from the highest limb, because he preserves the Veda, and because he is foremost, the Brāhmaṇa is lord of this whole creation. The Self-born, having performed austerities, created him from his own mouth for carrying the oblations and offerings to the gods and ancestors, for the sake of this whole creation.” (Manu Smṛti 1.92, 1.94.)

“Those communities in the world that are outside the groups born from the mouth, arms, thighs, and feet, whether their speech is that of mlecchas or that of Āryas, are all remembered as Dasyus.” (Manu Smṛti 10.45.)

Change of jāti through another birth

The following passages state that a higher varṇa is attained in later births through particular actions.

“Performing the Agnihotra and the Upasad, offering oblations according to rule, attending to every guest, and eating food left after the offering: when a Vaiśya performs the rites prescribed by the mantras of the three fires, he is born into a pure and eminent Kṣatriya family. That Vaiśya, born as a Kṣatriya, is sanctified from birth; initiated and devoted to vows, he becomes an honored twice-born. He gives, sacrifices with duly performed rites and fitting fees, studies while seeking heaven, and always tends the three fires. Endowed with knowledge and understanding, refined, and versed in the Veda, the Kṣatriya becomes a righteous Brāhmaṇa through his own action.” (Mahābhārata, Anuśāsana Parva 143.)

At the hermitage of a certain harmless ascetic, all creatures—lions, tigers, and others—stayed close to him. He was exceedingly fond of a certain dog. Once a leopard came there to eat it. Seeing the fierce animal, the dog, seeking to live, took refuge with the sage. To remove the danger, the sage turned the dog into a leopard. In succession, he made it a tiger, an elephant, a lion, and a śarabha. Having become a śarabha, it then set out to devour the sage himself. The sage said:

“You, a dog, attained the form of a leopard; the leopard became a tiger; the tiger became a mighty elephant; and the elephant attained the state of a lion. Having gained great strength, you became a śarabha again. Overcome with affection, I did not consider your lineage. Since you, wicked one, wish to kill me, who am innocent, you shall return to your own birth: you will indeed become a dog again.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 116.)

“Servants who are fit for each task should be placed and kept securely in their proper posts. A king surrounded by men of poor lineage does not prosper. An uncultivated man of poor lineage, on obtaining great wealth and power, may be despised and turn hostile. A high-born, educated, discerning man, accomplished in knowledge and understanding, should be appointed minister.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 118.)

“A śarabha should be placed in the station of a śarabha; a mighty lion in the station of a lion. A tiger should be appointed as a tiger, and a leopard likewise. Servants should be assigned their posts in due order according to the division of duties. Men should not all be appointed indiscriminately; appoint the virtuous, well-born, brave, learned, and free from envy. A dog should not be honored as fit to leave its own station; when raised from its place to another, it goes astray. Those endowed with the qualities of their own jāti and established in their own duties should be appointed as ministers; assigning them to an unsuitable place is not proper.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 119.)

If there were no intermediate jātis among human beings, who would be one’s own jāti, and who another’s? Who would occupy the place of a dog, a lion, or a śarabha? All these statements would be devoid of meaning.

Discussion of Śūdra’s eligibility for sacrifice

“The mantras Svāhā and Vaṣaṭ are not prescribed for a Śūdra. Therefore the Śūdra should perform the Pāka sacrifices himself, without Vedic observances. The sacrificial fee for a Pāka sacrifice is said to be a full vessel. We have heard that a Śūdra named Paijavana gave a hundred thousand full vessels as the fee according to the Aindrāgni rite.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 60.)

“Eight handfuls make a small measure; eight such measures make a puṣkala; four puṣkalas are called a full vessel.”

But the revealed text says, “Therefore a Śūdra is not admitted to sacrifice” (Taittirīya Saṃhitā 7.1.1.6). How, then, can there be a sacrifice for a Śūdra? The answer is: “Sacrifice belongs to all the varṇas, O Bhārata; among all sacrifices, the sacrifice of faith is prescribed as foremost.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 60.)

Nīlakaṇṭha says: “The fires of Upakośala imparted the knowledge of Brahman to him as he served his teacher’s fires with faith. In the same way, a Śūdra who serves Brāhmaṇas and others with faith indeed shares in the fruit of a sacrifice performed by a Brāhmaṇa.”

“Great faith itself is the deity, and what the worshippers offer is pure. O Brāhmaṇas, each one’s own deity is supreme. They performed sacrificial sessions here, intent on their several enduring desires; the offspring in the three varṇas are joined with the Brāhmaṇas.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 60.41–42.) “Offspring” is the meaning of “creations.” Since all the varṇas are born from Brāhmaṇas, it follows that a Śūdra too is entitled to sacrifice. Thus Nīlakaṇṭha explains.

“Whatever even the gods among the gods declare is for the highest good. Therefore the sacrifices of all varṇas are joined together, not for desire. One who knows the Ṛg, Yajus, and Sāman should always be honored like a god; one who knows none of the Ṛg, Yajus, or Sāman is a servant of Prajāpati. O Bhārata, sacrifice through understanding is prescribed for all varṇas.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 60.)

Nīlakaṇṭha explains: “All sacrifices, Śrauta and Smārta, are naturally joined with all the varṇas, including Śūdras, and are not performed for desire. A twice-born man of the three varṇas is to be honored by a Śūdra. Because he attends upon them, the Śūdra is a servant. Though without the Veda, he belongs to Prajāpati, and Prajāpati is his deity, just as the Brāhmaṇa is associated with one deity and the Kṣatriya with Indra. In a mental sacrifice—the sacrifice consisting in the offering of an object to a deity—all varṇas are entitled.”

“The gods do not seek the sacrifice of one who performs it in this way, nor do other people. Therefore a sacrifice of faith is prescribed among all varṇas.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 60.)

“The Brāhmaṇas once continually sacrificed to their own deity through their own rites, and they also sacrificed for the other varṇas. The Vaiśya was lower and mixed; among the varṇas, the Brāhmaṇa was the source of sacrifice. Therefore the varṇas are orderly, are varṇas by jāti, and are joined as his transformations. One Sāman, one Yajus, one Ṛc, and one Brāhmaṇa are seen among them.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 60.)

Since the Brāhmaṇa is the deity particular to all three varṇas, the Brāhmaṇas did not refrain from sacrificing for the other varṇas. They declared, “I have been appointed by so-and-so, for such-and-such desire, to perform such-and-such sacrifice,” and performed the rite. This rule is to be understood from the account of ancient practice. The use of the parasmaipada form “they sacrificed” is fitting, since the fruit of the action goes to another. “Lower” means inferior. “Vitāna” means the spreading of the fires; in arrangements with five altars, these are to be understood as ordinary fires, and where there is one altar, there is only one ordinary fire. The Gṛhya authors begin, “Wherever one intends to make an offering,” and explain that mantras are also associated with it. Even in a Śrauta sacrifice the fire for the southern altar is brought from the house of a wealthy Vaiśya. For this reason, the Brāhmaṇa is the source of sacrifice among the three varṇas.

If it is objected that, even if there is this equality, there is no equality of jāti, the answer is this: the Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, and Śūdra jātis too are transformations of the Brāhmaṇa, joined through offspring born from Kṣatriya and other women, such as the Mūrdhāvasikta. Thus all the varṇas are joined with the Brāhmaṇa by dharma and by birth. When their true nature is determined, the first Brāhmaṇa was created like Brahmā, as the source of the Brāhmaṇa lineage; all of them are Brāhmaṇas. The example is this: one Sāman, one sound “A”—all speech—when expressed through unvoiced sounds and the like, becomes many forms. Just as the one syllable “A” takes the forms of Sāman and the rest, Brahman itself appears in the forms of Brāhmaṇas and the others. Thus Nīlakaṇṭha explains.

Here the ancient knowers recite the verses of the song of sacrifice, O King, for the Vaikhānasa sages who wish to sacrifice: “Whether the sun has risen or not, the faithful, self-controlled man offers to the fire according to dharma; faith is indeed a great cause. What has spilled comes first, and what has not spilled comes after. There are many forms of sacrifice and varied fruits of action.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 60.)

Nīlakaṇṭha explains: “The Bahvṛca Brāhmaṇa teaches sixteen forms of the Agnihotra, beginning with the Rudra, the cow, and the Vāyavya. In the Vāyavya rite, even what spills is part of the offering, and is therefore mentioned first. What is offered according to the Agnihotra rule is the latter and highest. ‘Many’ refers to the forms beginning with the Rudra; these are the sixteen forms of the Agnihotra.”

“Whoever understands these, settled through the determination of knowledge, a twice-born person endowed with faith, is fit to sacrifice. Whether a thief, a sinner, or the greatest sinner, whoever wishes to perform a sacrifice is called good. The sages praise him; this is indeed good, without doubt. The conclusion is that all varṇas should always sacrifice. Nothing in the three worlds is equal to sacrifice. Therefore they say that a person should sacrifice without envy, taking refuge in faith and purity, and giving according to his ability and desire.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 60.) Nīlakaṇṭha supplies the omitted word “scripture” after “taking refuge.”

Birth-based varṇa in the Chāndogya

The Chāndogya and other Vedic texts, together with their commentaries, state that Brāhmaṇa-hood and the other conditions arise by birth. Thus:

“Those here whose conduct is lovely will quickly attain a lovely womb: the womb of a Brāhmaṇa, a Kṣatriya, or a Vaiśya. Those here whose conduct is foul will quickly attain a foul womb: the womb of a dog, a pig, or a Caṇḍāla.” (Chāndogya Upaniṣad 5.10.7.)

Among these, those whose conduct here in this world is lovely—that is, whose way of life and character are good—are called “those of lovely conduct,” marked by such conduct. Those whose actions are auspicious and whose deeds yield merit are called so. Those whose conduct is cruel and deceitful are of foul conduct. Through that residual merit from good action, after the remainder of its fruit in the lunar world, they quickly attain a lovely womb—a Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, or Vaiśya womb—each according to their own action. Conversely, those whose conduct is the opposite, marked by evil deeds and an impure disposition, quickly attain a foul womb according to their own action: a dog’s, a pig’s, or a Caṇḍāla’s.

By this it is explained that birth in the womb of a Brāhmaṇa and the others is attained according to the residue of action accumulated in a previous birth. Just as there is a distinction between the wombs of a dog and a pig, the distinction between the wombs of Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, and the others is also stated and explained.

Śaṅkara’s view

“Then he churned. From the mouth and from the womb of the hands he created Agni. Therefore both are hairless on the inside, for the womb is hairless on the inside.” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.6.)

Śaṅkara explains: “Prajāpati placed his hands at his mouth and churned, rubbing them toward the mouth. From the mouth and from the two womb-like hands he created Agni, the benefactor of the Brāhmaṇa jāti. The Brāhmaṇa too was born from Prajāpati’s mouth. Since they have one source, the elder Brāhmaṇa is joined with Agni as the younger. Therefore the Brāhmaṇa has Agni as his deity. This is well known from the Mahābhārata and the Smṛtis: the Brāhmaṇa is born from the power of the mouth.”

“Likewise, from the two arms, which are the support of strength, he created the Kṣatriya, who governs the Kṣatriya jāti. Therefore the Kṣatra is known in Śruti and Smṛti as the strength of the arms. From the thighs, which are the support of effort and activity and are characterized by livelihood, he created the Vaiśya, who governs trade and cattle-rearing and is devoted to agriculture. And from the feet he created the Śūdra, able to serve. This is established in Śruti and Smṛti.”

There, the creation of the Kṣatriya and the other deities is stated; the commentary concludes by summarizing the entirety of creation and what is to be said later.

By this it is clearly understood that Bhagavatpāda explains the distinct creation of the Brāhmaṇa and the others from Prajāpati’s mouth and other limbs, and accepts the varṇa order and the Brāhmaṇa and other jātis as arising by birth.

Birth-based varṇa in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka

“In the beginning this was Brahman alone. Being one, it did not flourish. It created a superior form, Kṣatra. Among the gods, the Kṣatriyas are Indra, Varuṇa, Soma, Rudra, Parjanya, Yama, Mṛtyu, and Īśāna. There is nothing higher than Kṣatra. Therefore, at the Rājasūya, the Brāhmaṇa worships the Kṣatriya from below and confers that glory upon Kṣatra. Brahman is the source of this Kṣatra. Therefore, though a king reaches the highest position, in the end he rests upon Brahman, his own source. Whoever injures that source becomes worse, as one who injures a superior.” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.11.)

“In the beginning this was Brahman alone. Having created Agni and entered the form of Agni, it is called Brahman because of its identification with the Brāhmaṇa jāti. The Kṣatra and the other jātis were also Brahman alone, without distinction. That one Brahman unfolded into Kṣatra and the other forms through action. Thinking, ‘I am the Brāhmaṇa; this is what I must do,’ it created the excellent form that is the cause of the Brāhmaṇa jāti. What was that form? Kṣatra, the Kṣatriya jāti.” (Śaṅkara’s commentary.)

Can there now be any doubt among the followers of Śaṅkara about the relation of Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, and the other jātis? Or is this statement of the Teacher due to confusion? “Indra is king of the gods, Varuṇa of the waters, Soma of the Brāhmaṇas, Rudra of cattle, Parjanya of lightning and the rest, Yama of the ancestors, Mṛtyu, and Īśāna of the hosts.” Śaṅkara’s commentary then says that the Kṣatriyas and other classes among human beings are to be understood in the same way as these deities.

“He did not flourish. He created the Viś: the groups of gods called the Vasus, Rudras, Ādityas, Viśvedevas, and Maruts. He did not flourish. He created the Śūdra varṇa, Pūṣan. This earth is Pūṣan, for it nourishes all that exists.” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.12–13.)

“That Brahman became Kṣatra, Viś, and Śūdra. Among the gods it became Agni; among human beings, the Brāhmaṇa. Through the Kṣatriya it became Kṣatriya, through the Vaiśya it became Vaiśya, and through the Śūdra it became Śūdra. Therefore the gods seek their world through Agni, and human beings through the Brāhmaṇa, for Brahman became these two forms.”

This is the creation of the four varṇas: Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatra, Viś, and Śūdra. What was created as Brahman among the gods became Agni alone; among human beings it became Brāhmaṇa in the form of a Brāhmaṇa. In the other varṇas it attained other forms: through the Kṣatriya it became Kṣatriya, through the Vaiśya it became Vaiśya, and through the Śūdra it became Śūdra. Since Brahman is modified as Kṣatra and the other forms, but remains unmodified as Agni, the gods seek the fruit of action through rites connected with Agni. For this purpose Brahman is established in the form of Agni as the basis of action. It is therefore fitting to perform a rite in that Agni and seek its fruit. Among human beings, when they seek the fruit of action, no rite such as Agnihotra is required; rather, fulfillment of their aims comes through the attainment of their birth-based nature. Only where fulfillment depends on the gods is action connected with Agni and the other rites required.

“By recitation alone a Brāhmaṇa may attain perfection; of this there is no doubt. Whether he performs other acts or not, the Brāhmaṇa who is friendly to all is so called.” (Manu Smṛti 2.87.) This is also seen in the teaching on protection. (Dāna Bhāṣya.)

The initiated person’s Brāhmaṇa status

It is said that through initiation even a Vaiśya or Kṣatriya becomes a Brāhmaṇa, and therefore Brāhmaṇa-hood and the other conditions arise from action. This is not so; the meaning has not been understood.

In the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, in the third kāṇḍa, second prapāṭhaka, first brāhmaṇa, sections 36–40, the initiated person is said to be a Brāhmaṇa because he is born from Brahman in the form of the sacrifice. It says: “Then one announces, ‘This man is initiated; this man is a Brāhmaṇa.’ By announcing him, one presents him to the gods: ‘This mighty man has reached the sacrifice; he has become one among you; protect him.’ One says this three times, because the sacrifice is threefold.”

Before this, in the discussion of the Jyotiṣṭoma, the construction of the hall and the consecrated entry are described, followed by the rites of initiation and the initiation offerings. Initiation in water and initiation with the black antelope skin are also described. There the announcement of the initiated person is assigned to the Pratiprasthātṛ. Its meaning is this: an Adhvaryu loudly proclaims, “This Brāhmaṇa is initiated; this Brāhmaṇa is initiated.” By this announcement he presents the man to the gods, saying, “This mighty one has reached the sacrifice; he has become one of you; protect him.” He says it aloud three times, because the sacrifice is threefold.

The Śruti itself states the purpose of the word “Brāhmaṇa” in this announcement: “Before this he is indeed unborn. Before this the Rākṣasas approach the woman; when the Rākṣasas deposit the seed, then he is born. This man is born from the sacrifice, from Brahman. Therefore even a Kṣatriya or a Vaiśya, when initiated, should be called a Brāhmaṇa. For he who is born from the sacrifice is born from Brahman. Therefore they say, ‘One must not kill the Soma sacrificer; whoever kills the Soma sacrificer incurs sin.’”

Thus, before this initiation, his birth is regarded as not yet complete. The reason is given: at conception the Rākṣasas approach the woman and deposit the seed; birth from the womb occurs in that way. The initiated person, however, is born from the sacrifice, from Brahman, and is therefore spoken of as a Brāhmaṇa. Thus even an initiated Kṣatriya or Vaiśya is called a Brāhmaṇa. (Kātyāyana Śrauta Sūtra 7.1.104.)

To praise birth from the sacrifice, a rule is stated: one must not kill a Soma sacrificer, one who presses Soma and performs the rite, even if he is one of the three varṇas; killing him on account of that rite incurs sin. The praise of initiation here is clear. Therefore this is only a secondary and temporary Brāhmaṇa-hood, arising from association with a particular quality, not Brāhmaṇa-hood by jāti. Thus even initiated Kṣatriyas and Vaiśyas do not have the office of the officiating priest, since that office is prescribed for the Brāhmaṇa jāti alone.

Manu too speaks only of the Brāhmaṇa: “He who, having initiated a pupil, teaches him the Veda …” (Manu Smṛti 2.190). He likewise speaks of the teacher, preceptor, and spiritual guide as Brāhmaṇa roles.

The Brāhmaṇa’s excellence

By distinction, by the superiority of the original creation, by the observance of discipline, and by the special character of the saṃskāra, the Brāhmaṇa is lord among the varṇas. (Manu Smṛti 10.3.)

The Brāhmaṇa’s superiority is stated to arise from the excellence of his jāti and from his birth from the highest limb of Hiraṇyagarbha:

“The Brāhmaṇa, the Kṣatriya, and the Vaiśya are the three twice-born varṇas. The fourth, the Śūdra, is once-born; there is no fifth.” (Manu Smṛti 10.4.)

The same-varṇa status of Kuṇḍa, Golaka, and others

“Children of the same varṇa are indeed born from men and women of the same varṇa. From blameless marriages are born sons who increase the lineage.” (Yājñavalkya Smṛti 1.90.)

From men of the varṇas beginning with Brāhmaṇa, sons of the same jāti are born from women of the corresponding varṇas beginning with Brāhmaṇī; they are similar, that is, sons of the same jāti as their parents. (Mitākṣarā.)

By saying, “This is the rule remembered among the varṇas,” Yājñavalkya determined that Brāhmaṇa-hood and the other jātis belong to offspring produced in this way alone. By the stated rule, a man joined with a wife of the same varṇa has offspring of the same jāti. Therefore Kuṇḍa, Golaka, Kānīna, Sahodha, and the like are said not to be of the same varṇa.

The jāti of Brāhmaṇas and the others, however, is marked by remembrance. Just as, though Brāhmaṇas are alike, gotra is marked by remembrance—as Kauṇḍinya, Vāsiṣṭha, Atri, and Gautama—so, among human beings who are alike, jāti such as Brāhmaṇa-hood is marked by remembrance.

The claim that, on account of weak digestive fire, even one’s own are cast out is trivial. Only those without discrimination between what may and may not be eaten can consume everything. No one can digest stone, wood, poison, and the like; even a strong digestive fire impels one to eat only what is suitable, not what is unsuitable. The body is certainly destroyed by eating what is forbidden and harmful. Those who are fit must indeed be received through expiation and the like. But if the unruly and self-willed are received, they will destroy society itself. Therefore, just as it is proper to exclude one whose condition is difficult to cure, so too their exclusion is not improper.

The beginninglessness of the tradition

Some say here: At first there was one Veda; through division into five branches it later became many. Then many branches disappeared. It is possible that one day all the branches may disappear. From the observed decline, the disappearance of those that remain may also be inferred. In this way, the Veda’s being authorless and eternal, and the like, becomes doubtful; and varṇāśrama and its conduct likewise become open to doubt.

This is not so. The Veda, the beginningless and endless utterance, is always present in the hearts of the eternal Lord’s recipients of grace and of the sages. Thus, even if the tradition is lost in some place and it is said there that the Veda has disappeared, it suffers no loss. For example:

“One who knows the domain of the ṛṣis is freed from the body and the rest. Having crossed to the far shore of darkness, he is honored in the heavenly world. The day of Brahmā is said to last a thousand yugas; throughout that time he shines in heaven above Nākas like the sun in the sky. Then, at the beginning of the Kṛta Yuga, the famous sage, purified by Brahman, all-knowing and steadfast, is born again, remembering.” (Sāmavedīya Devatādhyāya Brāhmaṇa.)

By this it is established that, through austerity, recitation, and the like, the power to behold the eternal perfected mantras is attained by divine power.

The Purāṇas’ agreement

In the Brahma Purāṇa:

“When that pure Kṛta Yuga begins again, the people who survived the Kali Yuga are then born as people of the Kṛta age. The perfected ones remain here, unseen as they wander; together with the Seven Sages, they are established there. Those remembered here as the seed—Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, Vaiśyas, and Śūdras—exist along with those born in Kali and are then without distinction. The Seven Sages teach dharma to them and to others. It is twofold, joined with the conduct of varṇa and āśrama: śrauta and smārta. Then, when they are engaged in rites, the people continue in the Kṛta age; the dharma of the śrauta and smārta rites is shown by the Seven Sages. Some remain here to establish dharma until the end of the age; those sages remain in positions of authority over the Manvantaras. As, after a forest fire in the dry season, new growth appears from the roots when the first rain comes, so the people of the Kṛta age arise among those born in Kali. Thus, from age to age, lineage continues without interruption until the end of the Manvantara.”

Yājñavalkya also says:

“There are eighty-eight thousand householding sages, who return again, are the seed, and set dharma in motion. Since there are the Vedas, Purāṇas, branches of knowledge, and Upaniṣads, as well as verses, sūtras, commentaries, and whatever else there is in literature.” (Prāyaścitta chapter 186–189.)

In the Vāyu Purāṇa as well:

“The perfected ones remain here and move about, together with the Seven Sages who are established there. Those remembered here as the seed—Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, Vaiśyas, and Śūdras—the Seven Sages teach dharma to them and to others. This dharma, joined with the conduct of varṇa and āśrama, is twofold: śrauta and smārta. They remain here to establish dharma until the end of the age; the sages remain in their appointed Manvantaras. As, when grasses have been burned by a forest fire in the dry season, new growth first appears from their roots with the rain, so the generations of one age continue into the next. They proceed without interruption until the end of the Manvantara.” (Vāyu Purāṇa.)

From this, the beginninglessness and endlessness of the Vedas and other scriptures, the dharma stated in them, and the system of transmission grounded in birth are clearly understood.

Jāti and evolution

Among human beings too, distinctions such as white and yellow occur by jāti, and changes in color, form, and the like are seen. Differences of climate, region, and time, and differences in the predominance or lack of predominance of kapha, vāta, pitta, and the like, bring differences in color, form, and disposition. Just as a woman’s conception is affected by environmental changes and other factors, the effects of food, environment, and the like upon women and men appear in their offspring as well. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka says:

“If one desires, ‘May my son be born fair, recite one Veda, and live his full lifespan,’ the two should cook rice with milk and eat it with clarified butter, wishing to beget a child. If one desires, ‘May my son be tawny and yellowish, recite two Vedas, and live his full lifespan,’ the two should cook rice with curds and eat it with clarified butter, wishing to beget a child. There is likewise an instruction for one who desires, ‘May my son be dark, red-eyed, and a teacher of three Vedas.’” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.14–16.)

The color, form, and taste of flowers and fruits can be changed by external treatments such as sprinkling them with juices and cultivating them. Change of form through present and former actions is accepted by scripture. Change of jāti through the boons and curses of gods and ṛṣis, and through powerful merit and sin, as in the cases of Nahusha, Nandī, and others, is accepted by the Yoga Śāstras. The omniscient Supreme Lord is fully able, in accordance with the karma of each, to create beings with their respective distinctive forms, colors, and the like. Inert nature and atoms, being unconscious, have no power in this matter.

Fairness and the like are not the basis of jāti

The idea that Āryas are fair and non-Āryas dark is entirely baseless. It is well known that Rāma and Bharata, born of King Daśaratha, were dark, while Lakṣmaṇa and Śatrughna were fair; Balarāma and Kṛṣṇa, born of Vasudeva, were fair and dark respectively. Vyāsa and others too were dark.

Indeed, given India’s six seasons and the differences among its regions, it is reasonable that people with various colors, forms, and the like should appear here. Thus, even where Ārya status and Brāhmaṇa status are the same among Maithilas, Bengalis, people of Pañcanada and the northern regions, Maharashtrians, Drāviḍas, and others, differences in body, color, voice, and the like are plainly seen. Among the gods too, Śiva is fair, Viṣṇu dark, and Brahmā red. Mahākālī, Mahālakṣmī, and Mahāsarasvatī are likewise held to differ in appearance. Since sattva is white, those in whom it predominates are white; since rajas is red, those in whom it predominates are red; and since tamas is black, those conditioned by it are black. By the same account, Viṣṇu was white, red, and yellow in different ages and has now become dark (Bhāgavata 10.8.13).

According to the Yogatattva Upaniṣad, earth is yellow, water white, fire red, air black, and space smoky. Differences in color due to the predominance of the respective elements are also fitting.

Astrological corroboration

The colors of the grahas—Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, and Saturn—are known respectively as copper, white, red, green, yellow, variegated, and black. A person’s color also accords with the ruling planet, according to the strength of the ascendant and navāṃśa rulers at birth. Complexion arises according to the strength of the lord of the ascendant’s navāṃśa, the planet placed in the ascendant, or the planet aspecting it.

“The body is like the lord of the rising navāṃśa, or like the most powerful planet; the complexion is that of the navāṃśa occupied by the Moon, and the body’s limbs are assigned by the signs beginning with the rising sign.” (Bṛhatjātaka 5.23.)

Even there, gradations of complexion arise according to differences of region, time, and jāti.

Views on jāti purity

Even according to the evolutionists’ account, sometimes through a sudden upheaval jātis with defects arise, while at that same time jātis endowed with good qualities continue to live. The effects of region and climate are reflected in forms and constitutions such as those of the Maithilas and others. Merit is also a cause of beauty, sweetness, grace of the limbs, and the like among living beings; gradations in these qualities arise from gradations of merit. Those whose nature is under their control through yoga or austerities can, by the force of resolve, change jāti in that very birth by bringing about the beginning of a jāti through the transformation and combination of atoms. By the words of such persons alone, Nahusha’s becoming a serpent and Nandī’s becoming divine occurred in that very birth. For ordinary people, speech follows meaning; for the primordial ṛṣis, meaning follows speech:

“For ordinary righteous people, speech follows meaning; but for the primordial ṛṣis, meaning follows their words.”

It is said that when a dark dog is joined with a white dog, four puppies are born: one white, one dark, and the remaining two mixed. In the next generation, among sixteen puppies there is one white, one dark, and the remaining fourteen mixed in color. In the third generation, among sixty-four puppies, two follow the colors of the parents and the rest are mixed. In this way, even among mixed breeds, some pure offspring of the mother’s or father’s jāti appear. Similarly, from the intermixture of white Westerners and Mongols, some appear Western and some Mongolian, while most resemble Iranians and Persians. Thus the claim that Persians and others are mixed is weak, since the opposite is seen elsewhere. Likewise, among mangoes produced by crossbreeding, no mango of the original jāti is seen.

It is also said, “Equality of form and color among individuals, excellence, roundness, and distinctness of the limbs are signs of the Śūdra jāti.” This too is false, because it has no authoritative basis. We see people with distinct limbs and people without distinct limbs among the jātis; no special difference there is caused by mixture or lack of mixture.

What is said—that among Indians purity arises in a later generation, whether the fifth, sixth, or seventh—is also trivial, because the matter is not understood. The method of attaining pure jāti in this case is different; it was explained while interpreting the statement, “Jāti’s elevation is known in the fifth or seventh generation.” (Yājñavalkya Smṛti 1.96.) If a Śūdra begets a daughter with a Brāhmaṇa woman, and she, having been married to a Brāhmaṇa, also begets a daughter, then in the seventh generation she gives birth to a pure Brāhmaṇa; for it is said that in the fifth or seventh generation the offspring is of the father’s jāti.

The difference of jāti produced by intermixture of blood is already covered in the descriptions of mixed jātis. Although such intermixture may occur in a foreign environment, it does not occur among families formed by Vedic saṃskāras. In her father’s family a woman is governed by the duties of her parents; in her husband’s family she remains under the authority of her father-in-law, husband, and sons. Married before menstruation, she lives secluded in her husband’s household, does not converse with her brothers-in-law, and is not free to come and go among the family or to form outside relations; thus she is considered entirely pure. The duties of a devoted wife and the other observances preserve her purity. Even though the Maithila, Bengali, and other Brāhmaṇas differ in complexion, speech, and the like, their Brāhmaṇa status remains the same. Just as among blue, red, white, and other kinds of cattle, the gotra and pravara are seen to be the same everywhere.

Varṇa gained according to occupation

Some say: The declaration of Brāhmaṇahood, and their being described as begging and the like, of Yudhiṣṭhira and the other Pāṇḍavas—foremost among Kṣatriyas, truthful, and devoted to dharma—during their forest dwelling and at Draupadī’s svayaṃvara, show only that their Brāhmaṇahood was based on qualities, actions, and the like. For at Draupadī’s svayaṃvara, saying, “We are Brāhmaṇas,” they were situated among the Brāhmaṇas and were treated as Brāhmaṇas.

The Mahābhārata’s corroboration

At Draupadī’s svayaṃvara, they were among the Brāhmaṇas and were treated as Brāhmaṇas. (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva 160.39.4.)

And by action, it is said: “I am pleased with you, O foremost of Brāhmaṇas, with your strength of arm in battle, your fearlessness, and your mastery of weapons and missiles.” By this Arjuna is called foremost among the Brāhmaṇas. (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva 160.16.)

Arjuna too declared of himself, “I am a Brāhmaṇa, foremost among warriors.” (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva.)

It is also said that Karṇa withdrew from battle, thinking the Brāhmaṇa’s radiance unconquerable: “Thus addressed, Rādheya Karṇa withdrew from battle, thinking that Brāhmaṇa radiance to be unconquerable, O mighty warrior.” (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva 190.)

Thus, in many passages the Pāṇḍavas declared their Brāhmaṇahood. From this it is understood that in those days an Ārya could, according to his own desire and preference, attain whichever varṇa—Brāhmaṇa and the like—he wished and undertake the duties of whichever varṇa he wished to perform. There was no strict bond of varṇa; by adopting the respective qualities, actions, and attire, he could easily change his varṇa.

Refutation of this view

That is trivial. If the attainment of varṇa and its duties according to one’s own desire is admitted, it conflicts with statements such as, “Death in one’s own dharma is better; another’s dharma is fraught with danger.” (Bhagavad Gītā 3.35.) Since preferences are unstable, the order too would become unstable.

The Pāṇḍavas, however, concealed their identities and declared themselves Brāhmaṇas while passing their time. In the episode of Jarāsandha’s killing, Kṛṣṇa and Bhīma too represented themselves as Brāhmaṇas by wearing that guise. Even after the dice episode, in the city of Virāṭa, they remained in other disguises, concealing their Kṣatriya identity. Their conduct as Brāhmaṇas for that purpose does not establish that their essential nature was Brāhmaṇahood.

Thus, by taking another guise and behaving accordingly for a purpose, there is no real attainment of the respective varṇa and its duties. Moreover, if varṇa could be changed at will by adopting dress and the like, what would make Brāhmaṇa radiance unconquerable? (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva 190.23.)

A Kṣatriya’s conduct as a Brāhmaṇa

It is also said, “A Kṣatriya cannot act according to the conduct of a Brāhmaṇa, since the duties of a Brāhmaṇa are forbidden to a Kṣatriya.” That too is not so: “In distress, a ruler may live by the occupation of a Vaiśya or by hunting; or he may act in the form of a Brāhmaṇa, but never by the occupation of a dog.” (Śrīmad Bhāgavata 11.17.48.)

Because a Kṣatriya is permitted to pass time in an emergency in the form of a Brāhmaṇa, there is no fault in it.

Varṇa classification by the teacher

It is said: Just as a royal physician skilled in examining diseases determines them, so an impartial teacher skilled in examining human nature, form, and varṇa can discern a child’s qualities from his form even in childhood. Thus, by determining the qualities and actions that are specially manifest, Brāhmaṇahood and the like can be determined.

“But the teacher, learned in the Veda, duly produces for him through the Sāvitrī the jāti that is true, ageless, and deathless.” (Manu 2.148.) From this statement it is said that, by examining the boy’s form, divisions, pronunciation, and the like, and by imparting the Sāvitrī, the teacher produces or reveals the jāti of the Brāhmaṇa and the other twice-born; that jāti is deathless. Here “jāti” means Brāhmaṇahood and the like. It is not established by birth, but produced by the teacher.

“Through study, vows, offerings, knowledge of the three Vedas, worship, offspring, the great sacrifices, and sacrifices, the Brāhmī body is made.” (Manu 2.28.) Parents produce only the body, not the jāti of Brāhmaṇahood and the like; those are produced only by the corresponding qualities and actions. “The birth which a mother and father mutually produce through desire should be understood as the physical birth from the womb.” (Manu 2.147.)

Here, as in Ayurveda, which provides means for examining diseases, no means of examining or determining Brāhmaṇahood and the like is found. The naming rite is seen on the tenth day; a Brāhmaṇa’s name is given with the ending śarman and the like. At that time, no one’s qualities and actions can be determined.

Likewise, even when a boy of five or eight years and the like is invested with the sacred thread as a Brāhmaṇa, it is impossible then to know the qualities and actions that determine Brāhmaṇahood. Although one may determine the development of knowledge and competence, knowledge of who is a Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, or the like is altogether impossible. For that reason, in the Rājasūya sacrifice it is determined in Mīmāṃsā that the word “king” is based on jāti, not on action. The rule “appearance reveals qualities” determines common qualities such as wickedness and nobility when they occur along with other traits; it does not independently determine Brāhmaṇahood and the like.

Brāhmaṇahood and the like grasped by a single declaration

Therefore, when Brāhmaṇas and others are assembled, no one can determine their varṇa by appearance. Jāti is understood through the ascertainment of Brāhmaṇahood and the like, but a jāti determined once by birth is accepted. Even so, because of human failings, doubt about Brāhmaṇahood may arise: “Are we Brāhmaṇas or non-Brāhmaṇas?” If it were by action, there would be no room for such doubt.

In the statement, “the jāti which the teacher produces,” the word “jāti” means birth, as Kullūka and others explain it. Thus, the teacher who knows the Veda duly produces the student’s birth through the Sāvitrī, with the complete investiture and recitation of the Sāvitrī; it is deathless because its result is attainment of Brahman. Through Vedic study preceded by initiation and the practice of its meaning, one who is without desire attains liberation.

A twice-born person has two births: one produced by the mother and father, and the second by the teacher. The teacher too invests him only after examining his Brāhmaṇa status and the like. Therefore, initiation is prescribed only for Brāhmaṇas and the others, not for Śūdras. If twice-born status could be created by rites performed at will, why would their prescription be restricted to Brāhmaṇas and the others? A rule can be prescribed only for what is already established, not for what remains to be produced. Therefore, initiation and the other rites are prescribed with Brāhmaṇahood and the like already established; all rites are prescribed for those already established as Brāhmaṇas and the others. Thus twice-born status arises through two births, and no one can be assigned Brāhmaṇahood and the like merely according to qualities chosen at will.

“Through the great sacrifices and sacrifices the Brāhmī body is made.” (Manu 2.28.) This refers to the making of a body fit for attainment of Brahman. Thus, a body fit for attaining Brahman and the self associated with it are made, since liberation is attained through knowledge of Brahman assisted by action. Here the making of a Brāhmaṇa jāti is not stated; there is no authority for it. The statement that birth alone makes one a Brāhmaṇa is a mistaken utterance. Śruti speaks of a Brāhmī body, not of a Brāhmaṇa jāti.

The doctrine of varṇa change

It is said that changes of varṇa according to qualities and actions are accepted: thus a thief became the sage Vālmīki. This too is unsound, because the examples are not comparable. The qualities and actions that bring about birth cannot all be known directly, yet their existence is certain, because they are inferred from their results. The result of birth in a Brāhmaṇa womb and the other wombs likewise permits those causes to be inferred. Therefore, birth in a particular varṇa follows from the qualities and actions that bring about a body of that varṇa, and the arrangement of varṇas follows birth.

If, instead, the arrangement of varṇa is determined by the qualities and actions that arise in a body after birth, then as those qualities and actions change, varṇa too would change every day and every moment, and there would be no order. An ordinary person cannot determine such subtle qualities and actions; the matter would end in uncertainty. The claim that some learned person or committee could determine the four varṇas is foolish, since no assembly of learned people whose judgment is accepted by all can be established.

Do qualities and actions remain uniform throughout one birth? They change from moment to moment. A person of good qualities and actions may, through carelessness, become attached to bad qualities and actions. One engaged in bad qualities and actions may, through the company of good people and personal effort, attain a higher quality of action. Vālmīki, Ajāmila, and others are examples. Just as distinctions are seen in the ages of Kṛta, Tretā, Dvāpara, and Kali, so the gradations of qualities are experienced each day and in each person, like the major, sub-, and sub-sub-periods of the planets. Thus, if varṇa followed differences in qualities and actions, it would change every day.

Everyone would be a Brāhmaṇa when, endowed with sattva in the morning, he worshipped and meditated on the Supreme Lord. When he took a staff to ward off dogs, he would be a Kṣatriya. When he undertook the purchase of salt, agriculture, or the tending of cattle, he would be a Vaiśya. When he set about cleaning and sweeping a house, he would become a Śūdra. Such an arrangement could not properly be called an order.

Moreover, a Brāhmaṇa endowed with tamasic qualities and actions would become a Śūdra and, joining with a Brāhmaṇa woman endowed with sattvic qualities, would beget a Caṇḍāla. Because of her fidelity, a wife cannot restrain even a badly behaved husband. A son endowed with sattvic qualities would regard his father, endowed with tamasic qualities, as a Śūdra and serve him accordingly. Thus, an arrangement of varṇa made to follow qualities and actions would become utter disorder.

Qualities and actions from a previous birth cannot appear in a later birth in their former form, because they perish moment by moment. Their results, once begun, manifest as the unseen and as fate, which cannot be comprehended. “What is beyond thought is fate; it is not destroyed even among beings. Who can dare to fight against fate, O Saumitri, when no other cause for what takes place is seen?” (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa 2.23.20–21.)

Conduct as the measure of virtue and vice

The great sages, free from attachment and partiality, established rules of punishment impartially. They assigned sovereignty and its pleasures to Kṣatriyas, mastery of wealth to Vaiśyas, mastery of various crafts to Śūdras, and a life of renunciation, with the giving up of all possessions, to Brāhmaṇas.

For drinking liquor and similar acts, the ordinary expiation was prescribed for Kṣatriyas and the other varṇas; for Brāhmaṇas, the prescribed penalty was death. For eating prohibited food too, they prescribed progressively heavier penalties for Śūdras, Vaiśyas, Kṣatriyas, and Brāhmaṇas. Punishment was prescribed for the welfare of each, to restrain them from prohibited acts. It too is a form of expiation. Even without an expiation in the form of a vow, one becomes free of sin through the prescribed penalty. Since a king is forbidden to kill a Brāhmaṇa, Brāhmaṇas themselves must perform an expiation ending in death for drinking liquor and similar acts.

Because Śambūka, a Śūdra, performed austerities contrary to his own dharma, a Brāhmaṇa boy died. Knowing that this had resulted from an act that transgressed the rule laid down by the sages, Lord Rāma punished Śambūka. Through the imposition of the proper punishment, the Brāhmaṇa boy came back to life. Those who do not accept that the boy’s death resulted from Śambūka’s austerities also cannot accept that his killing by Lord Rāma restored the boy to life. If the Rāmāyaṇa is authoritative in that account, the same account must be authoritative in this matter too; one cannot accept only half of it. Moreover, the story does not agree with the doctrine that varṇa is determined by action: on that view Śambūka would have been a Brāhmaṇa, not a Śūdra, so why would he have been killed?
