The one-varṇa position

Likewise, the very idea of a single varṇa is a manifestation of ignorance of the purport of the śāstras. The claim is made: “Brahman indeed was this in the beginning, one only; being one, it did not flourish. It created a more excellent form, the Kṣatra … It did not flourish; it created the Viś …” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.11–12). From this statement, it is said, we learn that in the beginning only the Brāhmaṇa existed, and the Kṣatriya and the others did not. Since it was capable of protecting and performing such functions, Brahman created the Kṣatriya, distinguished by strength, prowess, and valour. Hence, from “Brahman is the source of the Kṣatra” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.11), it is understood that the primordial Brāhmaṇa alone was the source of the Kṣatriyas. It then created the Vaiśya for agriculture, trade, and related work, and created the Śūdra varṇa for service. The claim is that in each case the Brāhmaṇa community assigned the Kṣatriyas and the rest according to their aptitude.

That, too, is trivial, because it contradicts the text. What is established here by Brahman being the source of the Kṣatriya womb is only that Brahman produced the Kṣatriyas. It does not establish that certain people among the Brāhmaṇas were designated Kṣatriyas according to their aptitude. There is no authority for such an interpretation, and the verb sṛj means “to create” or “produce.” Earlier, this same writer said that Brāhmaṇas and the others are not varṇas because the word varṇa does not occur alongside their names. Now he says that in the Kṛta age the Brāhmaṇa alone was the varṇa. Yet in the quoted Śruti the designation “Śūdra varṇa” is plainly present.

In truth, priority in the order of creation is natural. That does not make it possible to deny those who come later. Before the creation of wind and the other elements, space alone existed, and from it wind and the rest came into being. This does not prove that wind and the other elements are not distinct elements. In the same way, the Brāhmaṇa existed first, and the Kṣatriyas and the others arose later. That does not make the existence of a single varṇa an established fact, for that very passage states that the varṇas came from Brahman’s mouth and the other parts. Their production is not merely figurative, since that would contradict the statement that Kṣatriyas and the others arose from Brahman just as wind and the rest arose from space. In accordance with the Śruti, “The Rājanya was made from his arms,” the meaning to be understood is that Brahman, in the form of the Brāhmaṇa, had Brāhmaṇas born from his mouth, Kṣatriyas from his arms, Vaiśyas from his thighs, and Śūdras from his feet.

By this same reasoning, the passage “Brahman brought forth the gods; Brahman is this whole universe; from Brahman Kṣatra was fashioned, and Brahman was fashioned from Brahman itself” (Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 2.8.8.6) is also explained. Here, too, Brāhmaṇa-hood belongs to Virāj, the source of Hiraṇyagarbha, and from him the gods are said to have arisen. The interpretation that “the learned are the gods” proves that only learned people are gods is also trivial. If that were so, the statement of Vālmīki, “The learned are Brahmarākṣasas,” would entail that learned people become Brahmarākṣasas. The point here is neither to prescribe that learned people are gods nor that they are Brahmarākṣasas; it is that gods are learned, and Brahmarākṣasas, too, can be learned. “These learned Brahmarākṣasas seek out a flaw” (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa 12.18). Thus what is intended is the arising of the gods, the Brāhmaṇas, and the other varṇas from Brahman, Hiraṇyagarbha. The purport of the Mahābhārata passage has already been explained: the world was formerly of one varṇa, and in later births the same beings acquired Kṣatriya-hood and the like through differences in qualities and actions.

The statement, “Formerly there was one Veda, the syllable Oṃ, comprising all speech; the god was Nārāyaṇa, none other, and fire was of one kind” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva), describes the state before Kṣatriyas and the others arose; it does not deny their later existence. It is likewise appropriate to say that before the Vedas took their developed form and were transmitted through their branches, Oṃ alone was the Veda. Otherwise, statements such as “Speech is eternal” (Ṛg Veda Saṃhitā 8.75.6) and “This speech was known to those before us” (Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 2.12.6.2) would contradict the claim that the speech expressed in mantras is eternal.

The Vāyu Purāṇa says that in the Kṛta age there was no performance of good or evil actions, no arrangement of varṇa and āśrama, and no mixture. All beings were free from desire and aversion, equal in form and lifespan, with no higher or lower. This describes an age of pure sattva before the varied creation. It cannot be projected onto the present, where desire and aversion, differences in appearance and lifespan, and distinctions of higher and lower are plainly seen.

One may say that, from the standpoint of ultimate reality, because everything is a manifestation of Brahman, its identity with Brahman alone is ultimately real. “All this indeed is Brahman; one should worship it with the thought, ‘From it one is born, by it one is sustained, into it one is dissolved’” (Chāndogya Upaniṣad 3.14.1). But this does not cancel the practical order of the world or the distinctions of jāti and the like within ordinary experience. Thus, in the Mahābhārata, the fifth Veda, the story of Mataṅga explicitly explains Brāhmaṇa-hood as birth-based.

Even if varṇas were one in the Kṛta age, one cannot deny their existence in the Tretā and later ages, just as one cannot deny the distinct, scripturally established fires used in the Agnihotra. Dharma also differs according to time and place. An Agnihotra offering prescribed for the morning is not a religious duty at midday. In the same way, a duty prescribed in the Kṛta age need not be a duty in the Tretā or Kali age. A duty prescribed for the stage of brahmacarya is not thereby a duty for the householder; a householder’s duty is not thereby a duty for a renunciant. The arrangement described for the Kṛta age was appropriate then, not for the present age.

The Vāyu Purāṇa further says that those who spoke truly of Brahman were Brāhmaṇas; Kṣatriyas were established to protect the others; the Vaiśya’s work was assigned to those among the others who were weak; and those who were sorrowful, fleeing, devoted to service, and lacking in vigour, form, and strength were called Śūdras (Vāyu Purāṇa, Upodghāta-pāda 8). In accordance with the Śruti and Smṛti already cited, the qualities and actions appropriate to each womb are to be understood as the basis on which beings are born into Brāhmaṇa and other wombs. The Śruti says that those whose conduct is pleasing enter a Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, or Vaiśya womb.

It is also claimed that in the mantra “The Brāhmaṇa was his mouth,” the words for mouth and the other limbs are figurative, indicating qualities that arise from them. The first-case ending seen there is to be understood in the sense of the fifth case. Knowledge, humility, truthfulness, pleasing speech, and instruction are the principal qualities and actions associated with the mouth; from these, the Brāhmaṇa arose. Strength, energy, and valour belong to the arms; from them arose the Kṣatriya, devoted to protecting those in danger. The thighs are the middle part; from their qualities—earning and distributing wealth and grain, and generosity—and from agriculture, trade, cattle-keeping, and similar work, the Vaiśya arose. From the feet come such inferior qualities as dullness, stupidity, and toil, and such work as service and assistance; from these arose the dependent Śūdra, marked by the inferior qualities and work of service. Service and labour are not themselves inferior qualities, but because inferiority is superimposed on them through the association with dullness and stupidity, they are to be understood as inferior too.

This interpretation is baseless. There is no reason to abandon the primary meaning and impose a figurative one. Manu and others explicitly say that the Brāhmaṇas and the others arose from the mouth and the other limbs. Even if one accepts that Brāhmaṇas and the others arose from qualities and actions associated with the mouth and the other limbs, this does not harm the position that varṇa is rooted in birth: the word “arose” still concerns birth. It is accepted that beings are born into Brāhmaṇa and other wombs because of qualities and actions from previous births. If “arose” is instead made to mean “were figuratively assigned,” the primary meaning has again been abandoned without basis, and the explanation becomes needlessly complicated.

Besides, are knowledge, humility, truthfulness, and pleasing speech qualities and actions found only among Brāhmaṇas? They are desired in all varṇas. How has it been established that dullness and stupidity are qualities of the feet? This has not been explained. The claim that service and labour are inferior qualities also calls for examination. If inferiority is merely superimposed on them, why not superimpose strength and valour on the feet as well? The statement in the Bhaviṣya Purāṇa does not contradict our position. The Ṛg Veda says, “The moon was born from the mind.” Since the moon is the mouth of Virāj, the Brāhmaṇas born from Virāj’s mouth are called “born from Soma.” The Purāṇas say that the Sun is the arm of the Lord; therefore the Kṣatriyas, born from the arms, are called “born from the Sun.” The Purāṇas also describe the ocean as the Lord’s belly; therefore the Vaiśyas born from the Lord’s belly are called “born from the ocean.” Skill in crafts and the like is attributed to the feet because Śūdras are entitled to develop skill in crafts and similar work.

It has also been said, relying on a passage in the Bhaviṣya Purāṇa, that no evidence of difference is found in the forms, intellect, qualities, or defects of Brāhmaṇas and the other groups, while evidence opposing such difference is found in their common origin. On this basis, some advocate abolishing jāti, including Brāhmaṇa-hood, in order to establish a society without classes. The text says: “Brāhmaṇas are not white as moonbeams; Kṣatriyas are not the colour of the kiṃśuka flower; Vaiśyas here are not like yellow orpiment; and Śūdras are not black as charcoal” (Bhaviṣya Purāṇa 1.41.41). If Brāhmaṇa-hood depended on being fair and white, only Europeans would be Brāhmaṇas. Some Vedic people in the Madras region, especially young women, have black hair and dark complexions; often only their teeth appear white. A Śūdra may also be fair-complexioned and, by clear pronunciation, may appear just like a Brāhmaṇa. The skin, flesh, fat, and bones do not differ.

Another passage says: “In bodily measure, form, conception, speech, intellect, sense organs, lifespan, strength, the three aims of life, disease, and medicine, there is no distinction made by jāti.” It compares four sons of one father, all of one jāti, and continues: “Since all beings have one father, and are alike through having one father, there is no difference of jāti among them” (Bhaviṣya Purāṇa 1.41.43–45). It also says that fruits of one kind of fig tree, whether growing near the trunk, in the middle, or at the top, are alike in colour, shape, touch, and taste, and so one should conclude that there is one jāti (Bhaviṣya Purāṇa 1.41.46). It lists a further series of supposed absences of difference: in external and internal features, happiness, wealth, knowledge, fearlessness, strength, form, senses, occupation, lifespan, bodily health, weakness, steadiness, agility, wisdom, dispassion, dharma, valour, skill, appearance, medicine, birth, movement, bodily waste, affection, measure, and hair. “Even when carefully examined in all these respects, the difference between Śūdra and Brāhmaṇa is not seen, even by the assembled gods” (Bhaviṣya Purāṇa 1.41).

That, too, is trivial. In the Mahābhārata, the fifth Veda, Kaśyapa tells Garuḍa that a person whose throat burns upon hearing the Veda is to be recognized and protected as a Brāhmaṇa. Therefore, Brāhmaṇa-hood is to be understood as birth from faultless Brāhmaṇa parents. The Mahābhārata’s account of Karṇa and Paraśurāma says that extraordinary endurance of suffering is a mark of a Kṣatriya. It is also said elsewhere that being born from the powers represented by the Lord’s mouth and the other limbs is what makes one a Brāhmaṇa and the other varṇas.

Varṇa differences despite gestational birth

An objection is raised: “All Brāhmaṇas and the others are now born from a womb, not from the mouth and the other limbs. What, then, accounts for their distinction?” The answer is that, at the beginning of creation, scripture teaches that Brāhmaṇas and the others arose from the mouth and the other limbs of Virāj. That account cannot be rejected as unauthoritative. The mantra is not of human authorship, so every suspicion of human error is removed. If a passage in the Bhaviṣya Purāṇa contradicts it, that passage must be interpreted in a way that agrees with the Veda. The Purāṇa is of human authorship and has no authority of its own; its authority is accepted only insofar as it is rooted in the authorless Veda.

Thus, although everyone is now visibly born from a womb, one cannot insist that every act of creation must have occurred in the same way. Otherwise, if all beings had always arisen only through embodied mothers and fathers, accepting the Supreme Lord as their cause would be pointless. The Purāṇas also describe mental creation, and it must be accepted that the first body arose from what had no body. The Supreme Lord’s creation of the Brāhmaṇas and the others from his mouth and the other limbs has already been shown elsewhere. Accordingly, the Brāhmaṇa is formed by the Gāyatrī, the Rājanya by the Triṣṭubh, and the Vaiśya by the Jagatī; no meter is stated to produce the Śūdra. For this reason, the sacraments are prescribed for only the three, not for the Śūdra. The Brāhmaṇa’s initiation is prescribed in spring, the Rājanya’s in summer, and the Vaiśya’s in autumn; initiation is not prescribed for the Śūdra. A distinction is likewise stated for the placing of the sacred fires and the other rites. The priestly office and the eating of offerings such as the puroḍāśa belong to the Brāhmaṇa, not the Kṣatriya or Vaiśya. A corresponding distinction is to be understood in regard to brahmavarchas and the rest.

One may ask, then, how the statements in the Bhaviṣya Purāṇa are to be understood. The answer is that statements which are praise or censure used to enjoin the combination of duties are not intended literally in their own right. For example, the Īśā Vāsya Upaniṣad says: “Those who worship ignorance enter blind darkness; those who delight in knowledge enter, as it were, still greater darkness” (Īśā Vāsya Upaniṣad 9). Here, ritual action and meditation are both prescribed, with action leading to the world of the ancestors and knowledge to the world of the gods. Though each is praised as a means to its result, each is censured in turn in order to urge their combination.

Likewise, the statement, “Who knows whether the other world exists for a person, or whether it does not?” does not intend to establish doubt about the afterlife as its own conclusion. It serves the injunction and is meant to praise what the injunction prescribes. In the same way, the texts do not mean that birth alone, action alone, or sacrament alone causes the varṇa that is the basis for the practice of dharma. Rather, birth, action, and sacrament together are its cause. To establish this particular meaning, the texts make many exaggerated objections and say that birth, sacrament, or the other factors are of no use by themselves. The details are explained elsewhere in this work.

The claim that beings belong to one jāti because they arise from a single source is subject to the same difficulty. Even though everything arises from the Supreme Self, everyone accepts distinctions among animals, birds, human beings, and other jātis. The argument therefore fails. The Brāhmaṇa’s particular excellence in dharma and austerity is well known, and the Gītā and other texts explicitly state that the qualities of calm, self-control, courage, and energy arise according to the nature of the different varṇas. Therefore, the jāti of Brāhmaṇas and the others cannot be denied by the example of fruits from an udumbara tree.

Meaning of jāti

It is claimed that “jāti is characterized by common birth,” and that because men and women produce offspring in the same way, they must therefore belong to one jāti. This is merely the blind imitation of ignorant people who do not understand the śāstras. If that were so, one would have to deny jāti in eternal atoms and selves. How, on that reasoning, could one speak of different jātis among such distinct things as pots and women? In truth, we speak of one jāti where the same conception arises. Thus, “pot” and the like are accepted as jātis because they give rise to the same conception. Even among animals, though the jātis differ, the relation of producing offspring is seen to be the same. Therefore, although the relation of parent and offspring is alike among Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, and the others, their difference of jāti is still accepted.

It is further said that Kabīr, Rāmdās, Vivekānanda, Aurobindo, Govind Singh, and other devotees of the Lord who have conquered their senses, though regarded as Śūdras, are accepted as excellent, venerable, and worthy of worship. This argument is empty. Even if their greatness is present among their followers, those who accept the authority of the Veda and the other śāstras do not accept them as gurus or objects of worship in that way. If one is to be accepted by any means, then because the Buddha is regarded as the most venerable of all, one would have to accept only him. The Buddha’s teaching has not been accepted by the learned in India because it is not accepted by them and contradicts the Veda.

The doctrine of abolishing jāti

The proposal to set aside pride in jāti, family, and the like, and establish sameness of jāti, equal education, and so on, by using Japan as an example, is also trivial. Imitating another people in a way contrary to one’s own dharma, śāstra, and tradition is not proper. There are many countries and peoples in the world, and each has its own respected books, traditions, and dharmas. What is suitable for them need not be suitable for others. Material advancement is achieved through practical efforts, and such efforts are not prohibited here. But abolishing distinctions of jāti and dharma does not bring about that advancement; striving to abolish them for this purpose is foolish. Dogs, too, are without such distinctions of jāti and dharma, yet they do not thereby attain that kind of advancement.

The Badrinath account

It is said that, when reflecting on the Indian nation, the thought arises: “We are the highest; these people are low and are not to be counted. Only we have authority, and no one else.” Such a narrow attitude is growing even toward one’s own Hindu brethren. Those of one varṇa regard not only Harijans and the untouchable as inferior; they generate hatred toward everyone else as well. Drāviḍa and southern Brāhmaṇas call the Gauḍa Sārasvatas and others “Kāśī Śūdras.” Only Nambūdiri Brāhmaṇas worship the image of Badrinātha. Vedic Brāhmaṇas of the Himalayas, though of good conduct, cannot even touch that image. Staff-bearing mendicants, through the false pride of being Brāhmaṇas, see Paramahaṃsa mendicants without staffs and growl like dogs: “These Śūdras are fallen and accursed; give them no alms, and let no learned person teach them.” This mutual contempt among Hindus is the mother of a Christian community numbering more than ten crore.

Where a society gives no respect or standing to human beings, and one sees hatred that opposes love and goodwill, and conduct that humiliates people on account of jāti, what thoughtful person would wish to remain there? In Kerala and elsewhere, as long as the outcast people regard themselves as Hindus, bear names such as Padmanābhadāsa and Janārdanadāsa, and worship the tulasī, the gods, and so forth, they remain untouchable and despised, unfit even to enter their own streets. The same people, when they become Christians, admire foreign languages, dress, and customs, turn away from their own language, dress, and customs, and oppose Hindu dharma and society; then they are immediately honoured by the highest Hindus, addressed by the respectful title “Sahib,” and considered fit not only to enter their streets but even their homes. When they rise to power in denominational, regional, or other administrations, they greatly insult Hindu society. The text says: “When the Śūdra, who is the foot of the social body, is separated from it, why would people of other faiths not trample upon its head?”

That, too, is entirely a reaction. Although some ignorant people may be proud in this way, the learned who are devoted to Vedic dharma, its duties, and its civilization regard everything as Brahman in the ultimate sense, since all is the work of Brahman. Yet, in ordinary life, they also accept the distinctions of jāti and dharma, the duties belonging to each, and the resulting relative precedence and subordination, as established by the śāstras. In practice, not every distinction can be erased. The priestly and teaching duties belong to the Brāhmaṇa, not to the others; eligibility for the Rājasūya belongs to the Kṣatriyas. The scriptural precedence of Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, and Vaiśya in that order is also accepted. The law books establish distinctions among their staffs, sacred girdles, mendicant practices, and the names used for them; this has been explained elsewhere.

Doctors are not to be blamed for treating people differently with medicines and procedures suited to their conditions. A father does not bow to his son as he bows to his own father. In ordinary life, people wash their hands with special care after touching a person overcome by passion or poison, but they are not thereby haters. This sense of self extending to all beings belongs not only to Hindus: for people of noble character, the whole world is one family, and they feel identity with all living beings. First there is identification with Mahāvirāj, whose body is the countless universes; then the sense of non-difference with Hiraṇyagarbha, the total subtle body; then identification with the unmanifest, the great causal state. After that comes the sense of identity with the supreme Self, which is one with the inner consciousness, and the direct realization that accords with it. Before the sense “I am Mahāvirāj” arises, the thought “this is mine” develops. Since the body and its parts are objects of “mine,” they are also objects of “I.” Just as one regards one’s own body as belonging to oneself, the total body of Virāj is likewise regarded as belonging to oneself. Individual identification alone is death; identification with the totality is immortality. This realization unfolds by stages: first the family, then the lineage, then the jāti, then the country, then the earth, then the universe, and then the succession of universes. Nevertheless, actions relating to this world and the next must still be carried out according to the Vedas and other śāstras. To the ignorant, such conduct appears narrow. Even though dogs and jackals are the Self, it is not possible to conduct ordinary eating and drinking with them.

Renunciation and eligibility

In northern India, even Brāhmaṇas are often without the sacraments and without Vedic study; for this reason they are called “Kāśī Śūdras.” It is precisely through reproach that people are moved to undertake Vedic study and the other disciplines. If those born outside lawful unions were honoured everywhere in the same way as those born within a family, how would anyone be moved to avoid such conduct? For that reason, those who perform what is forbidden are censured and those who perform what is enjoined are praised. The purpose of censure and praise is not to establish a final judgement about the people, but to turn them away from what is forbidden and encourage them to do what is enjoined. The special right to touch the image of Badarī Nārāyaṇa is also firmly grounded: since the sponsor is to be served by officiants and priests of his own Vedic branch, the Ācāryas who installed the image of Badarī Nārāyaṇa restricted the right of worship to those of their own branch and jāti. The Kṣatriyas and Vaiśyas have lost their sacraments; hence it is said that they remain only at the beginning and end of the Kali age.

Only Brāhmaṇas are entitled to the life of wandering renunciation. A verse says: “Bearing the mark of Viṣṇu is the dharma of those born from his mouth; this dharma is not praised for those born from the arms or thighs.” According to some, those two groups too may be entitled to renunciation without the marks. But taking up renunciation without ever having received initiation is condemned by every view. Tulasīdāsa also censured this situation: “Those of the lowest varṇas—the oilman, the potter, the dog-cooker, the Kirāta, the Kol, the Kalavāra; a woman whose husband is dead, whose household wealth is lost—shave their heads and become renunciants. They make Brāhmaṇas worship their feet and destroy both worlds with their own hands.”

Some Brāhmaṇas enter those communities through ignorance; some say, with good intent and not from hatred, that by becoming disciples of Śūdras they too fall. They present this as the śāstric principle. Entry into Muslim or Christian communities, however, takes place through ignorance of the greatness of one’s own dharma and through enticements of status. They deceive people by offering livelihoods, giving women in marriage, and similar means. Even learned people among the Brāhmaṇas and other varṇas become involved in this; how much more readily, then, those without the sacraments. Although describing virtues and faults may itself be a fault, one cannot choose what to accept and what to avoid without knowing the virtues and faults. Thus such descriptions are found throughout the Śruti, Smṛti, and Purāṇas. The learned describe faults so that they may be abandoned, and virtues so that they may be accepted. Following the reasoning of the Apaśūdra-adhikaraṇa, learned people regard those of the fourth varṇa as good disciples and, in matters that do not require Vedic authority, as their equals or as qualified in other ways.

“One should not accept instruction from someone inferior.” “I was born in a Śūdra womb; I do not dare say anything else” (Mahābhārata, Āraṇyaka Parva 46.5). In accordance with the Mahābhārata and other texts, those of a higher varṇa are not to receive instruction in dharma from those of an inferior varṇa. Manu also forbids honouring even in words a Brāhmaṇa who engages in prohibited conduct: “One should not honour him even with speech” (Manu 4.30).

Wherever Mlecchas or fallen people are honoured after being appointed to positions of government, the honour is for the office of government, not for those particular persons or their particular jāti. The respect shown to the English and the Mughals arose from their status as rulers. The honour given to a poor Brāhmaṇa who is learned, devoted to dharma, and austere is not because he is a ruler, but because he is religious. In a household, children are taught, disciplined, punished, and rebuked to turn them away from a bad path and set them on a good one; this does not mean that their parents hate them. Even daughters who behave wantonly are rebuked. Those who have taken the path of righteousness unite and strive for the country’s freedom.

It is claimed that Hindus, because of an atmosphere of hatred, false pride, and contempt, became distressed and took refuge in Islam and other religions, thinking they had made their lives blessed; thus hundreds of thousands of Yavanas became known by names such as Bohra and Khoja. It is also said that Brāhmaṇas became Bohras, Lāhāṇas, and Bhāṭiyās, and Kṣatriyas became Khojas. This contradicts the argument itself: it establishes that Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, and others also accept another religion through folly or greed, not only people of supposedly lower jāti who act because they are treated as inferior. If, out of fear of conversion, restraint and discipline are abandoned, everyone will do as they please. If regulation is forbidden, the fear of conversion will spread, and all rules and dharmas will perish. No institution in the world can survive by indulging every member’s whims. For the good of its remaining members and of the institution itself, it expels even eminent people who act arbitrarily and break its rules.

The responsibility for people leaving Hindu dharma, being branded Mlecchas, and destroying Hindu families falls upon reformers such as these, who uproot faith in the śāstras. When faith in the śāstras is strong, even the ignorant, the poor, the Śūdras, and the outcastes preserve Hindu dharma and protect Hindu identity. When faith in the śāstras and dharma is shaken, even Brāhmaṇas, learned people, and the wealthy destroy Hindu dharma by acting as they please and become Mlecchas. People of other faiths describe Hindu dharma in the present age as contrary, narrow, useless, and harmful, and praise the dharmas of Mlecchas. Reformers such as these also condemn Hindu śāstras, Hindu dharma, its teachers, and their followers. What enemies say, these reformers—who are friends in name but enemies in disguise—also say. Ignorant people hear both sides, abandon Hindu dharma, and enter another religion. Therefore, whatever is charged against us falls back upon you.

The Siddhapura account

It is said, with reference to the Siddhapura account, that one division of the Brāhmaṇa community, through attachment and hatred, set aside another division by placing beef in a well and making them drink its impure water, then declared them fallen and Mlecchas. Those who drank water left over by Yavanas, it is said, should not be regarded as Brāhmaṇas or even as Hindus. Having trusted the Hindus and then been insulted and humiliated by them, they supposedly abandoned Hindu identity and Brāhmaṇa-hood, took Yavana gurus, and became Muslims known as Bohras. They still live in that region, wear Yavana clothing, and some call themselves Dvivedī or Trivedī. This whole account is without evidence. Thousands of fabricated histories have been invented to show that Hindus are unworthy.

Even if someone really had unknowingly eaten beef or drunk water left by a Yavana, the dharma texts prescribe expiation. Brāhmaṇas or Kṣatriyas cannot suddenly abandon Hindu identity or Brāhmaṇa-hood without a settled decision by learned people in a matter that is difficult to determine. Therefore, this claim is certainly made by people opposed to Sanātana Dharma. Muslims publicize that they are Dvivedīs, Trivedīs, and so on in order to encourage Hindus to enter the Yavana religion; or it should be understood that they are people who acted through a mistake and did not perform the prescribed expiation.

It is further said that Śūdras and those considered below them, being treated as low and untouchable and wholly cut off from the higher varṇas, became Yavanas through greed or force during the Yavana rule, and that no effort is made to restore them. Because they are cut off, when Yavanas abduct the daughter of someone of a lower varṇa, Hindus ignore it; if they likewise ignore the abduction of the daughters of others, everyone will inevitably face danger. This, too, is unsound and arises from ignorance. No learned person supports such a view. Although Hindus may regard one another as fathers, sons, or close kin and may have degrees of precedence among themselves, when others oppose them they should stand together. As it is said, “When we are insulted by outsiders, we are one hundred and five.” It is mostly those of the higher varṇas who become adherents of other religions because faith in the śāstras is absent, not those who fear dharma, such as Śūdras. Since hostile propaganda has a malicious purpose, the learned allow people to re-enter Hindu dharma after purification and the performance of expiation according to the śāstras. Social groups accept not only those people but also Yavanas and Englishmen generally: they purify them, give them their own daughters in marriage, and bring them into their community. Yet in many cases, after taking a wife, they become Yavanas again.

Sentiments of unity

It is also said that among Sikhs and Muslims everyone says, “I am a Sikh” or “I am a Muslim,” and that this awakens a sense of kinship among them. Among Hindus, by contrast, people say, “I am of such-and-such a varṇa”; different varṇa and sub-varṇa distinctions then arise, and a feeling of kinship does not awaken. People of lower varṇas feel shame because they consider themselves inferior, while those of higher varṇas feel pride because they consider themselves superior. Because this separation is so strong, they think the higher varṇas will not help them in a crisis, whereas Sikhs and Muslims, through a sense of kinship, protect one another. With this weakness in mind, people of lower varṇas flee when enemies attack and accept subordination to them. This, too, is trivial, for it has no proof.

Even among Christians and Muslims there are divisions within their communities. Just as a sense of kinship can arise among them because they share the identity of Christian or Muslim, it can also arise among Hindus who differ as Brāhmaṇa and the like. Every thing shares some feature with another and differs in some other respect. Though particular things may differ as earthly substances or as material substances, they are accepted as alike in being substances. In a single fruit, there may be outer differences and inner unity; likewise, though Hindus differ in varṇa, they are united by their commitment to the same śāstras and dharma and by being members of the same body of Virāṭ. A Brāhmaṇa may have kinship with someone as a Brāhmaṇa; as a Hindu, he has kinship with other Hindus; as an inhabitant of India, he is connected with all Indians; and as a human being, he can have a sense of kinship and goodwill toward every living being.

Reasoning under the authority of scripture

A further claim is made: “Instruction in dharma should be given in accordance with place, time, age, condition, understanding, and ability, just as medicine is given to the sick. One who investigates the teaching of dharma through reasoning, without contradicting the Veda and the śāstras, knows dharma; another does not” (Manu 1.106). The Nirukta also says: “People asked, ‘Who will become a seer?’ To them the seer gave this reasoning, an inquiry into the meaning of the mantra. Therefore, whatever a reciter investigates through such reasoning is established.”

The claim that a decision should rest on scripture alone, without reasoning, is also rejected: “Where there is inquiry without reasoning, dharma is lost.” The objection that Brāhmaṇas and Hindus do not consider these statements or their context, and do not examine what comes before and after them, is itself an unrestrained rant. It does not consider that envy, hatred, contempt, and separation bring about social degradation; as a result, Mlecchas from abroad perform a naked dance in sacred Bhārata.

This charge is baseless. It arises from ignorance of the works of Vyāsa, Jaimini, Bhaṭṭapāda, Śaṅkara, Maṇḍana, Vācaspati, and others. The Vedas, Smṛtis, Purāṇas, histories, works of the sages, explanations in the Bhāṣyas and Vārttikas, and treatises such as the Mitākṣarā, Vyavahāra-Mayūkha, Hemādri, Vīramitrodaya, and Parāśara-Mādhava are well known as authorities for determining dharma and adharma. Reasoning is abundant in these works and is not hidden from those who understand them. The cited statement honours reasoning that does not contradict the Veda and śāstras, not reasoning that does contradict them. The Nirukta, too, honours the reasoning of Vedic reciters, who are learned in the Vedas and śāstras.

In the Nirukta, the word tarka means the reflective inquiry into the meaning of a mantra, as the text itself says: “An examination of the mantra’s meaning” (Nirukta 13.12). Such inquiry is not independent. A mantra must be explained with reference both to Śruti and to reasoning, not through reasoning alone, separately from Śruti. “There is no direct perception of these [mantras] by those who have not practised austerity” (Nirukta 13.12); “one should seek this as one’s life, not leave it unexplained” (Nirukta 13.13). Even learned Vedic scholars must interpret repeatedly by relying on the Veda, not by the force of reasoning alone. An effort of that sort is a cause of cutting one’s life short.

The Kashmir account

It is said that the Brāhmaṇas living in Kashmir were made Mlecchas by the Yavana emperor at Delhi. The Kṣatriya king of Kashmir asked learned men to purify them, saying, “These Mlecchas were formerly Āryas; purify them and admit them among the Āryas.” The learned replied, “There is no scriptural authority for purifying Mlecchas. Therefore we cannot do anything contrary to the śāstras.” It is claimed that all India now suffers the result of that refusal. This, too, is a delusion, already answered by what has been said. Their Hindu identity could have been restored by establishing a separate group, since Hindu identity is brought about by faith in Hindu dharma according to the Vedas and other śāstras. The Bhāgavata and other texts describe the common dharma accessible to all human beings, including devotion, knowledge, and the other thirty characteristics. Such a false account has been publicized by deceitful people.

The claim that “making the whole world Ārya” means turning non-Āryas into the Ārya jāti is the talk of ignorant people. Here the word Ārya means only “excellent”; it does not denote a jāti. What is intended is excellence: “Make the whole world excellent, endowed with the qualities of the excellent.”

The verse “Increasing Indra, O apturaḥ, making the whole world Ārya, driving away the arāvaṇaḥ” (Ṛg Veda Saṃhitā 6.63.5) has been explained as follows: “May you increase Indra, the Supreme Lord, and spread everywhere through praises the noble, Vedic, eternal dharma taught by him. Performing pure and praiseworthy good deeds, striving to acquire the powers of knowledge, strength, wealth, and the like, make the whole world Ārya, endowed with excellence; drive away and crush the wicked, non-Āryas who follow other teachings.” That explanation is not sound, because it contradicts grammar and the rules of word-meaning. Although the word Indra can refer to the Supreme Lord on the basis of its etymology, established usage prevails over a newly constructed etymology; here Indra means Indra of the hundred sacrifices. In “Indra moves in many forms through his powers,” the word may mean the Supreme Lord because of the Vedānta context. Even if it meant the Lord here, there is no basis for making Indra stand figuratively for the Vedic dharma taught by him, and the word Indra certainly does not mean the spread of dharma. Nor is there authority for taking apturaḥ to mean “performers of good deeds” or arāvaṇaḥ to mean “non-Āryas”; the word arāti means an enemy.

According to Sāyaṇa’s commentary, the third hymn of thirty verses beginning “Ā pavasva” is a Gāyatrī hymn of Kāśyapa Nidruvi, whose deity is the purifying Soma. The deity of a mantra is the subject it expresses; therefore this hymn praises Pavamāna Soma. Sāyaṇa accordingly explains the line as “increasing Indra,” with apturaḥ meaning “directing the waters,” and “making the world”—that is, Soma—favourable and auspicious for our ritual work. The following phrase is connected with “approach the non-givers.” No agent needs to be supplied here. The words “the tawny Soma drops, pressed and flowing, approach Indra” occur in the next mantra (Ṛg Veda Saṃhitā 6.63.6). The word indavaḥ refers to the gods presiding over Soma, and “the world” refers to the substance that is Soma. Thus there is no conflict between the grammatical subject and object.

Actions are the cause of birth, and birth establishes jāti. The Mahābhārata says: “By action, one is born again and again in a particular jāti; one remains as long as the fruit of that action lasts, and when the action is exhausted, one goes on again” (Anuśāsana Parva). Only earlier actions whose fruits are ready to ripen, called prārabdha, cause birth. Actions performed by the present body cannot determine its jāti before that body falls.

Moreover, if varṇa were determined by action in the present life, one could not distinguish one’s own duties from another’s duties. Then statements of the Gītā such as “one who is devoted to his own work” (18.45) and “one’s own work, though defective” (18.48) would make no sense. The texts say that, outside an emergency, abandoning one’s own dharma and performing another’s leads to a fall. Manu says: “Better one’s own dharma, though imperfectly performed, than another’s, even if well performed. Living by another’s dharma, one immediately falls from one’s jāti” (Manu 10.97). Therefore varṇa is not determined by livelihood and similar factors; it is determined by birth.
