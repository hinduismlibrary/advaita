Livelihood-based varṇa

Is a system of varṇa based on livelihood meant only for men, or for women as well? If for women too, are livelihoods prescribed for them as well—livelihood through sacred learning, through weapons, through trade, and through service? Women are not seen engaged in these ways, nor is such an arrangement possible. If so, how could a varṇa order corresponding to their actions be established? Or, if they are without varṇa, statements such as “For twice-born men, marriage with a woman of the same varṇa is commended” (Manu 3.12) would become inapplicable. Rules such as the following likewise make sense only when a woman’s varṇa is accepted by birth, not by her occupation: “A bow is prescribed for a Kṣatriya woman, a goad for a Vaiśya maiden, and a measure of cloth for a Śūdra woman” (Manu 3.44; the printed OCR gives a different verse number).

In the same way, the rules prescribing unions in the regular and reverse orders are coherent only if varṇa is acknowledged by birth. Statements that speak of mixed descent do not authorize an occupation-based varṇa; rather, they assign each group a particular livelihood. If varṇa were determined by work, everyone born to everyone would have to be a Śūdra, since everyone is born through labor. But this is not observed, and Brahmins do not become laborers merely by birth.

Moreover, Manu says: “A twice-born man who, without studying the Veda, takes up other work quickly attains Śūdrahood, together with his descendants” (Manu 2.168). On this account, someone who lives by the śāstras or by trade, but does not study the Veda, would make himself and his children Śūdras by earning their livelihood through service. How, then, could the rites of naming and initiation, the prescribed materials and measurements for the girdle, sacred thread and staff, the manner of begging, conduct, rinsing and other observances—all of which follow distinctions of varṇa—be accounted for? If varṇa is determined by qualities and actions, how could the arrangements for conception rites and the other life-cycle rites be made? If varṇa were assigned by inference from qualities and work at the time of the coming-of-age rite, what would be the purpose of prescribing varṇa-specific rites for Śūdras?

The claim that Śūdra status is assigned only to someone unfit for training in weapons, the śāstras and other disciplines is also untenable. Without eligibility for initiation, a person does not begin that study; one cannot first infer his lack of fitness from his not having studied. If the statement “one is born a Śūdra” establishes Śūdrahood by birth, the doctrine that varṇa is established by action is contradicted. And if Śūdra status is accepted as determined by birth, why should the other varṇas not likewise be determined by birth? If livelihood alone established varṇa, those who live by astrology, bardic recitation, begging, service, and other occupations outside the named livelihoods would have to be without varṇa.

Further, if the word varṇa is taken to mean only livelihood, then “varṇa by action” would mean “work by work.” The expression “determining varṇa by livelihood and action” would mean determining livelihood by livelihood, which is pointless and incoherent. Lexicons, by contrast, give varṇa the meanings “twice-born and the rest,” “Brāhmaṇa and the other classes,” and also “colour” and “form.” The dictionaries therefore establish that the word also denotes the groups beginning with Brāhmaṇa.

Pāṇini’s rule “brāhma when there is no jāti” (Aṣṭādhyāyī 6.4.171) gives brāhma where no jāti is meant, whereas when a jāti is meant in the sense of a descendant, the form brāhmaṇa is established. The texts prescribe the expiation for killing a Brāhmaṇa even when the child in a Brāhmaṇī’s womb has not yet been born: “Having killed an embryo whose identity was not known, he should perform this very observance” (Manu 11.87). Yājñavalkya and Āpastamba likewise speak of an unborn child in a Brāhmaṇī’s womb as a Brāhmaṇa. A child still in the womb cannot have its varṇa determined from its actions. The possibility that a Brāhmaṇī has conceived is enough for the embryo to be treated as Brāhmaṇa; hence she is called Ātreyī, and killing her is assigned the expiation for killing a Brāhmaṇa. The Bhaviṣya Purāṇa likewise prescribes expiation for killing a Brāhmaṇa even when he has no qualities, and for killing a person who is a Brāhmaṇa by birth alone. All this coheres when varṇa is established by birth.

Nor is a change of varṇa proved by examples such as Viśvāmitra. An example by itself does not establish a general rule; otherwise, if the conduct of one person of poor character were acceptable, the same would have to be accepted for all people of poor conduct. In the case of powerful seers, changes of varṇa brought about by curses or boons are exceptions, not general rules. No example in the Purāṇas or epics shows varṇa changing through occupation or livelihood. Though Kṛṣṇa and Yudhiṣṭhira taught spiritual knowledge, they remained Kṣatriyas; though Paraśurāma and others fought as warriors, they remained Brāhmaṇas. The Mahābhārata’s Anuśāsana Parvan also teaches that varṇa is established by birth.

It says that after the fruit of merit is exhausted, a person is born in the world of Bhārata in a family of twice-born people who have mastered the Veda and its auxiliaries. Another passage says that the four groups—Brāhmaṇas and the rest—exist naturally; attaining another varṇa is difficult, and comes about in a later birth through a distinctive course of action. It then describes the fruit of those actions: a Brāhmaṇa should carry out the duties enjoined by the Veda, uphold truth, give gifts and perform sacrifice, and not abandon the duties of his jāti. Living in this way, he reaches heaven and enjoys its pleasures; when that merit is exhausted, he is born again among human beings as a Brāhmaṇa. Thus, through true action, he again attains his own jāti.

These passages establish that varṇa is connected both with action and with birth: actions bear fruit in a subsequent birth. One who abandons the duties of his jāti and takes up Śūdra work is punished in Yama’s realm and is born as a Śūdra in another life. The text then describes the opposite case: a person born from a Brāhmaṇa womb who, through the darkness of ignorance, dishonours that birth and lives otherwise. He should give up the livelihoods and acts that belong to a different varṇa, but instead lives like a Śūdra, seeks Śūdra occupations, associates continually with Śūdras, and neglects purity and auspicious conduct. He too is punished in Yama’s realm, and, when born among human beings, is born as a Śūdra.

If, however, a Śūdra is devoted to truth, restraint, gifts and reverence for Brāhmaṇas, then after death he enjoys the happiness of heaven and is born in a Brāhmaṇa family. Another passage says that a Śūdra who gives up Śūdra occupations, is devoted to truth and charity, honours the mantras and Brāhmaṇas, and is kindly disposed toward Brāhmaṇas may, after death, be born in heaven and later in a distinguished Brāhmaṇa family where the Veda is studied. In the same way, Kṣatriyas and Vaiśyas are born into higher or lower wombs according to their own actions. Therefore, having attained a distinguished jāti, one should carefully preserve it.

The text continues: “Through their own actions they are born in distinguished or inferior wombs. Thus reversal of jāti takes place in a later existence, in another birth; otherwise, the order of the world could not be maintained. Therefore, even a learned person who has attained a distinguished jāti with difficulty should always preserve it, so that it is not lost.” Āpastamba says the same: “By practising dharma, a person of the lowest varṇa attains the preceding, higher varṇa, with a change of jāti. By practising adharma, a person of a higher varṇa attains the lower one, with a change of jāti” (Āpastamba Sūtra 10.6.10–11).

In the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, Sumati, who remembers his former births, says that he was born in the wombs of Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, Vaiśyas and Śūdras, and also in the homes of innumerable animals, deer and birds (10.21). The passage therefore treats the wombs of Brāhmaṇas and the other groups as distinct birth-classes just as it treats animal births: as an animal born in a deer’s womb becomes a deer, so those born in Brāhmaṇa and other wombs become Brāhmaṇas and the rest. It also says that, through merit and sin, beings rise and fall among wombs of higher and lower standing.

It says: “A man is born in other sinful and painful wombs, among Caṇḍālas, Pulkasas and the like. With the remainder of his sin, together with merit, he then attains an ascending birth, such as Śūdra, Vaiśya, Kṣatriya, Brāhmaṇa, or even the status of a god; afterward he may descend again. In this way, those who perform sinful actions fall down into hell” (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa 10.87–90). The same Sumati says, “I was born in the household of a Vaiśya in my seventh birth” (13.1). This shows that, like animal and bird births, Brāhmaṇa and other births are distinct jāti-groups, and that they arise by birth.

Bhīṣma also states the fault in instructing a person lacking the relevant jāti: “Instruction should not be given to anyone who lacks the jāti; when instruction is given, a great fault is attributed to the teacher” (Mahābhārata, Anuśāsana Parvan 104). As an example, the text tells of a Śūdra who approaches the Kuru king and says: “I am low in varṇa, lord; by birth I am a Śūdra, best of men. I wish to render service. I have come to you; be gracious to me” (Mahābhārata, Anuśāsana Parvan 10.15). Such statements speak of Śūdra status as arising from birth. The doubt “Are we not Brāhmaṇas? Are our fathers not Brāhmaṇas?” would make no sense if Brāhmaṇahood were determined by action, since actions could be examined directly. It does make sense if status is by birth, because doubt can arise about one’s mother’s lineage. Brāhmaṇahood cannot be determined by teaching alone, since the texts also prescribe study under a non-Brāhmaṇa in certain circumstances.

Descent, birth, and jāti

Manu allows study from a non-Brāhmaṇa in a time of emergency (Manu 2.241). That exception does not mean that a person’s varṇa is determined by study: instruction in an emergency is a special allowance. The text next says: “The husband, entering his wife, becomes an embryo and is born here again; for this is what makes her a wife—that he is born again in her” (the scanned text cites Manu 6.8). It continues: “He is formed from limb to limb and is born from the heart. Thus the father himself is born again in the form of a son. Therefore the son belongs to the father’s jāti. In the world too, the son is seen to belong to the same seed. A mango does not grow from a neem seed.” In procreation, the woman is described as the field.

When the field is fertile, favourable, and properly prepared, the seed placed in it according to the proper season produces its own kind. Therefore, protection of women is prescribed to prevent admixture, and the rules about intercourse during the proper season are observed. The author claims that where the birth-based varṇa arrangement has been lost in a country or family, there is correspondingly little regard for protecting a wife’s fidelity; he says that in India, even among Brāhmaṇa families, adultery is prevalent. Manu, by contrast, says: “Therefore, for the purity of offspring, a woman should be carefully protected” (6.6), and: “By carefully guarding his wife, a man guards his offspring, character, family, himself, and his own dharma” (6.7). The author says Manu thus strongly commended the protection of women.

Today, he continues, widow remarriage, divorce, remarriage, and marriage across jāti boundaries are being promoted in India too in the name of social progress. The Mahābhārata says: “Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, and Vaiśyas are the three twice-born varṇas” (Anuśāsana Parvan, cited in the scanned text). It names Brāhmaṇas and the others as varṇas, not livelihoods. If varṇa were determined by work alone, a Śūdra’s son could become a Brāhmaṇa through his actions. Then the statement “They regard the son of a Śūdra as no Brāhmaṇa, because of his lack of competence” would be incoherent. Nor would it say: “One born from a Brāhmaṇa man and a Brāhmaṇī is certainly a Brāhmaṇa; there is no doubt.” Thus varṇa is plainly established by birth, not by work. Just as the jāti of animals is determined by birth, so too are the birth-classes beginning with Brāhmaṇa.

The text then gives a passage about the jīva’s successive births: “The jīva, joined to its covering, enters semen, desiring embodiment; encountering the woman’s menstrual flower, it obtains [a body] at the time of birth. Having undergone great suffering, it is born in a womb. Hear from me how, through its actions, it is born in one womb or another, while deluded.” The sequence says that a being is born in an ass’s womb and becomes a Brahmarākṣasa, then is born as a Brāhmaṇa; born in a worm’s womb, it becomes an ass and is later born as a Brāhmaṇa; after death it is born in a divine womb, then as a monkey. It continues through births as a fish, deer, goat, mouse, boar, crow, rooster, human, tortoise, pigeon, parrot, swan, and hare. The source’s OCR is damaged in parts of this list, but its point is that the text describes Brāhmaṇa and other births alongside animal births as distinct wombs attained through past action.

Meanings of the word jāti

Some people use jāti for a group based on birth in a particular region or lineage—for example, the Kurus, Yādavas, Rāghavas, Sārasvatas, Bengalis, Gauḍas, Germans, Chinese, and Greeks. The use of jāti for groups associated with a country, family, or people does not establish that jāti means varṇa in every passage. The word has other meanings too, including birth and class. The texts also describe the distinction between one birth and two births: “The first birth is from the mother; the second is at the tying of the girdle” (Manu 2.166). Of that second, Brahmanical birth, the mother is called Sāvitrī and the father is called the teacher (Manu 2.170).

Manu says: “The jāti which the teacher, learned in the Veda, properly produces for him through the Sāvitrī [mantra] is true and imperishable” (2.148). Here, the word jāti within dvijāti means birth. Yājñavalkya says that Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, and Vaiśyas are twice-born because they receive initiation; since a Śūdra has no initiation, he is of one birth. Manu says: “The fourth, the Śūdra, is of one birth” (10.4). The word jāti means varṇa in statements such as “Those of the same varṇa are born as members of the same jāti,” and in rules prescribing penalties for same-varṇa, upward, and downward unions, and in statements about people born from different jātis.

In other passages, jāti refers to groups formed through intermixture. Manu lists the Pauṇḍrakas, Coḍas, Draviḍas, Kāmbojas, Yavanas, Śakas, Pāradas, Pahlavas, Cīnas, Kirātas, Daradas, and Khaṣas, and says that peoples outside the recognized groups, whose speech is not Ārya speech, are called Dasyus (Manu 10.45–46). Such passages use jāti for peoples distinguished by region or lineage; that usage does not settle the meaning of the word everywhere.

Otherwise, there would be no separate reference to the dharma of a jāti alongside family customs, regional customs, and guild customs. Manu says: “A person who knows dharma should examine the duties of jātis, regions, and guilds, and then declare the proper duty, as with the duties of families” (8.41). He also says: “Whatever is practised by good and righteous twice-born people should be established for regions, families, and jātis, provided it is not contrary to dharma” (8.46). These passages distinguish the dharma of a jāti from the duties of a region or family. Other passages use jāti for varṇas beginning with Brāhmaṇa and Kṣatriya.

Varṇa by action and by birth

If varṇa were determined by action, Arjuna’s qualities of tranquillity, self-control, and detachment would make not only Brāhmaṇa-hood but renunciation his own duty. By the same reasoning, Drona, Kṛpa, and others, because of their courage and related qualities, ought to be Kṣatriyas. Yet Arjuna is said to be a Kṣatriya, and unfit for the mendicant’s life, on the basis of the doctrine that varṇa follows birth: “Better to die in one’s own dharma,” and “a person attains perfection by being devoted to his own work” (Bhagavad Gītā 18.45). Drona and the others are censured for practising the warrior’s duty in opposition to their own duty.

It is also argued that, since all human beings live on earth, varṇa should be arranged by livelihood, or else human dharma would not apply to everyone. This does not follow. If “human dharma” means common duties such as non-injury and truthfulness, those can indeed apply to all human beings. If it means the duties of varṇa and āśrama, then those are not duties prescribed for every person alike. Manu’s statement, “Let each learn his own conduct,” concerns learning the conduct proper to each person; it does not mean that the same varṇāśrama duties are prescribed to everyone.

For that reason, Manu prescribes livelihoods in the tenth chapter for the Pauṇḍrakas, Coḍas, Draviḍas, Kāmbojas, Yavanas, Śakas, Pāradas, Pahlavas, Kirātas, Daradas, Khaṣas, and groups born from intermixture, including work such as fishing, hunting, leatherwork, making musical instruments, and stonework. For Brāhmaṇas and the other varṇas, he gives their duties separately. Manu says: “Having examined all this completely with the eye of knowledge, a learned person should remain devoted to his own dharma, on the authority of Śruti” (2.8). The author objects to misleading people who do not know the texts with false inducements, initiating them into another’s dharma merely to increase one’s own following. The lawgivers instead support steadfastness in one’s own duty. The Gītā says: “A person attains perfection by being devoted to his own work” (18.45).

Birth and prescribed duties

Under the view “varṇa is by birth,” birth first gives a person the eligibility belonging to a varṇa, and action follows that eligibility. Under the view “varṇa is by action,” action would come first and the varṇa that confers eligibility only afterward. The texts, however, prescribe particular actions with reference to Brāhmaṇas and the other groups already established by birth. Only those born into the corresponding varṇa are eligible for its specific rites and duties; doing those acts does not itself create Brāhmaṇa-hood or another varṇa. The texts call this “the dharma of varṇa and āśrama” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parvan 208). The basis for the action must therefore already exist by birth. Duties are prescribed for daughters and others on the basis of their existing relation; actions do not make someone a daughter. Otherwise, whoever performed a wife’s work would become a wife, and there would be no stable arrangement. Serving one’s parents is a son’s duty, but service does not make a person a son; inheritance in ordinary dealings depends on that relationship.

Manu likewise says that the three varṇas should study while remaining established in their own duties, and that among them the Brāhmaṇa is to teach: “The three varṇas should study, each established in his own work; the Brāhmaṇa should instruct them, and not the other two—this is certain” (Manu 10.1). This too establishes varṇa by birth. It is well known that conduct contrary to one’s own dharma brings a harmful result. A Brāhmaṇa by birth ought to strive to cultivate the qualities and actions appropriate to that birth. Even if these are absent in a particular case, his Brāhmaṇa status by birth remains; this is called Brāhmaṇa-hood by jāti.

Āpastamba says that the four varṇas are Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, and Śūdra, and that each earlier one is superior to the next by birth. The text then distinguishes degrees of excellence: a person born a Śūdra who has the qualities and conduct proper to that birth is better than one who lacks them; a Vaiśya by birth is higher still, then a Kṣatriya by birth, and above these a Brāhmaṇa by birth. It also distinguishes the principal Brāhmaṇa from further grades such as one who has merely been born a Brāhmaṇa, a Śrotriya, and an Anūcāna. The Vyāsa Smṛti describes further grades between a Brāhmaṇa-samā and a Brāhmaṇa-bandhu.

It says: “One born from the seed of a Brāhmaṇa, but without the mantras and rites of consecration, who lives only on his birth-status, is a Brāhmaṇa-samā. One who does not perform the rites beginning with conception with their mantras, does not teach, and does not study, is called a Brāhmaṇa-bandhu” (Vyāsa Smṛti 4.41–45). Atri’s text lists ten kinds associated with Brāhmaṇas, including divine, sage, royal, Vaiśya, Śūdra, Niṣāda, animal, Mleccha, and Caṇḍāla forms. The Mahābhārata’s Śānti Parvan (chapter 76) lists six: Brāhmaṇa-samā, deva-samā, Kṣatra-samā, Vaiśya-samā, Śūdra-samā, and Brāhmaṇa-Caṇḍāla.

Grades of Brāhmaṇa status

If being a Brāhmaṇa were produced by action alone, these distinctions would make no sense. They do make sense when Brāhmaṇa-hood is established by birth and qualities and actions determine a person’s degree of excellence or decline. The text explicitly calls a Brāhmaṇa who lacks conduct appropriate to his birth a “Śūdra-samā Brāhmaṇa.” Vyāsa’s statement is repeated: “One born from Brāhmaṇa seed, without mantra-consecration, living only by birth, is a Brāhmaṇa-samā.” Manu says: “Among human beings, Brāhmaṇas are held foremost; among Brāhmaṇas, the learned are foremost” (1.96–97); “The very birth of a Brāhmaṇa is the eternal embodiment of dharma” (1.68); and “A Brāhmaṇa, at the moment of birth, is born as the foremost on earth” (1.69). He also says: “As a wooden elephant and a leather deer are only called by those names, so too a Brāhmaṇa who does not study bears the name only” (2.157). Atri Smṛti says: “A Brāhmaṇa is to be known by birth.” Thus Brāhmaṇas and the other varṇas exist by birth; distinctions made by qualities are secondary.

The rites prescribed for the principal Brāhmaṇas by birth are not extended to those who are Brāhmaṇas only in a secondary sense. The Śruti and Manu use the expressions “Brāhmaṇa-womb,” “Kṣatriya-womb,” “Vaiśya-womb,” and “Śūdra-womb,” which make the birth-basis explicit. The Chāndogya Upaniṣad says “a Brāhmaṇa womb” (5.10.8), and Manu speaks of people born from Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, and Śūdra wombs (8.162). In the rules concerning śrāddha, certain Brāhmaṇas who follow occupations outside the prescribed conduct are declared unfit to be invited. If varṇa were established by action and qualities, their lack of Brāhmaṇa status would already make them ineligible; there would be no need to state this separate exclusion. The source lists those who do not study, sell meat, trade, serve as hired teachers, performers, oil sellers, makers of weapons, animal trainers, and people who follow other disapproved livelihoods. These exclusions make sense, the author argues, because they remain Brāhmaṇas by birth while their conduct makes them unfit for the rite.

Human welfare and the four aims

The verses quoted above describe fierce, tiger-like, vulture-like, aquatic, and other dispositions among Brāhmaṇas. The author says they show that the śāstras do not establish varṇa by qualities and actions alone. Human life, he argues, is not directed only to wealth; it is directed to dharma, artha, kāma, and mokṣa—the four human aims. The purpose of varṇa is therefore not merely to divide livelihoods or reduce economic conflict. Rather, the dharma texts aim to make the practice of dharma easier and its results attainable.

Manu says: “Living in these duties properly, a person reaches the world of the immortals and enjoys all the desires he has formed” (2.5). He also says: “A human being who follows the dharma taught by Śruti and Smṛti wins fame here and, after death, unsurpassed happiness” (2.9). The Bhāgavata says that a Brāhmaṇa’s body is not intended for the pleasures of Śūdra life, but for arduous discipline here and endless happiness after death (11.17.42). The Mahābhārata says that through giving, hospitality, and religious acts, Vaiśyas also attain a good destination (Vana Parvan 150.51). Manu states even for a Śūdra: “The more a person, free of resentment, practises good conduct, the more he obtains a favourable, blameless state in this world” (10.12). Yājñavalkya says that arthaśāstra is subordinate to dharmaśāstra (2.21). The division of livelihood, then, is not itself the ultimate purpose of varṇa; the author presents it as a means that supports religious duties and their results.

Livelihood, aptitude, and order

Even though today people freely take up every kind of work, the author says, social order and the absence of conflict can still be seen where people follow the livelihoods received through family tradition. Like a bird’s capacity for flight and a fish’s capacity for swimming, success in a livelihood follows both the dispositions formed in earlier lives and birth into a family where those abilities are cultivated. The Gītā says that one who has fallen from yoga is born in the home of the pure and prosperous after living for many years in heaven, or in the family of wise yogins; there that person recovers the understanding acquired in a former body and strives further for perfection (6.41–45).

Under a system in which varṇa is determined by work, no one would have a settled responsibility for any particular work. Without the prospect of gain, people would not take up a task. Under birth-based assignment, a person naturally develops an inclination toward the work received through family tradition; neglect of it is censured. Practice in work associated with one’s birth brings skill. If everyone could choose any work according to personal preference, there would be disorder, since people’s preferences are innumerable. Convenience arises from the skill developed in the work inherited through one’s family.

Some people may neglect their duties even when eligibility is based on birth; the remedy is vigilance. The same failure would occur under a work-based system. If a committee were created to decide varṇa, its members would be subject to accusations of bias in their decisions. Who could guarantee that there would be no favouritism or injustice? Influential people seeking profitable occupations could use their power to obstruct impartial decisions. A work-based system would not remove rajas and tamas altogether, nor would it eliminate desire, anger, and greed from even the greatest people. Their influence can be seen in the minds of Yudhiṣṭhira, Parīkṣit, and others. Why would these qualities be absent elsewhere? The author concludes that qualities should be cultivated and incidental defects removed within the stable birth-based order received through beginningless tradition.

The Yudhiṣṭhira dialogue

The argument that the dialogue between a yakṣa and Yudhiṣṭhira establishes varṇa by action is also rejected. That dialogue does not mention livelihood as the basis of varṇa. It asks what makes a Brāhmaṇa; it does not discuss Kṣatriyas, Vaiśyas, and the others. The actions it names are not means of livelihood. The question is: “Who is a Brāhmaṇa, O king?” The answer says: “Truth, giving, forgiveness, good conduct, harmlessness, austerity, and compassion—where these are seen, lord of men, that person is remembered as a Brāhmaṇa” (Mahābhārata, Vana Parvan 180.21). None of these is a livelihood. These qualities may be present in Brāhmaṇas who live by teaching or by spiritual practice, but they may also be present in Kṣatriyas who live by ruling and Vaiśyas who live by trade. If so, would all of them become Brāhmaṇas? That would destroy the fourfold arrangement.

Or the text says: “Wherever all these marks are seen, that person is remembered as a Brāhmaṇa; where they are absent, that person is called a Śūdra” (Mahābhārata, Vana Parvan 180.26). Taken in this way, it would establish only two groups, Brāhmaṇa and Śūdra. The dialogue does not concern the arrangement of the four varṇas; it describes the spiritual, sattvic character useful for knowledge of Brahman—the highest, ultimate Brāhmaṇa-hood. The Śānti Parvan says that when the mind is purified by actions and established in knowledge, it rests in the highest state; it lists generosity, forgiveness, tranquillity, non-injury, truth, freedom from malice, humility, patience, and self-restraint as the path to Brahman (Mahābhārata 270.38–40).

In the yakṣa–Yudhiṣṭhira dialogue, when asked whether family conduct, Vedic study, or observance of vows establishes Brāhmaṇa-hood, the answer is that these alone do not establish twice-born status; conduct is the cause. Therefore one should strive to preserve conduct, especially a Brāhmaṇa’s conduct. The point is the preservation of an already established Brāhmaṇa status, not the creation of varṇa by livelihood. The text says: “Conduct should be carefully protected, especially by a Brāhmaṇa” (Mahābhārata, Vana Parvan 613.106), and: “Even one who knows all four Vedas, if of bad conduct, is inferior to a Śūdra” (Bhāgavata 3.13.11). The Brāhmaṇa status is already present by birth; the person must make an especially strong effort to preserve the conduct appropriate to it. Even the concluding description—“one devoted to the agnihotra, self-controlled, is remembered as a Brāhmaṇa”—does not make agnihotra or self-control a livelihood.

Action and varṇa across births

Statements that appear to say “varṇa is by action” can be harmonized with the Śruti and Smṛti statements establishing varṇa by birth. In the Bhṛgu–Bharadvāja dialogue, it is said: “There is no difference among the varṇas; this whole world is Brāhmaṇa. By actions it became divided into varṇas. They became twice-born, Kṣatriyas, and Śūdras” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parvan 188.10). The author says this means that through those actions people attain Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, or other births in a later life. The passage does not say that twice-born people become Kṣatriyas in that same birth; the words “in another birth” must be supplied from the context.

Manu likewise says that a twice-born person who transgresses the prescribed rule may, after death, obtain a lower birth (3.190); this is not a change within the same life, which would contradict what is observed. The same should be understood here. The next cited verse says: “If this mark is found in a twice-born person, it is not found in a Śūdra; a good person does not become a Śūdra, nor does a Brāhmaṇa cease to be a Brāhmaṇa” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parvan 186.8). The author uses it to distinguish the primary birth-status from the secondary assessment of conduct and qualities.

Birth-status and conduct

The point of the cited dialogue is to teach the importance of conduct; it does not deny varṇa by birth. Thus the statement “a Śūdra is not a Śūdra, and a Brāhmaṇa is not a Brāhmaṇa” means that a person born a Śūdra who lacks the conduct proper to that status is not even a proper Śūdra, while a person born a Brāhmaṇa who lacks the qualities and actions proper to that birth is not a true Brāhmaṇa in conduct. A lion without courage is not truly lion-like; a person called Devadatta who displays a lion’s courage is spoken of as a lion. In the same way, a person not born a Brāhmaṇa may be called a Brāhmaṇa when endowed with the Brāhmaṇa qualities of tranquillity and the rest. It is therefore a mistake to treat the Bhṛgu–Bharadvāja dialogue as teaching that varṇa is established by work.

Another text says that Brāhmaṇa status depends on Brāhmaṇa seed, the womb, the rites of consecration, and the like; by Brāhmaṇa conduct and learning, a person becomes excellent, while in their absence he is inferior. Even in the ritual systems, the highest standing is assigned to those whose lineage is Brāhmaṇa and whose rites, conduct, and learning are present. But eligibility for rites and conduct proper to a Brāhmaṇical birth belongs to those born from Brāhmaṇa seed and womb. The author then asks: suppose a landowner has four sons, one practising medicine, one a craft, one legal advocacy, and one priesthood, while none farms. To whom should the land be given? Should the king take it? How would their ownership of the land remain? Even proponents of varṇa by work do not want to give up land inherited through family succession.

Social order and the work-based objection

The claim that modern transport and communications—aircraft, submarines, airships, telegraph-like instruments, and long-distance listening devices—have made the whole world closely connected, and that this makes a birth-based order impossible, is also called trivial. There is no practical contradiction in a birth-based arrangement. The author quotes the saying: “When the king is righteous, the people are righteous; when he is sinful, they are sinful. The people follow the king; as the king is, so are the people” (Cāṇakya Nīti). Manu likewise says: “All the varṇas would be corrupted, all the boundaries would be broken, and the whole world would be thrown into confusion by the failure of punishment” (7.24). The king is said to be the age itself: when the ruler is righteous, the people’s righteousness follows.

Some say that in the age of Vikrama, varṇa everywhere was based on action, and that this produced all-round prosperity. The author asks how such an arrangement then disappeared, and how the opposite arrangement came to prevail. Manu says: “A person of a higher jāti who, through greed, lives by the duties of a lower one should be punished by the king and quickly sent away” (10.96). Yet, the author says, people now commonly take up priestly and teaching work for the sake of income; many officiate at sacrifices, and discernment in accepting gifts is rare. Even Brāhmaṇas take up work outside their proper duties to support their families. Under a work-based definition, each person’s varṇa would simply follow whichever livelihood he happened to pursue, so scriptural prohibitions on mixing livelihoods would become pointless.

The Mahābhārata says: “Disregard for the Vedas, violation of the śāstras, disorder everywhere—these bring about one’s own ruin” (Śānti Parvan 79.19). The author takes this as showing that transgressing the limits taught by the Vedas and other śāstras leads to harm. An objection is raised: although passages establishing varṇa by birth are more numerous in the Vedas and Smṛtis, statements establishing it by action are fewer; still, the fewer statements should be given greater weight, just as a few gems are more valuable than many clods of earth. The answer is that there is no proof that those passages have greater authority. By the reasoning already given, passages that directly establish varṇa by birth are stronger; statements about action are either secondary in purport or refer to a later birth.

The four varṇas in the Veda

An objection says that the Vedas do not mention four varṇas at all. It cites verses that speak of two: “Having slain the Dasyus, he protected the Ārya varṇa” (Ṛg Veda Saṃhitā 3.34.6); “He hid the low Dāsa varṇa” (Ṛg Veda Saṃhitā 2.12.4); and “He nourished both varṇas, the dark and the light” (Ṛg Veda Saṃhitā 1.176.6). Although the Veda mentions many groups through words such as Brāhmaṇa and Rājanya, the objection says, that does not prove they are varṇas. It also cites the Mahābhārata’s statement: “Formerly this whole world was one varṇa, O Yudhiṣṭhira; through differences of action and rites, the fourfold varṇa was established,” and concludes that the fourfold arrangement is recent rather than ancient.

The author replies that this misunderstands the passages. The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa expressly says: “There are four varṇas: Brāhmaṇa, Rājanya, Vaiśya, and Śūdra” (5.5.4.9). The Maitrāyaṇī Saṃhitā likewise says: “There are four persons: Brāhmaṇa, Rājanya, Vaiśya, and Śūdra” (4.4.6). The Vājasaneyi tradition also names Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, and Śūdra as the four varṇas, with the Brāhmaṇa foremost. The Veda uses “Ārya” for the first three varṇas and “Dāsa” for the Śūdra. The Ṛg Veda says: “The three Ārya peoples, bright, and the others” (7.31.7). Sāyaṇa explains “Ārya varṇa” as the higher three varṇas and “Dāsa varṇa” as the Śūdra and the rest. In the verse “He nourished both varṇas,” the word varṇa means the two things praised there—desire and austerity—not two human social groups. Thus, the author says, the Veda’s fourfold varṇa arrangement is established.

The Purāṇic statement about one varṇa

The statement “formerly the whole world was one varṇa” is not found in the Mahābhārata in the form alleged; even if it is, the author says, its meaning is different. Before a creation in a particular kalpa, the beings of the preceding kalpa existed in a condition comparable to Brahmā. In the next creation, differences of action and sense-faculties, formed from the impressions of earlier actions, gave rise to distinctions in study and other activities and thus to the four varṇas. This interpretation accords with Śruti. The Mahābhārata says: “The fourfold varṇa was created earlier by the Self-born Lord” (Anuśāsana Parvan 143). The author argues that the passage describes varṇa manifesting in a creation according to previous actions; it does not mean that human beings once had no distinctions of birth.

A future Purāṇa passage is also cited to argue that all beings are children of one father and therefore must be of one jāti. The author rejects this inference: fruits growing on different parts of the same fig tree may belong to one species, yet there are still distinctions such as male and female. All human beings share humanity, but that does not erase other birth-groups. In the present case, one Supreme Father gives rise to gods, ancestors, humans, animals, birds, insects, moths, trees, stones, and other classes; distinctions of jāti are plainly present. The passage, the author concludes, refers to the combined role of birth and action, as already explained.

Max Müller’s claim

The claim that “varṇa-by-work is not found in the Veda” and that the whole varṇa system is absent there is attributed to Max Müller’s book What Can India Teach Us? (lecture 2, page 1865 in the cited edition). The author calls this mistaken and cites further Vedic passages: “He hid the low Dāsa varṇa” (Ṛg Veda 2.12.1); “the three Ārya peoples, bright, and the others” (Ṛg Veda 3.34.9); a Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa statement distinguishing the divine varṇa, Brāhmaṇa, and Śūdra; and a Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa passage comparing the horse, Vaiśya, Śūdra, donkey, Brāhmaṇa, and Kṣatriya in sacrificial procedure. The Taittirīya Saṃhitā also says: “Therefore the Śūdra is not admitted to the sacrifice” (7.1.1.6). The author says these passages establish differences among varṇas and their relative standing, and that Müller’s statement should therefore not be accepted.

Manu and the Vedic tradition

The cited Vedic texts also say that a Śūdra is not admitted to the sacrifice and is not to perform the milking required for the sacrificial rite (Maitrāyaṇī Saṃhitā 4.6.2.22; Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 3.2.3.1.6; Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 3.2.3.10). The author takes these rules to exclude the Śūdra from sacrificial participation and from certain relations such as shared dining and marriage. Another text says that the words of Manu and Yāska are remedies among remedies (Tāṇḍya Brāhmaṇa 22.3.16), which, he argues, shows that Manu’s teaching is highly respected by the Veda. It is therefore mistaken to claim that Manu’s varṇa arrangement is not accepted by the Veda.

The modern division into a “Vedic age,” a “Smṛti age,” and a “Purāṇic age” is also rejected. The Vedas themselves mention the Vedas, ancillary disciplines, Itihāsa, and Purāṇa. The Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad lists the Ṛg, Yajur, Sāma, and Atharva Vedas, together with phonetics, ritual, grammar, etymology, metre, and astronomy (1.1.5). Nārada says: “I know the Ṛg Veda, the Yajur Veda, the Sāma Veda, the Atharva Veda as the fourth, and Itihāsa-Purāṇa as the fifth Veda” (Chāndogya Upaniṣad 7.1.2). The Bṛhadāraṇyaka says that the Ṛg Veda, Yajur Veda, Sāma Veda, Atharva and Aṅgiras, Itihāsa, Purāṇa, knowledge, Upaniṣads, verses, aphorisms, explanations, and commentaries are all the breath of the Great Being (2.4.10). The author cites these and other passages to show that Itihāsa and Purāṇa are not later, unrelated additions.

Social unity and the charge of neglect

An objection says that, because there was no mutual affection or organization, Brāhmaṇas did not try to unite people, even while witnessing the great distress of Hindus and their dharma under foreign rule. In Kerala, it says, Śūdras and Ati-Śūdras, treated as untouchable and neglected by Brāhmaṇas and others, became more than a million people known as Māppilas, and a great custom of hatred toward Hindus arose. The author calls this account mistaken. Even when Vedic Brāhmaṇas and others intended mutual affection and organization, greed, attachment, hatred, and other causes led some to enter other religious communities, increasing their numbers. Rāma’s affection did not prevent Rāvaṇa and the Rākṣasas from acting in the opposite way; Yudhiṣṭhira’s affection did not prevent Duryodhana and the others from doing so. Many Hindus, including Brāhmaṇas, have nevertheless continued to work for the protection of dharma and the independence of their religious tradition. The author adds that Tilak and Mālavīya, too, were Brāhmaṇas.

The hymn to the Maruts

The objection appeals to the Ṛg Veda verse: “They are neither eldest nor youngest, nor middlemost; they have grown in might. Well-born, with Pṛśni as their mother, born on earth, come to us from heaven” (5.59.6). It is said that this verse denies distinctions of high and low varṇa. The author replies that its subject is the forty-nine Maruts, whose equality in age is being stated; it does not deny distinctions of varṇa among human beings.

A proposed explanation takes “well-born, with Pṛśni as their mother, born on earth” to mean that all Ārya people are children of Bhāratabhūmi, the sacred land of India: “You are brothers, joined by affection, goodwill, humility, and other virtues; unite against dangers, speak truthfully and pleasantly for the nation’s welfare, and grow in glory.” It then treats the Maruts as brave destroyers of external and internal enemies, equal in affection, and born of Earth; their father is said to be the eternal Lord, protector of devotees and destroyer of the wicked. The verse is further expanded into a prayer that the people be fortunate and that Earth, like a well-nourished cow, yield learning, agriculture, wealth, grain, and other gifts. The author calls this a lengthy, fanciful explanation that reverses the mantra’s meaning. Mutual affection and unity are good aims, he says, but that does not prove they are the meaning of this particular mantra. Its meaning must be established from its seer, deity, grammatical construction, and context.

Moreover, the fact that all beings are children of the Supreme does not abolish distinctions among animals, birds, and the other kinds of life. In the same way, calling all people children of Pṛśni does not erase the distinctions among Brāhmaṇa and other birth-groups. If “Pṛśni” means the Earth as mother, why should only Indians be her children? The cited verse does not say that all humanity has one jāti.

According to Sāyaṇa, the hymn beginning “They are neither eldest nor youngest” is addressed to the Maruts. The third hymn of the relevant section is dedicated to them; its eighth verse is in Triṣṭubh metre and the preceding seven are in Jagatī. Thus the hymn praises the Maruts, not all human beings. They are neither eldest nor youngest, are not inferior in strength, and grow through their might; they are well-born, born from Pṛśni, and beneficial to human beings. They come from heaven toward the worshippers. The remainder of the verse likewise refers to the Maruts and their father Rudra, and to Pṛśni as their mother.

Another possible reading addresses the priests: “O well-born children of Pṛśni, come here to the earth from heaven; come toward us.” This too concerns the Maruts, who are brothers and equal to one another, and who grow in splendour and bring good fortune. Thus, the author concludes, abandoning the traditional meaning established by context, seer, and deity in favour of invented explanations that seem useful for social unity may produce a socially welcome message, but it cannot be presented as the meaning of the Vedic mantra.

Common aims, different duties

The hymn’s words about having a common resolve and common hearts may also be understood as praying that people’s intentions, efforts, and conduct be alike in being straight, free of crookedness, and adorned with affection and other virtues. Their hearts should be united and should not work against the nation’s honour, freedom, or welfare. The Gītā supports seeing others as oneself: “One who sees the same everywhere, by comparing with oneself, whether in pleasure or pain, is considered the highest yogin” (6.32). Another saying is: “One should not do to others what is adverse to oneself.” Yet it does not follow that everyone must perform every action. The Gītā says: “Better one’s own dharma, though defective, than another’s dharma well performed” (3.35).

People may gather and speak together for a good purpose even though their ways of travelling and acting differ. An army seeking victory may use chariots, steam vehicles, aircraft, and different kinds of weapons; the variety of means does not prevent them from pursuing one goal together. In the same way, agreement and cooperation do not require everyone to perform the same work.

The same mantra and the assembly

It is claimed that “May your mantra be one; may your assembly be one; may your mind and heart be one” (Ṛg Veda 10.191.3) means that everyone must use the same secret language and the same plan, in one form, to serve the good of society and the nation. Another interpretation says that the Gāyatrī mantra and other mantras should be common to all Āryas living in India, and that assemblies and councils should be uniform. The author replies that this cannot mean that everyone has the same tasks: that would erase the world’s variety. Even plans for the welfare of society and the nation differ according to people’s qualifications and responsibilities. Nor is the Gāyatrī to be recited by everyone; the texts cited by the author prohibit the recitation of the Sāvitrī and praṇava by women and Śūdras. He quotes: “They do not wish the Sāvitrī, praṇava, and sacrificial formulas for women and Śūdras,” and, “If a woman or Śūdra learns it, he or she goes downward at death” (Nārada Pañcarātra 1.3). A twelve-syllable great mantra is prescribed for all, while the mantra with praṇava is for the twice-born, according to the Bṛhannāradīya Purāṇa.

Nor can everyone sit with the same rank at an assembly: distinguished people are given distinguished seats. If everyone’s seat were the same, why would anyone occupy a higher seat? Distinctions between independence and dependence, and between what is desired and undesired, are appropriate. Manu says: “Everything subject to another is suffering; everything under one’s own control is happiness” (4.160).

Sāyaṇa’s ritual interpretation

The meaning given by Sāyaṇa and other traditional commentators is the one that fits the hymn. The seer of the hymn is Saṃsamidvasu; the deity of the opening verse is Saṃjñāna. Since the context is ritual and mantras are connected with sacrifice, the words address the officiants. Sāyaṇa explains: “O singers, come together; be assembled and united. Speak to one another, abandoning opposition, with one and the same statement. Let your minds understand one and the same meaning. As the ancient gods, united and of one accord, obtained their shares of the offerings, so you too, giving up disagreement, may receive your share”—that is, the sacrificial fee.

The same hymn occurs in the Atharva Veda with some differences. There, the verses are assigned to a rite for concord: a pot of water or liquor is filled, consecrated with the hymn, and carried through the village according to the prescribed ritual. Sāyaṇa accordingly explains it as a rite for people who desire agreement. The gods, such as Indra, who formerly acted with a common understanding, received the portion prepared for the sacrificer; the people should likewise set aside mutual hostility and attain the desired result.

According to the Ṛg Veda’s ritual context, “May your mantra be one” can refer to the hymn or praise shared by priests engaged in one rite. It does not mean that everyone in society must do the same work. A common mantra, assembly, or intention does not prohibit distinctions prescribed by the śāstras, including different offerings and ritual shares for different officiants.

The Atharva Veda’s harmony verses

The next mantra says: “May your minds, the inner instruments of thought, be alike. May the knowledge arising from your thoughts and understandings unite in a common purpose. I recite and prepare for you a single, common mantra; with the same common oblation—clarified butter, a cake, or other offering—I make the offering for you.” Another verse says: “May your resolve be one; may your hearts be one; may your mind be one, so that you may live together in happiness.” Here, “resolve” means intention and determination; “heart” means the inner organ; and “mind” is used distributively for each person’s mind. The prayer is that their thoughts should be in accord so they can live together.

The Atharva Veda’s Sāyaṇa commentary likewise explains the assembly as people engaged in a common undertaking. With a shared oblation, such as clarified butter, the sacrificer offers into the fire for those connected with the rite. The mantra’s practical setting is a concord ritual; it does not state that every member of society has identical duties.

Another mantra says: “I make you of one heart, of one mind, free from hatred toward one another. Love one another as a cow loves the calf just born” (Atharva Veda 3.30). It continues: “May the son follow the father; may he be of one mind with his mother. May wife and husband speak words full of sweetness and peace.” It also says: “Let not a brother hate a brother, nor a sister hate a sister. United in purpose and of one observance, speak words that are auspicious.” These verses have a clear meaning: they urge affection and concord within family and community.

The hymn further says: “By that knowledge of Brahman through which the gods do not disagree and do not hate one another, we make that shared understanding in your home for all people.” Another verse says: “With common understanding, working together toward a common accomplishment, sharing the same undertaking, do not separate from one another. Come together speaking kindly to one another.” The words call for cooperation and kind speech; they do not forbid the suppression of evil or the description of virtues and faults, which the mantras themselves contain.

The final verse says: “May your drinking place be common; may your share of food be common; I yoke you together with the same harness. Worship the fire together, like the spokes joined around a wheel’s hub.” The author reads the common well, food, and yoke as shared resources and common participation in a task. A common undertaking still allows each participant to perform the work assigned to him.

Common prayer and common duties

The prayer continues: “I make you face one another, united in mind, through the shared performance of one observance. May goodwill be yours in the morning and in the evening, as the gods, protecting the immortal nectar of heaven, are united in mind.” The author says that ritual texts permit common food and drink only where they are not contrary to the rules of Smṛti and Śāstra. The groups seeking concord are generally families grounded in the Veda, since Vedic rites belong only to those eligible to perform them. Even at an assembly, equal-mindedness does not mean that all distinctions disappear; distinguished people are given distinguished seats.

Religious duty and political defeat

An objection says: “Muslim rulers abducted the wives and sisters of Indian Kṣatriya kings, who gave them to the rulers. The Hindus endured this helplessly. Those rulers destroyed Hindu temples; the demolition of the Somnātha and Kāśī Viśvanātha temples, and the mosque now standing at the site of the Viśvanātha temple, appear to mock the great pride of those who lived there.” The author says that this account is true, but that the cause was the grave misfortune of abandoning dharma, not devotion to dharma or pride in religious duty.

He argues that people who lacked confidence in the śāstras and in the afterlife, who were atheistic, and who were careless and idle through habitual comfort, failed to organize armies, weapons, and their supporting equipment. This led to defeat. Had the proper qualities and work of each varṇa been valued—the Brāhmaṇa’s discernment, learning, and courage; the Kṣatriya’s valour, strength, energy, and radiance; the Vaiśya’s agriculture, cattle-keeping, and trade; and the Śūdra’s craft, labour, and service—there would have been no thought of defeat. The Mahābhārata says that in the age of truth there was no king, punishment, or punisher: people protected one another through dharma (Śānti Parvan 59.14). The author says that this ideal does not prove that duties and roles were identical.

The body analogy

An objection compares the Śūdra, separated as the feet of society, to a body that would suffer if its feet were trampled: “How could they fail to see the body’s suffering when the foot is struck? By disrespecting the Śūdra limb, the twice-born have surely caused this pitiable state for the whole body.” The author says that this charge rests on a mistaken understanding and that the arrangement itself does not cause disrespect.

Hands, feet, head, and other limbs are assigned different tasks according to their capacities; that does not mean any limb is dishonoured. The mouth takes food; the arms protect; the belly gathers and distributes. In the same way, the Śūdra is assigned work involving labour, craft, and service. There is no insult in this, since abandoning one’s natural work can itself bring fault. The weakening of religious feeling, he says, arose from spreading teachings that oppose the scriptures defining these duties. People were enticed by those who rejected dharma and accepted other religions.

How social weakness arose

Some people, violating social and religious rules, became degraded and left their community for another religion; then they harassed those who remained faithful to their former religion. Those who were defeated appealed to the courts; those who lost jāti status and its duties complained about dharma; those unable to obtain what they wanted denounced others—so the author characterizes their conduct. Over time, neglect of ethics and political learning, and carelessness in organizing the strength of the people and the army, produced weakness. The kingdom was lost and temples were destroyed. The author says that neither devotion to dharma nor fidelity to the śāstras should be blamed for this decline. People who witnessed oppression but lacked discernment and strength could not resist it.

Some scholars cite Manu’s statement: “From the learned Brāhmaṇa born in this land, all human beings on earth should learn their own particular conduct” (2.20), and say this is arrogance. The author calls that conclusion mistaken. Even when circumstances are adverse, stable character and the greatness of good conduct remain worthy of honour. The sun may set and appear to have lost its light, but its intense radiance cannot thereby be denied.

Discussion of widow remarriage

An objection says: “Learned people regard widow remarriage as contrary to the śāstras, while Mlecchas abduct widows and other women. From these women many children were born, hostile to Hindu dharma and culture and displaying Mleccha ways; this is why Mlecchas became numerous in Bengal. From the standpoint of the śāstras, widow remarriage may be a small transgression, but adultery, abduction, and the like committed by Mlecchas are sixteen times more sinful. To prevent a great sin, a small sin may be accepted, as the wise say that one should avoid a greater loss even at the cost of a lesser one.”

The author rejects this reasoning because the situations have different causes. In a condition of weakness, abduction may happen to unmarried girls and married women as well as widows. The proper remedy is therefore to remove the weakness, not to permit widow remarriage as a way to do so. If the rule of restraint is discarded, this would itself encourage weakness and misconduct.

In truth, he says, even unmarried girls may become immoral in a corrupt environment, while widows can remain of good conduct when the surroundings cultivate good conduct. Another objection points to renunciants who take vows of asceticism and detachment but, after obtaining wealth and property for their monasteries, become attached to them, acquire wives and children, and return to household life; it asks what can be expected of helpless widows. People desire a young woman without a husband, the objection says, as birds desire meat thrown on the ground. It quotes: “All people desire a woman who is without a husband, as birds desire meat cast upon the earth” (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan 158.12).

The objection continues that young widows, driven by desire, have secretly formed relationships in ways contrary to their family’s conduct. It cites cases in which a washerman’s wife became involved with the wife of a newly established Brāhmaṇa, a Brāhmaṇa widow in Gujarat—said to have been oppressed in her father-in-law’s home—became the wife of a Muslim, and other widows rebelled and married Yavanas. It concludes that for young widows attached to sensual pleasure, arranging a suitable marriage within their community would prevent greater harm. The author calls this argument elaborate but unsound: from time immemorial, people have lied, committed adultery, theft, and murder despite the existence of religious and ethical rules. Such conduct is no reason to abolish the rules. The prohibition on widow remarriage, he says, is prescribed for welfare in this life and the next. Animals have no marriage system and act by instinct; human beings, unlike animals, are distinguished by religious rules and restraints.

Widowhood, restraint, and conduct

For human beings, the author says, accepting the authority of Śāstra is the distinctive feature of human life. Dharma and adharma are known through Śāstra; they are not inferred only from worldly loss or gain. Widow remarriage, he states, is expressly prohibited in the texts he follows.

If widow remarriage is proposed because some widows commit adultery, he asks, should the duties of ascetics also be relaxed because some ascetics behave improperly? No such relaxation is accepted; fallen ascetics are censured. Separate arrangements may be made for people who have fallen from their observances, including arrangements for food and marriage, without changing the rule for those who remain within the tradition. In the same way, the misconduct of some widows does not establish that widow remarriage should be permitted.

The author repeats that an environment supporting good conduct will encourage good conduct among married women and widows alike; without it, misconduct can arise among wives and unmarried girls as well. He points to the Kṣatriya women of Mewār: even when their husbands attained a warrior’s death, their widows entered the funeral fire rather than becoming unchaste. He says this also explains the Gītā’s statement that when women are corrupted, social intermixture follows. The remedy is to foster good conduct, following the example of exemplary people: “Whatever a superior person does, other people do the same” (Bhagavad Gītā 3.21). The solution is not licence, encouragement to violate dharma, or abandonment of restraint. If Arjuna’s argument against fighting had been accepted, the Gītā’s teaching would have led to refusing the war; instead, it upheld the duty appropriate to the situation. The author says that neither the Kaurava and Pāṇḍava women nor the women of Mewār became unchaste merely because their husbands died in battle. Restraint, not encouragement of unrestrained conduct, is the remedy.

The argument that widows’ desires make remarriage necessary is also rejected. Individual misconduct is not a reason to discard a rule established by Śāstra. The Veda itself teaches, “Choose what is free from blame, not what is otherwise” (Taittirīya Upaniṣad 1.11). Desire, anger, and similar impulses do not make adharma into dharma or dharma into adharma.

Age differences in marriage

The next objection says that marriage between an older man and a young woman also causes the same harms as widowhood. It asks whether anyone has heard of a young woman willingly marrying an old, worn-out man; whether such a husband can love her; and whether individual happiness is possible without affection. It accuses learned people of prescribing this only to fulfil selfish interests. It quotes the rule: “A wife must never be abandoned by her husband, even if she is old, impotent, leprous, maimed, learned, or foolish; this is the eternal dharma.” Yet it observes that the reverse rule—“A young man must never abandon his wife, even if she is old, maimed, leprous, or unattractive”—is not stated in the same way. The texts say “he should take a wife who is his equal,” but do not say “she should take a husband who is her equal.”

Some modern interpreters therefore explain the rule to mean that a sixty-year-old man should marry a sixty-year-old woman. The objection says that a young woman whose natural desire is forcibly suppressed will seek union with someone, whether socially superior or inferior, or even a Mleccha. Draupadī is quoted: “I have five husbands, and a sixth too pleases me; men’s desires are never satisfied” (Mahābhārata, cited in the scanned text). The author calls this reasoning unsound. Marriage between an older man and a young woman is an exception, he says: when a wife has died, remarriage may be necessary to continue the agnihotra and the family line. If a husband dies, the wife’s observance of chastity is itself said to bring her the highest destination; the same necessity for a new marriage is not prescribed for her.

This also answers the question why women do not remarry like men, or why an older woman does not marry a young man. The author says that nature varies, and the duties of men and women are not identical. A man can father children in several unions during a year, while a woman bears one pregnancy at a time. Different natural capacities are accompanied by different rules of dharma; the aim is not to make all rules symmetrical. If women’s desire is eight times greater, as another maxim says, compassion on that basis could be used to defend a woman’s relations with many men whenever one husband does not satisfy her. The author rejects that conclusion because culture is founded on restraint.

The analogy with renunciation is likewise rejected. Renunciation is prescribed for the dispassionate, while one still attached to worldly life remains a householder. But, the author argues, the śāstras do not lay down a parallel rule that an attached widow should remarry while a dispassionate widow should not. The cited rule is general: after her husband’s death, a woman should not enter into a relationship with another man. One mantra is quoted: “This woman, choosing the world of her husband, lies down beside you; guarding what is ancient, establish here prosperity, offspring, and wealth” (Atharva Veda 18.2.1).

Vedic verses about widowhood

One objection cites a mantra to argue that widow remarriage is permitted. Its proposed meaning is: “For this mortal person, this woman before him, desiring [him], chooses the husband’s world—the husband’s place—and, as his companion in dharma, follows the ancient, eternal rule of joining him. Having gone from this mortal world, she follows you and reaches your company. Therefore, grant her children, descendants, and wealth, so that she may attain companionship with you.” The objection asks how a dead man could be addressed as a mortal and claims that the words “here,” “after you,” and “choosing a suitable husband” show that a desirous widow may remarry.

It also cites the mantra: “Rise, woman, to the world of the living. You have approached and are lying beside this dead husband. Rise from here; go to a living, suitable young man who will take your hand. You have entered into the status of wife to a man who desires to take you as a second wife” (Ṛg Veda 10.18.8; Atharva Veda 18.3.2; Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 6.1.14). It interprets didhīṣu as a man who marries a previously married woman for a second time, and cites the rule: “A man who desires the wife of a deceased husband, even when appointed according to dharma, is known as a didhīṣupati.”

The author rejects this as contrary to the ritual instructions. These two mantras are used in the funeral rite. The Sūtra says that, at the cremation, the widow is raised by her brother-in-law, a resident student, or an old servant, who recites “Rise, woman; go to the world of the living.” The preceding Sūtra passages also show that this happens in the funeral ceremony: a relative or attendant raises the woman who had been lying beside the dead man, points out her children and the living world, and consoles her so she will not follow her husband into death. To claim that, in front of an Indian woman at her husband’s funeral, a man is instructing her to take a new young husband would be barbarous, the author says, not the act of a civilized person.

The objection that a dead person cannot be addressed does not hold: even an inanimate weapon is addressed in the words “Do not injure him with this weapon.” Moreover, the person who has left the body is a conscious self; why could that self not be addressed? At a husband’s funeral, a woman generally does not desire another husband, so the proposed meaning “choose the husband’s home” is also incoherent. “The world of the husband” refers to the world of the husband already established, not a future husband who is not present. There is no authority for abandoning what is present in favour of an imagined future husband. The funeral context is concerned with the deceased man, who has just been mentioned; the reference to him is natural. Nor is there any basis in the mantra for taking the word “woman” to mean a woman who is desirous of remarriage.

The proposed grammatical connection—“here, choosing after you a suitable husband”—is likewise unsupported. The word “after” in “preserving the ancient dharma” cannot be attached in that way, and there is no basis for reading “choosing a husband” into the passage. The author’s own interpretation is that “here” means this world: the husband gives his righteous wife permission to remain here, and tells her to preserve the children and wealth that already exist, so she can maintain herself in widowhood and protect her descendants. The point is to secure their welfare and livelihood.

The claim that the same mantra would give contradictory meanings for a desirous widow and a dispassionate widow is dismissed. Such a reading would split one sentence into incompatible meanings, which the Mīmāṃsā tradition regards as a serious fault. The author’s interpretation is that the woman, the wife of the deceased man, chooses his world and approaches him; she wishes to share his company again after death, whether through joining him in death or through the observance of widowhood. She preserves the ancient dharma of a faithful wife, whose highest duty is with her husband. Sītā expresses the same wish in the Raghuvaṃśa: “I shall practise austerity, my gaze fixed upon you, so that in another birth too you alone may be my husband, and there be no separation.” The deceased husband, in turn, tells his righteous wife to remain here and care for the children and wealth already present.

The meaning of “Rise, woman”

Sītā expresses this commitment in the Raghuvaṃśa: “I shall practise austerity, my gaze fixed upon you, so that in another birth too you alone may be my husband, and there be no separation.” The husband’s request that she remain here is understood as a request to care for the children and wealth already present and to preserve her welfare and that of her descendants.

The author then explains “Rise, woman.” “O woman, you have come near and are lying beside this dead husband whose life has departed. Rise from this place. Look toward the world of the living—the home and the children, the living descendants. Leave this place and rise. You are lying beside a husband whose life has departed; therefore get up and come.” The words “one who takes your hand” refer to a man who will take her hand; didhīṣu here is read as one who desires to beget offspring. The woman has already obtained the state of wife through her relation with the deceased husband, and the author takes the mantra to tell her to leave the funeral place and care for her children, not to seek another husband.

The author says that this is the meaning suitable to the mantra’s use in the funeral rite. How could a priest at a funeral tell a widow to leave her dead husband and seek a living young man, so as to acquire a new status as wife? The form didhīṣu in this mantra is not the established technical word didhīṣupati for the husband of a woman married a second time. The latter has a long vowel; the mantra has the short-vowel form, which the author derives in its ordinary sense from a desire to beget. Since “the man who takes her hand” already expresses the idea of a husband, he argues, there is no reason to add a separate word meaning “second husband.”

The author says this reading is supported by Sāyaṇa and the ritual commentaries. The Atharva Veda’s funeral section directs that, at the first hymn, the woman be placed beside the dead man. The verse says: “This woman, choosing the world of her husband, lies down beside you, the departed; preserving the ancient dharma, grant her offspring and wealth here” (Atharva Veda 18.3.1). It speaks of the woman who has come beside her deceased husband, sharing the religious life they observed together. She chooses the world attained through her husband’s sacrifices, gifts, and offerings and follows him after death. The request for offspring and wealth concerns her children and descendants in a future birth, according to the author’s explanation.

A supporting verse says: “A woman who has entered the fire with her husband should be lifted out, as a serpent is drawn from its hole by a charm.” The author uses this to explain the rite for the woman who follows her husband in death: the verse concerns her attaining offspring and wealth in another birth. If, instead, the widow is to remain alive, the next verse is recited to raise her from beside the body and direct her toward the world of the living. In this way, the author distinguishes the ritual instructions for a woman who joins her husband in death from those for a woman who remains alive.

The cow in the funeral verses

The author says the ritual context is confirmed by the following two verses, which address the cow led in the funeral rite: “I saw a young, living cow being led among the dead, conducted around the corpse. Covered by dense, blinding darkness, she was turned from the east and led here” (Atharva Veda 18.3.3). The commentary identifies her as the anustaraṇī, the cow led around the funeral pyre with the wooden fuel. It is the cow—not a widow—whose circumambulation of the corpse is described. She is covered by darkness because she cannot distinguish what is beneficial and harmful; the officiant turns her away from the dead and brings her toward the living.

The next verse addresses the cow: “Knowing the world of the living, O unkillable one, following the path of the gods, go on your way, producing the offerings of milk and curds. This is your cowherd; receive him, and raise him to the heavenly world” (Atharva Veda 18.3.4). The author says the address “O unkillable one” is a familiar designation for a cow, and “your cowherd” clearly refers to her bull. The rite concerns a sacrificial cow associated with the deceased; interpreting the passage as an instruction for a widow to remarry contradicts both its words and its ritual context.

Heaven and sensual pleasure

The opposing interpretation says that the woman is a young widow, intoxicated by youth and alive, who wants to enjoy ordinary pleasures; she is being led away from the dead and toward a living man who can take her hand and become her second husband. It makes “the world of the living” mean the world of young men and women enjoying one another, and “heaven” mean the pleasure of sensual union. The author calls this understanding misguided. It would make heaven mean ordinary sensual pleasure, even though other texts describe thousands of Brāhmaṇa youths who remained celibate and attained heaven. The author argues that heaven cannot simply mean worldly sexual enjoyment.

The word devara and the Ṛg Veda passage

Another argument relies on the word devara, taken as a widow’s brother-in-law or a second husband. The author says that this depends on a different interpretation and does not establish remarriage. In a passage attributed to Yāska, devara is connected with play or sport; where a widow’s young brother-in-law is still a child, he is not the person who can take responsibility for her household. A widow may remain under the care of her father, brother, or other relatives; the word does not by itself prescribe a second marriage.

The argument also cites a Ṛg Veda hymn asking: “Where do you stay at night, and where during the day? Who invites you to his house? Who, like a widow, brings you near to her brother-in-law, as a woman does her companion?” (Ṛg Veda 10.40.2). The author says this is a hymn to the Aśvins and is recited in connection with Ghoṣā’s praise of them, not a rule about a widow’s marriage. The verse’s poetic comparison does not establish a legal or ritual injunction. Its context and the identity of the hymn’s deities must govern its meaning.

Vijāmātu, marriage, and widowhood

The Nirukta’s explanation of the word devara does not, the author argues, establish a general rule of widow remarriage. The Mahābhārata says that when a woman has no husband she may make her brother-in-law her husband; nevertheless, the author maintains that a wife’s following her husband in death is treated as the primary course, while a young girl does not follow him, in accordance with the cited smṛti rule. He reads Yāska’s account of the devara as describing a young brother-in-law, still absorbed in play and incapable of taking on the household responsibilities needed to support a widow.

The author next cites Durgācārya’s explanation, which describes a wife following her husband and sharing his company after death; he treats such passages as referring to anv ārohaṇa, a wife’s following her husband to the funeral fire. He also cites an explanation in which devara is understood in the sense of a lord or protector. On that reading, a woman without a husband should rely on her father, brother, son, parents-in-law, or maternal relatives for protection. The passage then invokes Manu’s instruction: “Let her remain patient until death, self-controlled and celibate, desiring the highest dharma of women who have one husband” (Manu 5.158).

The discussion turns to the word vijāmātu in Ṛg Veda 1.109.2. The hymn says: “I have heard that you two are more generous than a vijāmātu, or even than a wife’s brother. Then, when Soma is offered, I shall make a new hymn for you, Indra and Agni.” The author follows the Nirukta’s derivation of vijāmātu as an incompletely established son-in-law: a man whose marriage relation has not yet been consummated and who gives generously in order to obtain the bride. The word, he argues, does not mean a widow’s second husband.

To support that reading, he says that the bride and groom’s union takes place on the fourth day of the marriage, not earlier. The Gṛhya Sūtra prescribes that they observe celibacy, eat without salt or pungent seasoning, and sleep on the ground for three nights; only after those three nights does union take place. He cites smṛti passages concerning the fourth-day rites and a marriage that has not yet been consummated. He also cites Manu’s rule that a man of thirty may marry a beautiful girl of twelve, or a man of twenty-four a girl of eight (Manu 9.94, as numbered in the printed text).

Parāśara is quoted as prescribing another husband for a woman in five calamities: “When the husband is lost, dead, has renounced the world, is impotent, or has fallen, another husband is prescribed for women” (Parāśara Smṛti, cited in the text). The author treats this as a specific emergency provision, not as proof that widow remarriage is a general rule. He then distinguishes the courses he attributes to widows: the highest is to remain celibate; another is to follow the husband in death; a woman unable to do either may remarry; and he condemns adultery and an illicit pregnancy as the lowest course.

Irāvān and Ulūpī

An objection cites the story of Irāvān, Arjuna’s son by a Nāga princess, as an example of widow remarriage. The Mahābhārata account says that Irāvān was born to Arjuna and the daughter of the Nāga king; it also recounts that the woman had been left without a husband after he was killed by Garuḍa, and that Arjuna accepted her when she desired him. On this basis, the objection claims that a widow’s marriage is established by the epic.

The author rejects that inference. Even if an exceptional act is described in an epic, he says, it does not establish a general dharma, especially where other passages call widow remarriage improper. He cites the Bhāgavata’s statement that Rukmiṇī’s son knowingly accepted an unrighteous marriage out of affection, to show that an epic narrative may report an act without approving it as a rule. He likewise says that conduct attributed to Kerala Nambūdiri Brāhmaṇas, or to other people, cannot establish dharma when it conflicts with the śāstra.

He further argues that the Nāgas are described as a divine class and so their unions with Arjuna need not be treated as an ordinary human marriage governed by the same rules. In the Mahābhārata passage about Ulūpī, she is called a maiden and speaks as one who has no other husband; the author therefore reads the episode as no proof that a widow may marry again. The story, in his interpretation, concerns Arjuna’s union with a Nāga princess, not a general injunction permitting widow remarriage.

Birth, lineage, and exceptional births

The discussion next considers children born to royal women by Brāhmaṇa sages. The author says such children could hold royal authority because their mothers belonged to Kṣatriya families; this does not mean that a king’s lawful Kṣatriya wives and their sons lost their established status. He compares this with the sons of Vyāsa, including the Pāṇḍavas and Dhṛtarāṣṭra’s line, whose Kṣatriya standing is not denied.

An objection asks why Vidura, Vyāsa’s son, should be called a Śūdra if the father’s seed is decisive. The author answers by distinguishing the women who bore the children. Satyavatī, Vyāsa’s mother, was born to a fisherman, but she later became queen and bore the Kṣatriya princes Citrāṅgada and Vicitravīrya. The sons born to the Kṣatriya queens through niyoga were born in a Kṣatriya field; Vidura, by contrast, was born from a Śūdra maidservant. The author says that Vyāsa’s own Brāhmaṇa status is accounted for by the exceptional power of the sage Parāśara and Vyāsa’s extraordinary birth, whereas the ordinary rule gives priority to the mother’s field. He also notes a Kerala custom in which a sister’s sons, rather than her sons, inherit property; he says this customary inheritance does not settle the question of varṇa.

The author then discusses the word agrajanman, “one born first.” A verse uses it of the elder person in a group, whose precedence is relative to others. The author accepts that this is a possible general meaning, but says that among Brāhmaṇas the word is conventionally used for one born from a Brāhmaṇī’s womb. He cites the traditional explanation “because he is born first from his mother” and argues that the established meaning of the word in this setting cannot be displaced by its broader compound meaning.
