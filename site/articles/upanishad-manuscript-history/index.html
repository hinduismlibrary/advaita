Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad

बृहदारण्यकोपनिषद्

Textual home, recensions, and variation

The Bṛhadāraṇyaka belongs to the Śukla Yajurveda and forms the concluding Upaniṣadic material of the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa. It is organized into six adhyāyas and survives in two principal recensions: Mādhyaṃdina and Kāṇva. These are substantial school traditions, not merely two spellings of one printed text.

The two recensions preserve many common teachings and passages, while differing in readings, arrangement, section divisions, and some wording. A precise global count or percentage of variants is not available: no complete collation of every surviving manuscript worldwide has been published. The best practical comparison is to consult editions that identify the recension and report variant readings, especially Olivelle’s concordance of the two recensions.

Within one recension, copies also vary in ways that do not always represent a different ancient reading: missing leaves, scribal corrections, red accent marks, marginal additions, and inconsistent chapter labels are visible in the catalogue records. These details should be distinguished from the larger Mādhyaṃdina–Kāṇva differences.

Manuscripts, regions, and countable witnesses

There is no reliable worldwide census of all Bṛhadāraṇyaka manuscripts. One bounded count is possible: the current SAMHiTA digital catalogue returns 12 University of Pennsylvania records tagged to the Bṛhadāraṇyaka and its Yajurvedic subject. They are not 12 complete independent codices. Several are partial chapters, and items 994–999 are individually catalogued sections of a larger manuscript.

All 12 entries are Sanskrit in Devanāgarī on paper. Their assigned dates range from the 17th through the 19th century. The catalogue includes chapter portions as well as broader copies: for example, item 847 is titled “Adhyāya 1–6” but its descriptive note says chapters 1–3 and 5–6; item 1104 contains only chapter 1. Item 1238’s title says “chapters 7–8,” although its description says chapters 5–6. The catalogue labels need to be read alongside the record descriptions.

For production regions, the repository field is not enough: several catalogue summaries show “United States” because Penn holds the manuscripts, not because the manuscripts were made there. Better provenance evidence comes from the records themselves. The grouped chapter manuscripts 994–999 name the scribe Govinda Brahma, who is associated with Vārāṇasī, and are dated 1789 CE. Item 1238 is associated with Karavīra (Kolhapur) and is dated 1755 CE.

Printed editions and critical work

The Bṛhadāraṇyaka’s textual base is embedded in the two Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa recensions. The Mādhyandina Śatapatha was printed in an edition based on Albrecht Weber’s work; Julius Eggeling’s five-volume translation is a major reference translation. The Kāṇva Śatapatha has a complete modern critical edition and English translation from IGNCA, prepared using additional manuscripts beyond the previously printed edition. Since the Bṛhadāraṇyaka is the closing Upaniṣadic portion of these corpora, those recension-specific editions matter directly to its text.

For the Upaniṣad itself, Patrick Olivelle’s The Early Upaniṣads (1998) supplies a full Sanskrit text, reported variants, scholarly emendations, explanations of editorial choices, and a concordance of the Mādhyaṃdina and Kāṇva recensions. It is a strong modern working edition; it should not be mistaken for a census-based stemmatic edition of every manuscript in every repository.

Edition references: Olivelle, Oxford University Press · IGNCA Kāṇva Śatapatha critical edition · Mādhyandina text based on Weber.

Dating proposals from 15 scholars

The Bṛhadāraṇyaka is commonly treated as the oldest principal Upaniṣad as a whole, but its chapters and embedded sources need not all have been composed at the same time. The table records a range of scholarly approaches rather than implying that every author gave a separate date for this final form.

R. E. Hume (1921)Says the earliest texts can hardly be later than the 7th century BCE and ranks Bṛhadāraṇyaka first.

How to read the table. Most older scholars date a broad corpus, place the texts in relative order, or give a terminus rather than dating each layer of each Upaniṣad. The early texts are layered compositions, so these entries are not 15 independent exact dates. The useful modern working range is broad: around the 7th–6th centuries BCE, with a margin of roughly a century.

Was it recited before it was written?

Yes: the strongest historical account is oral transmission through the Śukla Yajurveda schools, with manuscripts written down later. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka’s two recensions, Mādhyaṃdina and Kāṇva, are direct evidence that the work travelled in distinct Vedic school lineages. Its final chapter (6.5) preserves vaṃśa, or teacher–pupil lineages, connecting knowledge to named teachers. This is consistent with an oral teaching culture, although the lineage passage by itself does not prove the exact date when a manuscript was first written.

Vedic oral tradition is independently documented as a highly disciplined memorized transmission, supported by recitation methods that preserve wording and accent. This is a better explanation for the early transmission of the text than assuming that the surviving 17th–19th-century paper codices were its original medium.

How many śākhās? The evidence supports two Bṛhadāraṇyaka recensions—Mādhyaṃdina and Kāṇva—within the Śukla Yajurveda. That is a real plurality of transmission, but it is not evidence that many unrelated Veda branches all recited this same text.

Sources: Vedic Heritage Portal, Bṛhadāraṇyaka · Finnian M. M. Gerety, “Vedic Oral Tradition,” Oxford Bibliographies.

Chāndogya Upaniṣad

छान्दोग्योपनिषद्

Textual home, recensions, and variation

The Chāndogya belongs to the Tāṇḍya school under the Kauthuma Saṃhitā of the Sāmaveda. It is the last eight of the ten prapāṭhakas of the Chāndogya Brāhmaṇa, and is divided into eight prapāṭhakas of its own when treated as a stand-alone Upaniṣad.

Unlike the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, the catalogue and reference descriptions consulted identify one primary Kauthuma/Tāṇḍya textual line; they do not document a second major recension parallel to Mādhyaṃdina and Kāṇva. That does not mean there are no manuscript variants. Copies show omissions, corrections, marginal additions, accent marks, different section numbering, and sometimes damage or missing leaves. Because no worldwide collation is available, a numerical variant total or percentage would be misleading.

Numbering can itself cause confusion: within the ten-chapter Brāhmaṇa, the Upaniṣad occupies chapters 3–10; in its stand-alone form those same chapters are called prapāṭhakas 1–8. Such numbering differences are not evidence of different words in the Sanskrit text.

Manuscripts, regions, and countable witnesses

The SAMHiTA catalogue’s Penn collection gives 13 catalogue records explicitly titled as Chāndogya Upaniṣad. They include one complete eight-prapāṭhaka copy, a second eight-prapāṭhaka copy with missing folios, the first five chapters, chapters 7–8, many chapter-6 copies, and two chapter-6 records with commentary. The count is therefore 13 entries, not 13 complete independent copies.

A separate early dated witness is held by the British Library: IO SAN 2389b, a 64-folio Sanskrit manuscript in Devanāgarī on paper dated 1525 CE. Its colophon names Anandapura and identifies the copy as written by Devajī. This is an early dated catalogued witness, not a claim that it is the oldest Chāndogya manuscript anywhere.

The Penn entries are mostly dated 1700–1850, with item 721 dated 1880 and item 1213 dated 1836. The catalogue’s general “place” field often reflects its repository in the United States; it does not establish an American place of copying. The British Library colophon gives a much more useful geographic clue, though Anandapura should not be assigned a modern location without further evidence.

Physical evidence shows variation in copying and preservation: item 945 is catalogued as complete in 74 folios, with corrections and marginal additions; item 721 has leaves 39–45 missing and omits the khaṇḍa subdivisions; item 1212 contains chapters 1–5 but not all eight prapāṭhakas.

Printed editions and critical work

Chāndogya was printed in the 19th century, including the Bibliotheca Indica edition (1849); the British Museum’s manuscript catalogue records Böhtlingk’s 1889 critical edition. For a modern scholarly working text, Olivelle’s 1998 edition provides Sanskrit, variants, emendations, and editorial explanations. It is substantially more transparent than a plain Sanskrit reprint, but it does not claim to collate every surviving manuscript worldwide.

The early print history and the current critical-edition status should not be conflated. A printed Sanskrit text is not automatically a modern exhaustive critical edition, and the absence of a documented second major recension does not make every manuscript identical.

Edition references: Olivelle, Oxford University Press · British Museum manuscript catalogue, noting Böhtlingk 1889.

Dating proposals from 15 scholars

Chāndogya is usually placed alongside Bṛhadāraṇyaka in the oldest prose layer. Its relative order varies by author: Hume places it second, while Olivelle notes that some Chāndogya passages may be older than some passages in Bṛhadāraṇyaka. That is a claim about layers, not a secure date for every chapter.

Was it recited before it was written?

Yes, as a Vedic text it was transmitted in an oral school tradition before the surviving manuscript copies. Chāndogya 6.1 depicts Śvetaketu’s formal Vedic study, while 7.1 has Nārada list the Vedas and other disciplines he has learned before asking for deeper knowledge. These are internal depictions of teacher–student learning; alongside the established oral transmission of the Sāmaveda, they support oral priority, though neither passage is a dated record of the first written copy.

Chāndogya’s Sāmavedic setting matters: the text discusses speech, songs, and chants, and is associated with a chanting school. This does not mean every prose paragraph of the Upaniṣad is sung as a sāman. It means that the text belonged to a tradition in which oral delivery and liturgical sound were central.

How many śākhās? The specific lineage documented for this Upaniṣad is Kauthuma/Tāṇḍya. The Sāmaveda has other extant branches, but the sources consulted do not establish that Jaiminīya and Rāṇāyanīya preserved this same Chāndogya Upaniṣad as additional recensions. The defensible answer is one documented primary line, with manuscript-level copying variants.
