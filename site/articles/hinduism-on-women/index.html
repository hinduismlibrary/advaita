1. Introduction

1.1 Preface and Methodology

Purpose

Dharma is one of the most difficult Sanskrit words to translate into English. It refers simultaneously to innate qualities and appropriate duties. It is the ideal way that a thing ought to exist. Insofar as dharma is often discussed, however, it is discussed in the context of the dharmas of specific Varnas, Jatis, Ashramas, roles such as ‘king’,‘teacher’, ‘student’, etc, and the chief object of discussion is the men whom these categories divide. Rarely is the dharma of women, half of the population, ever discussed on its own.

Even when it is discussed, it is discussed mostly in two ways. Firstly, from the lens of Indology, a completely secular and outsider perspective, which seeks to understand Hinduism as a naturally developing social structure which changed and evolved based on human factors. Secondly is through the completely ‘Traditionalist’ perspective, which, though recognising scripture as divine in premise, is practically more focused on auxiliary social practices, ideas of later thinkers and even internalised foreign influence. Rarely, if ever, is the actual statement of the Hindu scriptures themselves engaged on its own.

This paper attempts to do just that, understand the position of the Hindu scriptures themselves, without seeking to project ideas of ‘Later’ or ‘older’ on them, without contextualising them as mere human productions based on human factors, without mixing in external practices and influences, completely on its own terms as divine pramana.

Methodology

As stated, only the statement of scriptures will be considered primary evidence for this analysis. Scripture includes Sruti or Vedas as the supreme section, containing 4 subsections, viz., Samhita, Brahmana, Aranyaka & Upanisad. These very rarely speak of social laws. Besides Sruti is the wide category of ‘Smrti’, the writings of the authoritative sages. Contained within are many sub-categories like Vedangas, Dharmasastras (Social-legal treatises), Itihasas (History) and Puranas (which focus on a broad range of topics and are sometimes considered distinct from smrtis), and a few others. It is these alone which are the sources of Dharma, as they themselves state:

Lord Krsna says in the Gita, 16.23

Names of women

Sanskrit is one of the richest languages in terms of synonyms for a single thing. A synonym is not random but based on a specific quality of the thing it denotes. Fire, for example, while usually called ‘Agni’, is also called ‘Pavaka’ (purifier) as whatever is put in it is burnt and thereby purified.

The Amarakosa is a very famous lexicon which records such synonyms for each thing, arranged by topic in metrical verses. While not itself a scripture, its words almost always have examples of Scriptural use.

For ‘Men’ it contains only two lines, of mostly generic names deriving from ‘descendant of Manu’ (Manava, Manusa, Manusya, Manuja). The women, however, get five lines of highly descriptive names, a testament of how many qualities of women are recognised in scriptures. 2. Marriage

2.1 Introduction and Consent

Importance of marriage

The most important legal and social institution tied to women (and men) is perhaps marriage. Marriage is for this reason its own section in this document. Marriage in Hinduism is not merely a social contract, but a divine obligation with few exceptions for both women and men. The Manusmriti (9.96) describes it as the very purpose of both:

Manusmriti 9.96

"Women were created for the purpose of child-bearing, and men for the purpose of procreation. Hence it is that Religious Rites have been ordained in the Veda as common between the man and his wife.”

2.2 Forms of Marriage

The eight types

The eight types of marriage are summarised in short in the Garuda Purana, 1.95.7-10

Garuda Purana 1.95.7-10

  1. The marriage in which the father invites a man endowed with good qualities and lineage to give his daughter’s hand is called ‘Brahma’.
  2. That in which the daughter’s hand is given to an officiating priest is called ‘Daiva’.
  3. The one in which a pair of cow and bull is accepted as a symbolic gift is called ‘Arsa’.
  4. The one in which the groom himself asks for the daughter’s hand and is given it is called ‘Prajapatya’, a righteous form.
  5. The one in which there is an exchange of money is called ‘Asura’, an inferior form.
  6. The one which is done with mutual agreement of the bride and groom (and none else) is called ‘Gandharva’.
  7. The one in which martial force is used by the groom is called ‘Raksasa’.
  8. The one where the girl is deceived or cheated (by being intoxicated, asleep or lied to) is called ‘Paisaca’.

We often hear only one side of the Scriptural statements on spousal status. We often hear that the husband is called the lord and god for the wife, compared to Narayana and Siva, the only dharma whom she is called on to serve and please.

2.4 Dissolution of Marriage

No matter how perfect the marriage, it has to end in one way or the other. The principle verse on the dissolution of marriage is found in at least five different scriptures, the Parasara smrti (4.30) , the Narada smrti (12.97), Garuda purana (1.107.29), Agni purana (154.6) and the Visnu dharmottara purana (2.87.11), it is as follows :

Narada smrti 12.97; Garuda Purana 1.107.29; Agni Purana 154.6

Remarriage

(II) Has died: self-explanatory.

(II) Has taken renunciation : the husband is generally substantially older than the wife. If he has reached the time for renunciation and wishes to do so, and the wife does not, she may remarry after he renounces.

(IV) If he is impotent : as stated, the very purpose of marriage is primarily procreation. If the husband doesn't fulfill his sexual duties, or if he is infertile, the woman may annul the marriage and remarry.

(V) If he has fallen : “fallen” (Patita) is a technical term in Dharmasastra meaning one who has committed a major sin ( Pataka). In such cases too she may nullify and remarry.

The other Smrtis and sastras may contain content pertaining to other yuga, but the Parasara smrti, itself stated to be meant specifically for Kaliyuga, cannot contain any injunctions “pertaining to other Yugas” (Yugantaravisayah) as claimed by Vidyaranya and others. If a Purana claims anything enjoined by it to be forbidden in Kaliyuga, it would be in direct contradiction with the smrti itself, and must be rejected.

Thereby, to sustain the pramanya of the Aditya purana verse, a reconciliatory approach must be taken. ‘Marriage of one who is already married’ may plausibly be interpreted as referring to the practice of a woman marrying another man while her previous marriage remains valid, i.e., polyandry. This was indeed practiced in previous yugas, the most famous example being Draupadi in the dvapara yuga, and is now forbidden in kaliyuga.

This practice of following the husband in death and being burnt on the same pyre as him, is popularly called Sati, and more properly, Sahagamana. This verse itself has been quoted as being additionally from both Sankha and Angiras smrtis by Vijnanesvara on Yajnavalkya Smrti 1.86 Note that, the option of Sati is given last, first she is told to remarry, if she does not wish to do so, then live as a widow, and only finally is the option for Sahagamana. The mainstream traditional position accepts Sati as a Kamya ( desirable) act, but not even the most conservative of Acaryas consider it obligatory (Naimittika).

3.4 On Purdah

A very commonly accepted idea amongst Hindus, especially in the north and especially in the rural areas, is that women ought to cover their head, or even their face. One may see women of the house in the north rush to drape the ghunghat over their hair and face when a man other than their husband or blood relatives enters the home, or put their Saree’s dupatta over their hair when entering a temple. Even in the shows or films adapting stories from Hindu scriptures, all the good women cover their head, and only the demonic women are shown without such covering.

One may notice, however, that the sastras enjoin women to cover their lower body from the navel down to slightly above the ankles, and their chest, but nothing at all is stated about covering the head or face. In fact, the numerous head and face ornaments shown just above, would all become invisible and thereby useless, if the head or face were covered.

If this practice is not in the scriptures, then where has it come from? Both the word ‘Purdah’, which is a Persian word, and its geographic prevalence, the north of India, which was occupied by Muslim powers for far longer than the south, give us the answer. It is a borrowing of a muslim concept, and not a Hindu Scriptural one. Historian A.S. Altekar writes in his book, ‘the position of women in hindu civilization’ , pages 206-207:

A. S. Altekar, The Position of Women in Hindu Civilization, pp. 206–207

“There are absolutely no traces of any Purdah observed within the family in the Hindu period. The general adoption of the Purdah system by the ruling and aristocratic families of Hindu community is subsequent to the advent of the Muslim rule. It was accepted by Hindu society in imitation of the manners of the conquerors.”

Further he states:

A. S. Altekar, The Position of Women in Hindu Civilization, pp. 206–207

“This happened almost universally in northern India, where the Muslim rule and culture were in ascendancy for a long time. In the Deccan, the Muslim influence was superficial, and so the Purdah system got no footing in the Hindu society there”

Not only is the practice itself absent from Hindu scriptures and pre-islamic Hindu society as a whole, but even the rationale for it is explicitly rejected. In Islam, purdah is done to protect the women of the house from the male gaze. However, lord Rama himself states :

3.6 Education

Education in the Vedas

The discussion of women’s education in the Hindu scriptures must be separated into two separate discussions: (i) education in the Vedas and (ii) education in all the topics other than the Vedas. These are very different.

The general restriction of women from Vedic study

The Vedas are the most sacred scriptures of Hinduism. They are not a book that is bought and read; rather, they are heard (hence the name Sruti, “that which is heard”) from a guru. This hearing is not simple: it begins with initiation through the sacred thread ceremony, which is performed only for the first three Varnas, not the Sudra. Afterward, the child lives with the Guru, serves him, and follows hundreds, if not thousands, of rules. During this time, he must beg for alms (Bhiksatana). Only then may he learn the Vedic mantras and their use in yajnas (fire rituals) from the guru.

In the case of general women, however, the Manusmriti(2.67) states:

Manusmriti 2.67

“For women, marriage is equivalent to the Vedic initiation rite, serving the husband equivalent to living with (and serving) the guru, and the performance of household duties equivalent to the performance of Vedic fire rituals.”

Therefore women in general are not enjoined to study the Vedas. It is for them (and the Sudras and uninitiated) that the other Vedic literatures, the itihasas and puranas, called the fifth Veda, have been composed.

3.7 Religion and Moksha

Religious activities

In truth, several forms of religious activity have already been discussed. The household and marital duties are the highest religious duties of the women, even by their performance alone she can attain the highest good, all else is optional. Besides them we discussed purity, both external as well as internal, the latter consisting of meditation on God. The study of scriptures (Svadhayaya) is also a form of religious activity. Now let us see all the ones that remain, viz, (I) the obligatory occasional rituals (Samskaras), (II) Fasting (Vrata), (III) pilgrimage (Tirtha), (IV) chanting, (V) making offerings to the gods and (VI) charity (Dana).

Samskaras

There are a total of 16 obligatory occasional (Naimittika) rituals in Hindu scriptures. All 16 are performed for men of the three higher Varnas (and Brahmavadinis) using Vedic mantras. For general women, however, only ten are performed, without Vedic mantras. The excluded rites are those related to study of the Vedas (sacred thread, first alms, graduation, etc.), for which marriage is stated to be the equivalent. The Vyasa smrti states:

Vyasa Smriti

“The samskaras are reckoned to be sixteen. Of these, nine, ending with ‘Karnavedhana’ are to be performed for women, without mantras. Marriage is performed for them with mantras, except the Sudras for whom it is without, thus totalling ten.”

4. Addendum

4.1 Conclusion

Thus, almost every topic of a woman’s life—legal, social, and religious—has been explained, with scripture as the primary divine guide, without any historicisation, and with the Acaryas as supporting evidence. All the topics discussed may now be summarised in the order of a woman’s life.

When she is born, one ought to rejoice, for Devi herself has come to his home, and the daughter is equal or superior to the son. Her samskaras should be performed, and she should be given an education. If she is exceptional in religious matters, her sacred thread initiation should be performed, and she should be taught the Vedas as well, but not otherwise. When she reaches the appropriate age, she should be married in one of the higher forms of marriage, with her consent and without exchange of money, to a suitable husband. If a suitable husband is not found, she may remain unmarried, though marriage is superior for paying the rnas and entering the Grhastha stage.

When she is married, she should be loved, served, provided for and protected by her husband, for whom she is the supreme refuge. She too should serve him, perform the household work like cooking, cleaning, etc, and advise him on matters of family and business. When she has children she is more venerable to them than their father or teacher. If her husband has gone away for a long time, or is impotent, or has taken renunciation, or has fallen, then she may leave him and remarry. If he dies she may live as a widow or remarry, she should not perform sahagamana.

Whatever money she receives is her own and may not to be taken by anyone, or else they will be legally punished. If her husband has gone away without making arrangements for her she may work in respectable industries.

She receives inheritance from her father if there are no sons, or she is unmarried. She receives inheritance from her husband, equal to a son, or the entirety of it if there are no children. Upon her own death her property is inherited by her daughters, sons if no daughters or husband/parents depending on type of marriage.

She is to be guarded—not dominated— by her father in childhood, husband in youth and sons in old age. If not, by the government/king. She is inviolable. Any verbal, physical or sexual offence against her is a more serious crime than against a man. Abandonment and adultery are punishable offenses.

She should wear clothing such that it covers her chest, and lower body from navel to ankle. If she goes out, she should take an uttariya over arms and/or shoulder. All else is optional. She should wear the marital ornaments always, and other ornaments as per her wish. There is no fault in her going out in public.

Menstruation is the unparalleled means of purification for the women, like the flow of the river. Though it leaves them temporarily impure during that time, and they should cease religious household activities during it. At childbirth both she and her husband are impure for ten days. She should maintain internal purity by meditating on God with her husband, and external by bathing.

She may perform religious activities like fasting, pilgrimage, chanting, making offerings, charity, etc. though fulfilling her household duties itself is also sufficient. By performing bhakti of her preferred deity she attains Moksa.

Some may call the scriptural views ‘Progressive’, though this is a very bad label, because progress implies gradual improvement from a less than ideal state. The scriptures, however, are eternal. The only issue is the misinterpretation and addition. Thus what is to be done is not ‘progress’ but ‘purification’ to arrive at the original scriptural view.
