MMK 12.10

na kevalaṃ hi duḥkhasya cāturvidhyaṃ na vidyate
sarveṣāṃ bāhyānām api bhāvānāṃ cāturvidhyaṃ na vidyate || 10 ||

Not only is the fourfoldness of suffering not found; the fourfoldness of all external things is not found.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

na kevalaṃ hi duḥkhasya cāturvidhyaṃ na vidyate | sarveṣāṃ bāhyānām api bhāvānāṃ cāturvidhyaṃ na vidyate || 10 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

And explaining that just as suffering, when examined in four ways, does not exist, so too external entities, such as seed, sprout, pot, cloth, and the like, are to be understood, he says:

“Not only is the fourfoldness of suffering not found; the fourfoldness of all external things too is not found.”

Everything is to be joined as before. If, indeed, the fourfoldness of suffering and the like is impossible, then by which of these modes is their establishment now? The response: If these sufferings and the like existed by their own nature, then their establishment would necessarily be through one of these four modes. But they do not exist; therefore it is understood that suffering and the like do not exist by their own nature. Or, if the conventionality of suffering and the like, whose own existence is acquired through mere error, is sought, then, by the method set out in the chapter on the examination of action and agent, through rejection of the four positions stated, establishment through dependent arising in the sense of mere conditionality is to be accepted. As has been said:

“Suffering is held by logicians to be made by itself, made by another, made by both, or without a cause; but by you it was said to arise in dependence.”

And it is also said by the Blessed One in the Āryopāliparipṛcchā:

“Conventionally, the Victor has spoken of the Dharma as conditioned and unconditioned: see it. Here in reality there is no self or person; this is the characteristic of the whole world.

“Karmas, dark and bright, do not perish; having done them, they are to be experienced by oneself. There is no transmigration of the fruit of action, and one does not experience it without a cause.

“All existences are false, of their own nature empty, empty, hollow, and like foam. Always like illusion and mirage, all are empty; they are taught conventionally and are separate.

“Just as an echo arises in dependence upon rocks, caves, mountain-forts, and rivers, so understand all conditioned things thus: the whole world is like illusion and mirage.”

And so forth.

Thus ends the twelfth chapter, called “Examination of Suffering,” in the Madhyamaka commentary Prasannapadā composed by Ācārya Candrakīrti.
