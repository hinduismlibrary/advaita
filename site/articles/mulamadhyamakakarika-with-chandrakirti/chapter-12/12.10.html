MMK 12.10

na = not; kevalam = only; hi = indeed; duḥkhasya = of suffering; cāturvidhyam = fourfold nature; na vidyate = is not found; sarveṣām = of all; api = also; bāhyānām = external; bhāvānām = entities; cāturvidhyam = fourfold nature; na vidyate = is not found

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

na kevalaṃ hi duḥkhasya cāturvidhyaṃ na vidyate | sarveṣāṃ bāhyānām api bhāvānāṃ cāturvidhyaṃ na vidyate || 10 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

The analysis is not limited to suffering. External things such as a seed and sprout, or a pot and cloth, likewise cannot be established as arising from themselves, from another, from both, or without a cause. If they existed by their own nature, one of those four modes would have to hold; none does.

This does not erase conventional experience. When suffering and other things are examined as conventional appearances shaped by confusion, their dependent arising is explained through conditions and designation. The four intrinsic modes of production are rejected, while causal and linguistic conventions continue to function dependently. The teaching denies inherent existence, not the conventional account of causes and results.

Candrakīrti closes with scriptural praise of this distinction. The Buddha teaches phenomena conventionally, yet no person or being with an intrinsic defining mark is found. Wholesome and unwholesome actions are experienced without a self that transmigrates and without a break in causal results; experience is not causeless. All conditioned things are empty, insubstantial, and like foam or mirage. They are taught conventionally while being free of intrinsic nature.
