MMK 2.17

Investigation of Coming and Going

MMK 2.17

gamanaṃ saṃpravṛttiśca nivṛttiśca gateḥ samā || 17 ||

Going, its commencement, and its cessation are the same as movement.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

gamanaṃ saṃpravṛttiśca nivṛttiśca gateḥ samā || 17 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Here someone says: “Going exists because its cessation exists. Here, a goer who is ceasing from going begins to stand; if going did not exist, he would not cease from it.” In reply: Going would exist if its cessation existed; but it does not. Why? Because:

“One does not stand on what is being traversed, nor on what has been traversed or what has not been traversed.”

In this case, a goer does not cease from a path that has been traversed, because there is no going there; nor from what has not been traversed, for the same reason; nor from what is being traversed, because that is not apprehended and because the act of going is absent. Therefore, there is no cessation of going.

Someone says: “If going is unreal because standing, the opposite of going, does not exist, then we shall establish standing in order to establish going; when standing is established, going is established. Therefore, standing indeed exists because its opposite exists. For going is the opposite of standing; it exists, and therefore standing also exists because its opposite exists.” This too is unreasonable. Why? Because:

“Going, its commencement, and its cessation are the same as movement.”

Here, the meaning is that the going described in order to establish standing is the same as movement, and is equal to the refutation of movement. Just as the refutation was stated of standing, which had been taken as a reason for establishing movement, by the verse beginning “First, the goer does not stand,” so here too the refutation is to be stated of going, which has been taken as a reason for establishing standing, by changing the order of the two verses and beginning “First, the one who stands does not go.” Therefore, there is no going; because it does not exist, standing, its opposite, does not exist either. Thus, for now, going is to be rejected as equal to movement.

Now someone might say: “Standing exists because its beginning exists. Here, standing is begun by bringing going to an end; how could standing not exist?” In reply: The beginning of standing too should be said to be the same as that of going. Just as the beginning of going was previously refuted by [the verse] “On what has been traversed, going is not begun,” so here too:

“On what has stood, one does not begin to stand; on what has not stood, one does not begin to stand. One does not begin to stand on what is being stood upon. Where is one to begin to stand?”

Through the three-verse cycle beginning with this, the commencement of standing too is the same as that of going. The cessation of standing too is to be refuted as the same as the cessation of going. Just as, in denying going, the refutation was stated by “One does not stand on what is being traversed, nor on what has been or has not been traversed,” so, in denying standing, the refutation is equal to that of going by [saying] “One does not go on what is being stood upon, nor on what has been or has not been stood upon.” Therefore, standing does not exist. Since it does not exist, how can those who assert that standing exists as the opposite of going establish going?
