MMK 1.14

tasmān na pratyayamayaṃ nāpratyayamayaṃ phalam
saṃvidyate phalābhāvāt pratyayāpratyayāḥ kutaḥ

Therefore no effect is found that is made of conditions or of non-conditions. In the absence of an effect, whence conditions or non-conditions?

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

'Therefore a result consisting of conditions is not found.' One might then say: 'Let the result not consist of conditions.' [Nāgārjuna replies:] 'Nor is a result not consisting of conditions found.' That is, if cloth is not composed of threads, how could it be composed of vīraṇa grass?

Objection: 'Let there be no result; nevertheless the fixed distinction between conditions and non-conditions exists. You yourself say, “If a nonexistent result proceeds from conditions, why does it not also proceed from non-conditions?” But without results such as cloth and mat, threads and vīraṇa grass would not reasonably be conditions [for those results]. Therefore the result does exist.'

Reply: There would be a result if there were conditions and non-conditions. For when a result exists, one can say, 'These are its conditions; those are not its conditions.' But under examination the result does not exist, as [Nāgārjuna] states: 'In the absence of a result, whence conditions and non-conditions?' The expression is a compound referring to both conditions and non-conditions.

Therefore things do not arise through their own intrinsic nature. As it is said in the noble Ratnākara Sūtra:

“Knowledge of emptiness is not found anywhere, just as a bird's footprint is not found in the sky. What does not exist anywhere through its own nature will never exist through an independently other cause.”

“When a thing's own nature cannot be found at all, how can something without nature become a condition that is other? How could that which lacks intrinsic nature produce another thing? Such is the reasoning taught by the Sugata.”

“All phenomena are unmoving and stand firm, unchanged, undisturbed and peaceful. Like a path through the sky they are unborn, while the world, not knowing this, becomes confused about them.”

“Just as the mountains and rocky peaks are immovable, phenomena likewise are forever unmoving. They neither perish nor arise. Thus their nature was taught by the Victor.”

Likewise:

“The phenomenon that is neither born nor produced, that neither passes away nor ages, is taught by the Victor, the lion among men, and he establishes hundreds of beings therein.”

“No intrinsic nature of it exists anywhere, and no other-nature is found in relation to anything. It is found neither internally nor externally; the Lord establishes beings in this teaching.”

“The Sugata taught a peaceful course, yet no going whatsoever is apprehended. Still you speak there as one liberated from going: 'Having been released, I release many beings.'” and so forth.

Thus ends the first chapter, entitled Examination of Conditions, in Candrakīrti's Madhyamaka commentary called the Prasannapadā.
