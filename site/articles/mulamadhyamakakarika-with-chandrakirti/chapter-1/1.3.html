MMK 1.3

na hi svabhāvo bhāvānāṃ pratyayādiṣu vidyate
avidyamāne svabhāve parabhāvo na vidyate

The intrinsic nature (svabhāva) of entities is not found in conditions and the like. When intrinsic nature is absent, other-nature (parabhāva) is not found.

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

If the existence of things as effects were present before their production somewhere else—in the assembled or separated causal and other conditions, in conditions taken both separately and together, or anywhere else—then production from those conditions could occur. But it is not so that they exist before their production. If they did, they would be apprehended, and production would be purposeless. Therefore the intrinsic nature of things does not exist in conditions and the like. When intrinsic nature is absent, other-nature too is absent. Becoming, existence, is production; production from others is other-being, and that is not found. Therefore it is unreasonable to claim that things arise from conditions that are other than them.

Alternatively, the intrinsic nature of effects such as sprouts is not present in conditions such as seeds while those conditions remain in their unchanged form. For otherwise the consequence would be that [the effect] is without a cause.

Further, in dependence upon what are the conditions and the like said to be other? Otherness is relative between two existing persons, such as Maitra and Upagupta. But the seed and the sprout do not exist simultaneously in that way. Thus, when effects lack intrinsic nature, the otherness of seeds and other conditions does not exist. Since there is no ground for calling them ‘other,’ there is no production from another. Hence the opponent does not understand the intention of scripture. The Tathāgatas do not utter statements contrary to reasoning, and the intention of scripture was explained previously.

With the advocate of arising from conditions refuted in this way, an advocate of arising from an action thinks: 'The eye, forms and other conditions do not directly produce consciousness. They are called conditions because they bring about an activity that produces consciousness. That activity then produces consciousness. Thus, the activity of producing consciousness, which possesses conditions, produces consciousness, not the conditions themselves—just as the action of cooking produces cooked rice.' We reply:
