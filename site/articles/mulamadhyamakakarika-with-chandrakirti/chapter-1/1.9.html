MMK 1.9

If phenomena are not born, it is invalid for there to be cessation. Therefore, an immediate [condition] is unreasonable. What, having ceased, can also be a condition?

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

In the final half of the stanza, the order of the quarters must be transposed, and the particle ca belongs in a different position, with 'after cessation' (niruddhe ca). Thus the reading in its intended order is: 'And after [something] has ceased, what condition is there? Therefore an immediately preceding [condition] is not tenable.' The wording was arranged otherwise for the sake of metrical form.

Here the definition of an immediately preceding condition is that the cessation of the cause immediately before [an effect] is a condition for the arising of the effect. This is examined: when phenomena that are effects, such as sprouts, have not arisen, the cessation of their causes, such as seeds, is not tenable. In that case, because the cause has not ceased, what immediately preceding condition does the sprout have?

But if the cessation of the seed is accepted even when the effect has not arisen, then, after the seed has ceased and become nonexistent, what condition is there for the sprout? Or what is the condition for the cessation of the seed? [The text] indicates that both would be without causes by saying: 'And when [it] has ceased, what condition is there?' The particle ca refers back to 'not arisen.' Thus, even if the cessation of seeds and the like is accepted before the sprout arises, both would be without a cause. Therefore an immediately preceding condition is not tenable.

Alternatively, the beginning of the work has already refuted arising with the words 'Not from itself, not from another' and so forth. With this in view, [Nāgārjuna] says, 'When phenomena have not arisen, cessation is not tenable; therefore an immediately preceding condition is not tenable.' The other half-verse, 'And when [it] has ceased, what condition is there?' has the same explanation as before.
