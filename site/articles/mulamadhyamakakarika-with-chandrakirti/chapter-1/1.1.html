MMK 1.1

na svato nāpi parato na dvābhyāṃ nāpy ahetutaḥ
utpannā jātu vidyante bhāvāḥ kva cana ke cana

No entities whatsoever, anywhere or at any time, are found to arise from themselves, from another, from both, or without a cause.

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Here the word jātu means “at any time.” The word kvacana expresses a location and is synonymous with kvacit (“somewhere”). The word kecana expresses what is located there and is synonymous with kecit (“some things”). The words are therefore to be connected as follows: “At no time do any entities whatsoever, anywhere, exist as having arisen from themselves.” The three other propositions are to be construed in the same way.

Objection: When it is asserted definitely, “They do not arise from themselves,” the unwanted result follows that they arise from another. Reply: It does not follow, because the intended negation is a non-implicative negation (prasajya-pratiṣedha), since arising from another is also to be refuted. The reasoning by which arising from oneself is impossible is to be understood through passages in the Madhyamakāvatāra and elsewhere, such as: “For that reason there is no benefit whatsoever in its coming into being; nor is it reasonable for what is already born to be born again.”

Ācārya Buddhapālita, however, says: “Things do not arise from themselves, because their production would be purposeless and because an unacceptable further consequence would follow. For things that already exist by their own identity have no purpose in being produced again. And if something were produced even though it already existed, there would never be a time when it was not produced.”

Here some make the following criticism: “This is improper because no reason and example have been stated, and because defects alleged by the other party have not been removed. Moreover, since this is a statement of consequence, when the intended conclusion is reversed, the opposite conclusion and its supporting property become apparent. Thus there would be production from another, the usefulness of producing what does not yet exist, and the prevention of repeated production. This would conflict with the accepted doctrine.”

We see that this entire criticism is untenable. How so? First, the statement that a reason and example have not been given is untenable. Why? For when an opponent accepts arising from oneself, he is asked: “Does ‘from oneself’ mean that the very thing itself is both the cause and the thing produced?” We see no purpose in producing again what already exists, and we see that an infinite regress would follow. Nor do you wish to admit renewed production of what is already produced, and an infinite regress is also unacceptable to you. Therefore your position lacks a supporting explanation and contradicts what you yourself accept.

Once this challenge has been put, why should the opponent not acknowledge it? What use would there be in introducing a reason and an example? If the opponent would not withdraw his position even when its contradiction with his own commitments is pointed out, then, shamelessly, he would not withdraw it even before a reason and an example. Nor is our dispute with a madman.

Thus he merely displays his fondness for inference by introducing a formal inference where it has no place. It is not proper for a Mādhyamika to formulate an independent inference, because he accepts no separate doctrinal position. As Āryadeva has said: “For one who has no position of existence, nonexistence, or both existence and nonexistence, an objection cannot be stated even after a long time.”

It is also said in the Vigrahavyāvartanī: “If I had any thesis, from that alone I would incur a fault. But I have no thesis; therefore I have no fault at all.” And: “If I apprehended anything that I could establish or refute by objects such as perception, I could be reproached; but because there is no such thing, I cannot be reproached.”

When the Mādhyamika does not propound an independent inference in this way, from where would there arise an independent thesis such as “The internal sense bases do not arise from themselves,” in response to which the Sāṃkhyas might object: “What does this thesis mean? Does ‘from itself’ mean from its own nature as an effect, or from its own nature as a cause?”

And what follows from those alternatives? If it means from itself as an effect, it would establish what is already established. If it means from itself as a cause, the thesis would have a contradictory meaning, since it would be claiming the production of everything that is subject to production while already existing in the form of its cause. But where do we have the reason “because it exists,” which could lead to either the fault of proving what is already proved or the fault of contradiction, and for the avoidance of which we would have to exert ourselves? Since these faults alleged by the opponent do not arise for us, Buddhapālita had no need to state a remedy for them.

One might nevertheless say: “Because the Mādhyamikas' theses, reasons and examples are unestablished, and because they do not formulate independent inferences, perhaps they should not establish the proposition denying self-production, nor should they refute the opponent's thesis with an inference established by both sides. Yet, when they challenge the opponent's thesis by showing that it contradicts the opponent's own inference, the statement of the thesis, reason and example on the opponent's side must be free from faults. Since they have not stated these and have not removed their defects, the same criticism remains.”

We reply: this is not so. Why? Whoever asserts a proposition and wishes to produce certainty in others, just as he has certainty himself, must teach others the very reasoning by which he has understood his proposition. The rule, therefore, is that the proponent himself must provide the demonstration of the proposition that he accepts.

But the opponent does not provide an argument grounded in a reason and example that can establish his assertion; instead, he merely assumes the very proposition he is supposed to demonstrate. Because he accepts a position unsupported by an explanation, he merely deceives himself and is unable to produce conviction in others. This is the clearest fault in his position: the inability to establish his own proposition. What purpose is served here by separately declaring that it conflicts with an inference?

Yet if it is absolutely necessary to expose the fault of conflict with an inference, Buddhapālita has already done so. How? By saying, “Things do not arise from themselves, because that production would be purposeless.” Here the expression “that” refers to something already existing by its own identity. Why? Because the brief statement is explained by his next words: “There is no purpose in again producing things that already exist by their own identity.”

That very sentence contains an example of similarity accepted by the opposing side, associated with the property to be demonstrated and the property that establishes it. “Existing by its own identity” refers to the reason; “because production would be purposeless” refers to the property to be established.

For example, consider the inference: “Sound is impermanent because it is made.” What is made is observed to be impermanent, as with a pot. And sound is made; therefore sound is impermanent because it is made. Here being made is the reason made explicit by its application.

In the present case it is similar: “Things do not arise from themselves, because there is no purpose in producing again what already exists by its own identity.” We observe that something already present before us, such as a pot, has no need to be produced anew. Therefore, if you consider a pot or another thing to exist already by its own identity even in the state of a lump of clay, that independently existing thing is not produced. Thus the conflict with the Sāṃkhya's own inference has indeed been shown, through the reason “existing by its own identity,” which excludes further production without exception. Why, then, is it said that the argument is invalid because it lacks a reason and an example?

Not only is the charge that a reason and example were omitted inapplicable; the charge that the opponent's criticisms were not answered is also inapplicable. How? The Sāṃkhyas do not maintain that a pot standing before them in its manifested form is manifested again. Precisely this is introduced here as an example, since it is established. The unmanifested form, existing as a capacity, is what is to be examined with regard to the denial of production. How, then, could there be the suspected fault of proving what is already established, or the suspicion that the reason proves the opposite? Therefore, even when the contradiction with their own inference is pointed out, the previously described defects are absent. It must be understood that this criticism is without coherence.

Because the expression “a pot and so forth” is intended to encompass all things that are yet to be produced, there is no inconclusiveness on account of objects such as cloth. Or another formulation may be given: “Things other than puruṣa, according to proponents of arising from themselves, do not arise from themselves, because they exist by their own nature, like puruṣa.” That is an example that may be advanced.

Although someone who professes mere manifestation may not be refuted by a denial of production in the usual sense, the word “production” is applied to manifestation because, before and after, there is the similarity of nonapprehension and apprehension; hence the denial is not without force even in their case.

Objection: “Without an explicit declaration of the intended meaning, how can one obtain such a detailed analysis?” Reply: Statements have been composed which, because they are rich in meaning, express the sense described above in compressed form. When they are explained, they yield the intended meaning. Nothing has been introduced that was not contained in them.

The inverted meaning of the consequential argument belongs to the opponent alone, not to us, since we have no thesis of our own; consequently, no contradiction with our doctrine arises. And as many faults as fall upon the opponent through reversing the consequence are precisely what we accept as faults in his position. How could the wording of Ācārya Buddhapālita, a follower of Ācārya Nāgārjuna's uncorrupted view, leave an opening for the opponent to exploit?

When a proponent of things devoid of intrinsic nature presents a consequence to a proponent of things possessing intrinsic nature, how could he become committed to the opposite of the consequence? Words do not control the speaker as officers bearing staffs and nooses would control a person; rather, insofar as they can, they follow the speaker's intention. Since the sole purpose of presenting the consequence is the rejection of the opponent's thesis, no commitment to the inverted proposition follows.

The master himself repeatedly rejects opposing positions by drawing out their consequences. For example: “Space does not exist prior to the defining characteristic of space; for if it existed before its defining characteristic, it would follow that it was without a defining characteristic.” Likewise: “If form were free from its cause of form, form would follow as causeless; yet nowhere does there exist anything without a cause.” And: “Nirvāṇa is not an existent thing characterized by aging and death; for if it were existent, [those characteristics] would follow, since no existent thing is without aging and death.”

If it is imagined that the master's statements, because they are meaningful, can accomplish many purposes, why should Buddhapālita's explanations not be understood in exactly the same way? If one maintains that a commentator is obliged to set out fully expanded formal inferences, that is also not established; even the master, when composing the commentary to the Vigrahavyāvartanī, did not set out such formal inferential statements.

Further, the logical analyst, although having adopted the Madhyamaka view, wishes merely to display his extraordinary skill in the treatises on reasoning by stating an independent formal inference. This is observed to be a source of a great many faults. In what way? First, he has said: “Here the formal statement of inference is: the internal sense bases are not produced from themselves ultimately, because they exist, like consciousness.” Why is the qualification “ultimately” introduced?

If he answers that production accepted through ordinary worldly convention is not being denied, and that denying it would contradict what has already been accepted, this is not reasonable. Even conventionally, production from oneself is not accepted. Thus it is said in a sūtra: “This sprout, arising with a seed as its cause, is not produced by itself, nor produced by another, nor produced by both, nor arisen without a cause; it has not arisen from Īśvara, time, atoms, primordial nature, or intrinsic nature.”

Likewise: “Just as a sprout comes from an existing seed, that sprout is not the seed itself. Nor is it other than the seed, nor is it that very seed. Thus the nature of things is neither annihilation nor permanence.” Here too it will be said: “Whatever comes to be through dependence is not exactly that; nor is it something different from that. Therefore it is neither annihilated nor permanent.”

If the qualification is supposed to be made in relation to the opponent's doctrine, that too is not reasonable, because the opponent's manner of establishing things is not accepted even conventionally. Those non-Buddhist teachers who have fallen away from an accurate understanding of the two truths can quite properly be refuted in both respects. Thus the qualification is not justified even with reference to the opposing doctrine.

Nor do worldly people accept production from oneself, so that the qualification would serve any purpose in relation to them. Ordinary people, without entering into an inquiry about production from oneself, another and so forth, merely accept that an effect comes from a cause. The master too establishes this. Accordingly, the qualification is certainly useless in every way.

Moreover, if that qualification is introduced in order to avoid denying production conventionally, then there would be a defect in the subject of the inference because its basis is not established for oneself, or a defect in the reason because its locus is not established. For the sense bases such as the eye are not accepted as arising from themselves ultimately.

If he says that the eye and so forth exist conventionally, and therefore there is no defect, we ask: what does “ultimately” qualify? Suppose he answers that, since what is denied is the ultimate production of conventionally existent eyes and other sense bases, “ultimately” qualifies the denial of production. In that case he ought to state precisely this: “Conventionally existent eyes and other sense bases are not produced ultimately.” But he does not state it so. And even if he did, the other side accepts eyes and the like as existing substantially, and does not accept them as existing through mere designation. Thus the subject would not be established for the opponent. Therefore this explanation is unreasonable.

One might say: “Just as in ‘sound is impermanent,’ the property and the bearer of the property are taken in general, without specifying either of them, since specifying them would make the exchange of inference and inferable object impossible, so too here we shall take just the subject without qualifications.”

For example, if sound consisting of the four great elements is intended, that subject is not established for the opponent. If sound as a quality of space is intended, that subject is not established for the Buddhist himself. Similarly, when the Vaiśeṣika asserts impermanence of sound, if he takes sound as a product it is not established for the other side; if he takes it as something manifested, it is not established for his own side. And the destruction of sound, according to the possible alternatives, if it has a cause, is not established for the Buddhist; if causeless, it is not established for the opponent. Thus, in that example, only the general property and the general bearer of the property are taken. The proposal is to do likewise here.

That is not so. For as soon as the absence of production is adopted here as the property to be proved, the disappearance of the bearer of that property, whose reality was obtained only through error, is thereby acknowledged. Error and freedom from error are different. When through error an unreal thing is perceived as real, as hairs appear to someone suffering from an eye disorder, how could even the smallest part of a truly existent object then be apprehended?

And when, through freedom from error, the unreal thing is not superimposed, as hairs are not superimposed by one without the eye disorder, how could even the smallest part of an unreal object then be apprehended, such that conventional existence would be present at that time? This is precisely why the revered master said: “If I were to apprehend some thing to establish or to refute, by perception and other objects, I could be reproached; but because there is no such thing for me, I cannot be reproached.”

Because error and freedom from error are different in this manner, the erroneous cannot be present in the undeceived state of those who know. Where then is the conventionally existent eye that could serve as the bearer of the property? Thus the defect of a thesis with an unestablished basis, or a reason with an unestablished locus, is not removed. This is no solution.

Nor is the comparison with the example similar. There, sound in general and impermanence in general, without any intended particular qualification, exist for both parties. But an eye taken in general is not accepted conventionally by both those who teach emptiness and those who teach non-emptiness, and certainly not ultimately. Therefore the example is not comparable.

The same method used to demonstrate the fault of an unestablished basis in the subject of the inference must be applied to demonstrating that the proposed reason “because it exists” also has an unestablished subject.

That this is indeed so is admitted by the logician himself, when he accepts the point set out above. He gives a formal proof ascribed to the other side: “There exist causes and other factors producing the internal sense bases, because the Tathāgata has taught them. Whatever the Tathāgata has taught as existing is so, just as peaceful nirvāṇa.”

He himself states this criticism of that proof proposed by another: “What exactly do you intend by the reason here? That they have been taught in this way by the Tathāgata conventionally, or ultimately? If conventionally, the reason is not established from your own side. If ultimately, since a phenomenon that is existent, nonexistent, or both existent and nonexistent is not produced, how could a producer or cause be possible in these circumstances?” The statement means that such a factor is no productive cause.

Therefore, because being-produced and producing are not established ultimately, the proposed reason is either unestablished or contradictory. And since he himself accepts by this manner of reasoning that the reason is not established, all his own inferences too are invalidated whenever they set out reasons based on properties of things whose status he has not established independently.

For example: “The internal sense bases are not produced ultimately from those other conditions, because the conditions are other, as in the case of cloth.” Or: “The conditions intended as other are not apprehended as producers of the internal sense bases, such as the eye, ultimately, because they are other, like threads and similar things.” Here 'being other' and the like are unestablished from the proponent's own position.

Likewise, when he wishes to point out that another's reason is not established—“Internal phenomena are produced, because they occasion distinct usage referring to them”—he says: “If one seeks to prove that arising, going and so forth truly exist for a concentrated yogin who sees things as they are with the eye of wisdom, then the reason ‘because of such distinct usage’ is not established; going also is denied through the very denial of arising.”

The same observation must therefore be applied to his own proof, “Ultimately a path not yet traversed is not traversed, because it is a path, like a path already traversed”: its reason 'being a path' is unestablished from his own side. Likewise, in such proofs as “Ultimately the eye does not see a form resembling it, because it is the faculty of sight, like a form not resembling it,” and “The eye does not see form, because it is material, like form itself,” and “Earth is not hard by intrinsic nature, because it is an element, like wind,” the reasons and other components have to be understood as unestablished from his own position.

The reason “because it exists” is also inconclusive from the opponent's position. Does it mean that, because they exist like consciousness, the internal sense bases do not arise from themselves? Or that, because they exist like a pot, they do arise from themselves? If one says the reason is not inconclusive because pots and similar things are on the same footing as the subject to be established, the answer is no: the formal inference was not stated that way.

Objection: “Just as objections have been made against another's inferences, the same objections would apply to your own inferences: an unestablished subject, an unestablished reason, and so on. Since what is a fault for both parties cannot be used to criticize only one, all these objections turn out to be improper.”

Reply: Such a fault arises for those who set forth an independent inference. We do not employ independent inferences, because our inferences have as their result the rejection of an opponent's thesis. For instance, an opponent accepts that the eye sees. He is refuted by an inference established for him: you accept that the eye does not see itself, and you accept an invariable relation between seeing something else and seeing. Therefore wherever there is no seeing of oneself, there is no seeing of another either, just as in the case of a pot. The eye does not see itself, so it also does not see another thing. Thus seeing other things, such as blue, is contradicted by non-seeing of oneself through an inference the opponent himself accepts.

That alone is what our inferences bring to light. How then can the aforementioned faults enter our position, such that there would be equality of fault?

Question: Can an inference established by just one of the two parties defeat an inference? Yes: it can do so through a reason established by the person being refuted, not merely through one established by the other party, as can be seen in ordinary practice.

Sometimes in the world a party obtains victory or defeat through the testimony of a witness accepted as authoritative by the plaintiff and defendant; at other times through his own words. But through the words of the other party alone he obtains neither victory nor defeat. What holds in the world holds in reasoning also, since ordinary forms of conduct are what the treatises on reasoning deal with.

That is why some have said: “An inference is not refuted through the force of what is established only by the opponent, since the very position established by the opponent is what one intends to reject.” Even one who holds that only a proposition determined by both parties is a valid means of knowledge or refutation—not one doubtful or established for a single party—must nevertheless, if following the practices of the world, accept the method described here for inference.

So too, scripture need not be accepted by both parties for it to refute; it can refute even when established for only one. And in an inference for one's own understanding, what is established for oneself is invariably weightier than what is established for both. For this reason the statement of formal rules of reasoning is not indispensable: the Buddhas benefit disciples who do not understand through the reasoning established for each of them. Enough of this digression; we shall explain the subject at hand.

Things do not arise from another either, because the other is absent. This will be established in the passage: “The intrinsic nature of things is not found in conditions and the like.” Thus, simply because there is no other established as such, they do not arise from another.

Moreover, as the Madhyamakāvatāra says: “If a thing truly were other in dependence upon another, then deep darkness would arise from a flame. Indeed everything would arise from everything, because whatever does not produce something has exactly the same otherness as that which supposedly does produce it.” By arguments such as these, the rejection of production from another is to be understood.

Ācārya Buddhapālita explains: “Things do not arise from another, because the consequence would be that everything could arise from everything.” Ācārya Bhāviveka criticizes this: “Since it is a statement of consequence, when the relationship between what is to be proved and its proof is reversed, the result is that things arise from themselves, from both, or without a cause, because particular things arise from particular things. This contradicts the original thesis.” Otherwise, he says, the appeal to everything arising from everything would fall within a criticism of the proof, making the argument incoherent.

This too is incoherent. The point has already been established earlier, including that a consequential refutation of another's asserted thesis does not commit the Mādhyamika to the inverted proposition. The criticism has no force, and we shall not exert ourselves again over it.

Things do not arise from both either, because the faults stated for both positions would result and because each individually is incapable of producing. For it will be said: “If suffering were made by both, it would have to be made by each individually.”

Nor do things arise without a cause, for then the fault would follow that, “In the absence of a cause, effect and cause are not found.” Moreover, “If the world were devoid of causes, it could not be apprehended, just as the color and fragrance of a sky-flower.”

Ācārya Buddhapālita says: “Things do not arise without a cause, for then the consequence would be that everything arises always and everywhere.” Here Ācārya Bhāviveka criticizes him, saying that if one reverses the statement of the consequence, it would mean that things arise from causes at particular times and places, since particular things arise from particular things and purposeful undertakings succeed. Such an explanation, he argues, is unreasonable because of faults already given. But others answer that this criticism is itself unreasonable in light of the reply already made above.

If the denial of causeless arising is also supposed to include Īśvara and similar proposed causes, that special provision is unnecessary. According to the positions that accept them, Īśvara and the like are already included under production from oneself, another, or both.

Therefore it has been demonstrated that there is no production. Because production is impossible in this sense, dependent arising distinguished by non-production and the other qualifications is established.

Objection: “If you thus establish dependent arising as distinguished by non-production and the like, what of the Blessed One's statement that formations have ignorance as condition, and that through the cessation of ignorance the formations cease? And: 'Indeed those formations are impermanent, subject to arising and passing away; having arisen, they cease, and their subsiding is happiness.'”

“Likewise it is taught that whether Tathāgatas arise or do not arise, the nature of things remains established; that the four nutriments are one thing for maintaining beings; that modesty and moral restraint are two things that protect the world; and that there is arrival here from another world and going from this world to another. Thus the Blessed One has taught dependent arising with cessation and other characteristics. How, then, is there no cessation?”

Because arising, cessation and so forth are described in those teachings, the master composed this Madhyamaka treatise to clarify the distinction between scriptural statements requiring interpretation and those definitive in meaning. The arising and other characteristics attributed to dependent arising in such passages are not asserted with reference to the objects of the undefiled knowledge of those whose darkness of ignorance has disappeared; they are described with reference to the cognitive objects of eyes of understanding impaired by the darkness of ignorance.

But with reference to seeing reality the Blessed One said: “This, monks, is the highest truth, namely nirvāṇa, whose nature is not deceptive; all conditioned formations are false and are of a deceptive nature.” And: “There is no suchness or non-deceptiveness here; this too is of a deceptive nature, liable to pass away, false, an illusion that fools children.”

Likewise: “Material form is like a mass of foam; feeling resembles a bubble; perception is like a mirage; formations resemble the trunk of a banana plant; and consciousness has been called a magical illusion by the Kinsman of the Sun.”

And likewise: “The monk who sees phenomena in this way, energetically striving by day or night, clearly knowing and mindful, may reach the peaceful state, the auspicious stilling of formations.” The teachings also speak of the selflessness of phenomena and related matters.

Someone unfamiliar with the intention of these teachings may wonder which instruction concerns reality and which is spoken with a particular intention. Someone of limited understanding might also take an instruction requiring interpretation to be definitive. To dispel the doubts and mistaken understanding of both kinds of disciples, the master began this treatise using reasoning and scripture.

The reasoning has been stated with “not from itself” and the verses that follow. Scripture is also adduced: “What the Blessed One called deceptive is indeed false; all formations are deceptive by nature, and for that reason they are false.” “The Great Sage declared that a first point cannot be known; saṃsāra is without beginning or end, having neither first nor last.” “In the instruction to Kātyāyana the Blessed One, who discriminates existence and nonexistence, rejected both 'exists' and 'does not exist.'”

It is also said in the noble Akṣayamati Sūtra: “Which discourses require interpretation and which are definitive? Those discourses taught as an entrance into the path are called those requiring interpretation. Those taught as an entrance into the fruit are also called those requiring interpretation. Those discourses that teach the gates of liberation—emptiness, signlessness, wishlessness, absence of fabrication, unbornness, non-production, absence of being, selflessness, absence of a being, of life, of a person, and of an owner—are called definitive.” Such is reliance upon discourses definitive in meaning, venerable Śāradvatīputra, and not reliance upon discourses requiring interpretation.

So too in the noble Samādhirāja Sūtra: “One knows the distinction of discourses definitive in meaning, where emptiness has been taught by the Sugata; but where persons, beings and human individuals are taught, one understands all those phenomena in the interpretable sense.”

Therefore the master began his exposition of dependent arising to demonstrate that teachings of arising and so forth do not express ultimately real production.

Objection: “If, given the absence of production and the rest, the master began this undertaking to establish the falsity of all phenomena, would it not follow that whatever is false does not exist? Then there are no unwholesome actions, no unfortunate rebirths as their results, no wholesome actions, and no fortunate rebirths. Without fortunate or unfortunate rebirths there is no saṃsāra, so the entire undertaking would be pointless.”

Reply: We expound the false character of things by reference to conventional truth, as an antidote to worldly fixation upon 'this alone is true.' But noble ones who have accomplished their task do not apprehend anything as false or not false.

Moreover, for a person who has completely understood the falsity of all things, are there karmic actions or saṃsāra? Such a one apprehends no thing as existing or not existing. As the Blessed One said in the noble Ratnakūṭa Sūtra: “Kāśyapa, when the mind is thoroughly sought, it is not found. What is not found is not apprehended. What is not apprehended is neither past nor future nor present. What is neither past, future nor present is without intrinsic nature. What has no intrinsic nature has no arising, and what has no arising has no cessation.” The sūtra continues.

But someone who, owing to attachment to error, does not understand this falsity, clings to intrinsic nature in dependently arisen things. Because he is attached to the fixation 'this alone is true' concerning phenomena, he undertakes karmic actions and continues to wander in saṃsāra. While situated in error he cannot attain nirvāṇa.

Question: “Do things of a false nature also become causes of defilement and purification?” For example, an illusory young woman is a cause of defilement for those who do not understand her nature, while a creation of the Tathāgata is a cause of purification for those who have accumulated wholesome roots.

For it is said in the Dṛḍhādhyāśayaparipṛcchā Sūtra: “Son of good family, suppose a magician's performance is being presented. Someone sees a woman conjured by the magician and his mind becomes overwhelmed by passion. Fearing embarrassment in front of the assembly, he gets up from his seat and goes away. Having gone away, he contemplates that very woman as impure, impermanent, suffering, empty and without a self.” and so forth.

And in the Vinaya, there is a mechanical young woman produced by a craftsman. Though devoid of an actual woman, she appears in the shape of an actual woman and becomes an object of lust for the painter. In the same way, things of deceptive nature become conditions of defilement and purification for foolish people.

Likewise in the noble Ratnakūṭa Sūtra: Then about five hundred monks, unable to enter, understand, or assent to the Blessed One's teaching of the Dharma, got up from their seats and departed. At that time, on the road along which the monks were going, the Blessed One magically created two monks.

The five hundred monks approached the two magically created monks on that road. Having approached, they said to them: “Venerable ones, where are you going?” The two creations replied: “We are going to forest dwellings; there we shall live experiencing the happiness of meditative concentration. For we do not enter into, comprehend, or assent to the Dharma the Blessed One teaches. We are afraid and have become frightened.”

The five hundred monks said: “Venerable ones, we too do not enter into, understand, or assent to the Blessed One's instruction. We fear it, are terrified and have come to alarm. Therefore we shall go to forest dwellings and live experiencing the happiness of meditation.”

The two conjured monks replied: “Then, venerable ones, let us agree and not dispute; for freedom from dispute is foremost among the qualities of an ascetic. To abandon what have you entered the path?” They answered: “We have entered the path to abandon passion, hatred and delusion.”

The conjured monks asked: “But, venerable ones, do the passion, hatred and delusion that you intend to extinguish actually exist for you?” They replied: “They are not apprehended internally, nor externally, nor between the two; and they do not arise without having been conceptually imagined.”

The conjured monks replied: “Then, venerable ones, do not imagine or discriminate. When you do not imagine or discriminate, you will neither be attached nor become dispassionate. Someone who is neither attached nor dispassionate is said to be at peace. Moral discipline, venerable ones, neither wanders in saṃsāra nor passes into final nirvāṇa.

“Concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation, venerable ones, neither wander in saṃsāra nor pass into final nirvāṇa. By these phenomena nirvāṇa is indicated, yet these very phenomena are empty and by nature isolated. Abandon, venerable ones, this conception called 'final nirvāṇa.' Do not make a conception within a conception, and do not seek to know a conception within a conception. Whoever recognizes a conception within a conception has precisely that conception as a bond.

“Venerable ones, enter the attainment of cessation of perception and feeling. We say that a monk who has entered the attainment of cessation of perception and feeling has nothing further to do.”

Then the minds of those five hundred monks were liberated from the outflows without grasping. With liberated minds they approached the Blessed One, bowed their heads at his feet, and sat to one side.

Then the venerable Subhūti spoke to those monks: “Venerable ones, where did you go, or whence have you come?” They replied: “Venerable Subhūti, the Blessed One taught Dharma that is not for going anywhere and not for coming from anywhere.”

He asked: “Who is your teacher?” They replied: “One who has not arisen and will not pass into final nirvāṇa.” He asked: “How did you hear Dharma?” They replied: “Not for bondage and not for liberation.” He asked: “By whom were you trained?” They replied: “By one who has neither body nor mind.”

He asked: “How did you apply yourselves?” They replied: “Neither to abandoning ignorance nor to generating knowledge.” He asked: “Whose disciples are you?” They replied: “Of one who has not attained and has not become fully awakened.” He asked: “Who are your companions in the holy life?” They replied: “Those who do not move about in the three realms.”

He asked: “After how long will you pass into final nirvāṇa?” They replied: “When beings conjured by the Tathāgata pass into final nirvāṇa.” He asked: “Have you done what was to be done?” They replied: “By understanding the making of 'I' and the making of 'mine.'” He asked: “Have your afflictions been exhausted?” They replied: “Through the ultimate exhaustion of all phenomena.”

He asked: “Have you overcome Māra?” They replied: “By not apprehending Māra in the aggregates.” He asked again: “Have you overcome Māra?” They again replied: “By not apprehending Māra in the aggregates.” He asked: “Have you attended upon the teacher?” They replied: “Not with body, speech or mind.” He asked: “Have you purified the field of offerings?” They replied: “Through the absence of anyone who grasps or receives.”

He asked: “Have you crossed saṃsāra?” They replied: “Through neither annihilation nor permanence.” He asked: “Have you entered the field of those worthy of offerings?” They replied: “Through release from all grasping.” He asked: “Where are you going?” They replied: “Where those created by the Tathāgata are going.”

While the venerable Subhūti questioned them and the monks answered, the minds of eight hundred monks in that assembly were freed from the outflows without attachment, and thirty-two thousand living beings gained the pure, stainless and dustless eye of Dharma.

Thus, through two monks conjured by the Tathāgata and possessed of a deceptive nature, five hundred monks obtained the conditions for purification.

It is also said in the noble Vajramaṇḍā Dhāraṇī: “Mañjuśrī, suppose that in dependence upon a piece of wood, in dependence upon a fire-drill, and in dependence upon the exertion of a man's hands, smoke appears and fire is generated. The heat of that fire is not located in the wood, the fire-drill, or the exertion of the man's hands.

“In exactly the same way, Mañjuśrī, the burning of passion, the burning of hatred and the burning of delusion arise in a person bewildered by unreal errors. Yet that burning does not reside internally, externally, or in the space between the two.

“Moreover, Mañjuśrī, why is that which is called delusion called delusion? Because delusion is completely released from all phenomena, Mañjuśrī; therefore it is called delusion. Likewise, Mañjuśrī, all things are openings into hell: this is a formula of retention.”

Mañjuśrī asked: “Blessed One, how is this a formula of retention?” The Blessed One replied: “Mañjuśrī, hells are falsely constructed by foolish ordinary people who are inverted in their understanding; they arise through their own conceptualization.”

He asked: “Blessed One, where do the hells come together?” The Blessed One replied: “Mañjuśrī, the hells come together in space. What do you think, Mañjuśrī? Do hells arise from one's own conceptualization, or from their intrinsic nature?” He answered: “Blessed One, it is through their own conceptualization that foolish ordinary people conceive of hell, animal birth and the world of Yama. Through a false superimposition they experience painful feeling and suffer in all three evil destinies.

“Blessed One, as I see hells, so I see the suffering of hell. For example, a man who has fallen asleep and entered a dream may imagine that he has gone to hell. There he may imagine that he has been cast into a great iron cauldron, boiling and blazing, of a depth of many men. There he feels sharp, harsh, intense pain, and perceives a burning of his mind; he fears and is terrified.

“On waking, he cries out 'Alas, the pain! Alas, the pain!' and weeps, laments and wails. His friends, relatives and blood relations ask what has caused his pain. He replies: 'I have experienced the pain of hell.' He even rebukes them: 'I have experienced the suffering of hell, and you still ask what has caused my pain!'

“His friends and relatives answer: 'Do not be afraid, man. You were asleep. You have not left this house or gone anywhere.' Then he remembers: 'I was asleep. What I imagined was false.' He becomes joyful again.

“Just as that man asleep in his dream imagines himself in hell through false superimposition, so, Blessed One, all foolish ordinary people bound by unreal appearances imagine the sign of a woman. After imagining such a sign, they imagine themselves enjoying themselves together with women. They think: 'I am a man, this is a woman, this woman belongs to me.'

“With a mind dominated by attachment and lust, such a foolish person turns his mind to seeking enjoyment. From this he gives rise to disputes, quarrels and contention. His faculties being corrupted, enmity arises. Through that inversion of perception, at death he imagines himself experiencing painful feelings in the hells for many thousands of aeons.

“Just as that man's friends and relatives say: 'Do not be afraid, man. You were asleep; you never left the house,' so too the Blessed Buddhas teach Dharma to beings whose understanding is inverted by the four inversions: 'There is no woman here, no man, no being, no life, no human individual or person. All these phenomena are false; all are nonexistent; all are imagined; all resemble magic, dreams, things conjured, or reflections of the moon in water.' and so forth.

“Having heard this teaching of the Tathāgata, they see all phenomena as free from passion and delusion, without intrinsic nature and unobstructed. They die with minds established like space. After death they enter final nirvāṇa in the element of nirvāṇa without remaining substratum. Thus, Blessed One, I see hells.”

And it is said in the noble Upāliparipṛcchā: “The terrors of hell were displayed to me, and many thousands of beings were moved to fear. Yet there is no being here who dies and goes to the dreadful lower world.

“Neither an agent nor causes exist by whom swords, knives and weapons are made. Through the power of conceptual imagination a person sees swords falling upon bodies in those states of misfortune.

“Splendid, beautiful golden palaces adorned with flowers blaze delightfully; yet there too no maker exists, and those too are established through the power of conceptual construction.

“The world is fashioned by the power of construction, and fools construct it through grasping at conceptions. That grasping is no grasping and is without real being; constructions resemble illusion and mirage.”

Thus it is established that things without intrinsic nature, fabricated through their own inversions of understanding, become causes of defilement for foolish people within saṃsāra. How deceptive things can serve as causes of defilement and purification should be understood in fuller detail from the Madhyamakāvatāra.

Objection: “If things do not arise from themselves, from others, from both, or without causes, why did the Blessed One say, 'Formations depend on ignorance'?” Reply: That is convention, not ultimate reality.

Question: “How must convention be explained?” We accept the establishment of convention simply in terms of conditionality—'this depending on that'—not by accepting any of the four theses, because doing so would entail the doctrine that things possess intrinsic nature, and such a doctrine is unreasonable. If mere conditionality is accepted, cause and effect depend upon one another, so there is no establishment by intrinsic nature; thus no doctrine of intrinsic nature follows.

For that very reason it is said: “Suffering is held by the dialecticians to be made by oneself, made by another, made by both, or causeless. But you have taught it to arise dependently.” It will also be said here: “The agent exists in dependence on action, and action proceeds in dependence on that agent. We do not see another means by which they are established.”

The Blessed One too taught only this much: “There is this regularity of phenomena: when this exists, that exists; from the arising of this, that arises. Thus, formations have ignorance as their condition, and consciousness has formations as its condition,” and so forth.

Here some object: “Is this conviction, 'Things are not produced,' born from a valid means of knowledge or not born from one? If it is born from a valid means of knowledge, you must say how many valid means there are, what their characteristics and objects are, and whether they arise from themselves, from another, from both or without a cause.

“But if it is not born from a valid means of knowledge, that is untenable, because apprehending something knowable depends upon a valid means of knowledge. A previously unapprehended object cannot be known without such means. If there is no valid means of knowledge, there can be no apprehension of objects: whence then your certain and correct conviction that things are unproduced?

“Furthermore, whatever grounds your conviction that things are unproduced can equally ground my conviction that all things exist. Just as you are certain that all phenomena are unproduced, I can equally be certain that all things arise. If you have no conviction that all things are unproduced, then, since one who is not convinced himself cannot convince others, beginning this treatise is useless, and all things exist without being refuted.”

Reply: If we had anything called conviction, it might be produced by a valid means of knowledge or not produced by one. But we have no such conviction. Why? Conviction dependent upon an opposite might arise if uncertainty existed. But when we do not even have uncertainty, whence could there be a conviction either opposed to or consistent with it, without dependence upon the other member of the relation—just as a donkey's horn cannot be short or long?

When conviction is absent in that way, for the establishment of what would we posit valid means of knowledge? What place is there for their number, definition and objects, or their production from oneself, another, both or no cause? None of this is something we are required to explain.

Objection: “If there is no conviction at all, how is this apparently definite statement of yours found: 'Things do not come into being from themselves, from others, from both, or without a cause'?” Reply: This statement is established through the reasoning accepted by worldly people themselves, not through the standpoint of the noble ones.

Question: “Do the noble ones then have no reasoning?” Who has said either that they do or that they do not? The ultimate truth for noble ones is silence. Where, then, would discursive elaboration arise there, such that reasoning or the absence of reasoning could be found?

Objection: “If the noble ones do not state reasons, by whom will they bring the world to know ultimate reality?” Answer: The noble ones do not expound reasoning through the same acts of worldly convention; rather, they adopt precisely those forms of reasoning that are already established in the world and use them to make others understand.

For example, though bodily impurity is present, those overcome by error and passion fail to perceive it. They superimpose a beauty that is not there and become afflicted. For the sake of their freedom from passion, a god or a person created by the Tathāgata may describe the bodily faults that were formerly concealed by their conception of beauty: 'There are hairs in this body,' and so forth. Released from their idea of beauty, they gain dispassion.

In the same way, foolish people whose eyes of understanding are affected by the darkness of ignorance superimpose the mistaken intrinsic nature of things upon what the noble ones do not apprehend as independently existent at all, and are greatly afflicted by assigning various special characteristics. Noble ones awaken them through reasons already accepted by those very people.

For example, you already accept that a pot which exists does not arise from clay and similar material anew; likewise it must be understood that something alleged to exist before its production does not arise, because it already exists. And just as you accept that a sprout does not arise from things utterly other, such as flame and charcoal, so you should understand that it does not arise from seeds and other supposed conditions as intrinsically other things.

One may say: “But this is our experience.” That too has been addressed, since this experience is false, because it is an experience—like the experience of seeing two moons in the case of someone with an eye disorder. As experience is itself on the same footing as the matter requiring proof, it cannot properly be advanced as an answer.

Thus the first chapter begins with the remedy for superimposing a mistaken nature on things: 'Things are not produced.' The remaining chapters begin to remove the various further distinctive properties that people superimpose upon things. Their purpose is to demonstrate that none of the additional characteristics—the goer, what is gone, going, and so on—belongs intrinsically to dependent arising.

Someone may say: “In our treatise we have merely set out the worldly practice involving valid means of knowledge and objects of knowledge.” Then the fruit of this exposition must be stated. If one says, “Bad logicians spoiled worldly practice by providing incorrect definitions, and we have provided the correct definitions,” this too is unreasonable. If bad logicians had rendered the things designated by those definitions incorrect in the world by producing wrong definitions, there might be a purpose in this effort. But that is not the case. Hence the effort is useless.

Further, if apprehending an object of knowledge depends on valid means of knowledge, by what are those valid means themselves determined? The defect that follows from such questions was explained in the Vigrahavyāvartanī. Because it has not been removed, there is no successful presentation of correct definitions either.

Furthermore, if two valid means of knowledge are proposed in accordance with their two kinds of characteristics, the particular and the general, does that which possesses those two defining characteristics exist or not? If it exists, then another object of knowledge is present; how then are there only two means of knowledge? If the thing defined does not exist, its defining characteristic has no support and does not exist either; how then can there be two valid means of knowledge?

For it will be said: “When the definition does not operate, what is defined cannot be established; and when what is defined cannot be established, a defining characteristic is impossible.”

Suppose someone objects that the word lakṣaṇa ('characteristic') does not mean 'that by which something is marked,' but rather, according to a grammatical derivation using the affix lyuṭ in the sense of an object, it means 'that which is marked.' Even so, because what is marked by it cannot be established, and because that which functions as an instrument must be different from the object of that instrument, the same fault follows.

One might say: “There is no such fault because cognition is the instrument, and cognition is included under particular characteristics.” We reply: the distinctive form belonging to an entity alone, unlike anything else's, is its particular characteristic, for example earth's hardness, feeling's experience of an object, or consciousness's awareness of an object.

He claims that such a thing is marked by cognition, rejects the accepted etymology, and accepts a derivation making the thing described an object. By also accepting cognition as an instrument, he in effect declares the particular characteristic to be the object, and another particular characteristic to be the instrument. If consciousness's particular characteristic is the instrument, it must have an object distinct from it: thus the same fault follows.

Someone may say: “Hardness and the like, residing in earth and other things, are objects of consciousness, and these are not distinct from their own particular characteristics.” In that case the particular characteristic of consciousness itself cannot be an object, and hence cannot belong to what is knowable, for only the particular characteristic taking the form of an object is knowable.

Accordingly, the statement 'the knowable is of two kinds, the particular and the general' must be restricted: some particular characteristics are knowable because they are said to be marked, while others are not knowable because they are the means by which marking occurs. But if those too are construed as objects, they would require a different instrument, and the supposition of one cognition after another as an instrument entails the fault of an infinite regress.

Suppose you think self-awareness exists, and that through self-awareness an object is grasped and cognition is thus included among knowable things. We answer: self-awareness has been refuted at length in the Madhyamakāvatāra. Thus it is untenable that one particular characteristic is marked through another, while the latter is marked by self-awareness.

Moreover, that cognition itself cannot be established apart from its particular characteristic; when the thing to be defined does not exist, the operation of a definition lacking its basis cannot be established. Therefore cognition does not exist in that intrinsically established way, and whence would self-awareness come?

As the noble Ratnacūḍaparipṛcchā says: “Not seeing his mind in that manner, he searches for the stream of mind, asking whence mind arises. He thinks: 'When an object is present, mind arises. Is the object something other than mind, or is the very object itself mind?'

“If the object is other than mind, there would be two minds. If the object is the very same as mind, how could mind behold mind? Mind does not behold mind. Just as the very same edge of a sword cannot cut that same edge, and the tip of a finger cannot touch itself with the very same fingertip, so the very same mind cannot see that same mind.

“Applied attentively in this way, he knows and sees the stream of mind, the creeper of mind, the nature of mind, the mind's lack of abiding, non-annihilation, non-permanence, non-fixity, its not being causeless, its not contradicting conditions, its not being from itself or from another, its not being identical or different, its non-movement, invisibility and particular character, as they really are, as suchness, and without bringing them to cessation.

“And he knows and sees that isolation of mind just as it is. This, son of good family, is a bodhisattva's establishment of mindfulness consisting in the contemplation of mind within mind.”

Thus self-awareness does not exist. Because it does not exist, what would be marked, and by what?

Moreover, is the defining characteristic different from the thing defined, or is it not different? If it is different, then because it differs from the defined thing, the defining characteristic is no more a characteristic than something that does not define it. And because the thing defined differs from the defining characteristic, it is no more a thing defined than something that is not defined.

Likewise, because a characteristic differs from the thing it defines, the latter would exist without dependence on that characteristic. It would therefore not be something defined at all, since it did not depend on a defining characteristic—just as a flower in the sky is not something defined.

If the defining characteristic and the thing defined are not different, then, because the defined thing is indistinguishable from the characteristic, it would lose its status as what is defined, just as the characteristic itself is not a thing defined. And since the characteristic is not different from the thing defined, it too would not have the nature of a characteristic, just as the defined thing itself is not a characteristic.

As it has been said: “If a characteristic is other than that which it defines, the defined thing would be without a characteristic. If they are not other, you have explained quite plainly the absence of both.”

Apart from identity and difference, there is no other possibility by which the defining characteristic and the thing defined could be established. Accordingly it will also be said: “If two things have no establishment either through being one or through being many, how indeed can they have any establishment?”

If someone replies: “They will be established as inexpressible,” this is not so. Inexpressibility means that a division between the two has not been understood. But where that division cannot be understood, one cannot distinguish and determine: “This is the characteristic and this is the thing characterized.” Both are therefore absent. Consequently they are not established through inexpressibility either.

Furthermore, if knowledge is an instrument in determining its object, who is the agent? Instruments and the like cannot exist without an agent, as in the action of cutting. Suppose one imagines the mind to be the agent in this case. That too is not reasonable, since the function of consciousness is seeing the object as such, while the function of mental factors is seeing the particular features of the object.

For it is accepted that “the cognition of the object is consciousness, while the mental factors apprehend its particulars.” Instruments and other ancillary factors have their status as instruments by contributing through their own subordinate activities to the accomplishment of a single principal action.

But here knowledge and consciousness do not share one principal activity. Rather, the principal activity of consciousness is the determination of an object as such, while that of knowledge is determination of the object's particular features. Therefore knowledge is not the instrument and mind is not the agent. The same fault follows.

Someone might say: “Since scripture states that all phenomena are without self, there is no agent whatsoever; thus the conventional operation of actions and instruments exists even without an agent.” That too is not correct, because the intended meaning of scripture has not been determined properly. This was explained in the Madhyamakāvatāra.

Someone might instead say: “We speak of the body of a stone figure or the head of Rāhu, even though no stone figure exists separately from its body and no Rāhu exists separately from his head. Thus there is a relation between qualifier and qualified; likewise one may speak of the particular characteristic of earth even if no earth exists apart from that characteristic.”

That is not so, because the cases are unequal. Words such as “body” and “head,” like “hands of a statue of the Buddha,” are conventionally used in dependence upon accompanying things; when these words arise they invite the question, “Whose body? Whose head?” A further qualification is expected.

The expressions “stone figure” and “Rāhu,” following ordinary linguistic convention, remove the hearer's expectation of that further qualification. Their use is therefore appropriate. In our present case, however, where no earth apart from hardness and other qualities exists, the relation of qualifier and qualified is not established in the same way.

Suppose one replies: “The non-Buddhist schools accept a separately existent bearer of the characteristic; therefore it is not wrong to speak of a qualification in conformity with that usage.” This is not so. Things imagined by non-Buddhist schools that are inconsistent with reasoning should not be accepted in one's own system, for this would entail acceptance of their further kinds of valid means of knowledge and so on.

Moreover, just as in the designation of a person, a stone figure that appropriates a body exists as the basis of worldly practice, and Rāhu appropriating a head likewise exists as a conventional bearer. The qualifying terms are established conventionally without analysis. Hence the examples are not suitable.

If one says: “The examples are established because no entity apart from the body or head can be established and only these are apprehended,” we reply that the point fails. Ordinary worldly practice does not proceed by such analysis, and worldly objects exist without that analysis.

For example, a self examined as distinct from form and the other aggregates cannot be established, but in worldly convention the self is designated in dependence on the aggregates. The same is true of Rāhu and the stone figure; hence the proposed example has not been established.

Likewise, even though under examination no earth and other things are found as what is characterized apart from hardness and related qualities, and though a characteristic without a thing characterized has no support, the masters nevertheless established their conventional existence through their mutual dependence, precisely because this is convention.

This must be admitted. Otherwise convention would not be distinguished from ultimate truth by reasoning; it would be ultimate reality itself and not convention. And it is not only stone figures and the like that cannot withstand reasoned analysis: by the reasoning to be given later, form, feeling and the other aggregates cannot withstand it either. If the former are said not to exist even conventionally, the same must then be asserted of the latter. But that is not acceptable, so the proposed view is false.

Someone may ask: “Why such minute scrutiny? We do not say that the whole practice of valid means of knowledge and their objects is ultimately true. We merely explain what is well known in the world by this method.”

We reply: We too say, why introduce such minute scrutiny into worldly practice? Let convention—which obtains its apparent existence solely through erroneous apprehension—stand as the cause of accumulating wholesome roots that lead seekers of freedom toward liberation until reality is understood.

But you, through an understanding poorly trained in distinguishing conventional truth from ultimate truth, apply analysis at particular points and unjustifiably destroy this convention. I, being skilled in establishing conventional truth, remain within the worldly standpoint and oppose your reasoning, which was introduced to abolish one part of convention, by another piece of reasoning.

I am like an elder of the world correcting you when you depart from worldly usage; I do not overturn convention itself. Therefore, if worldly practice is intended, then alongside a characteristic there must also be something characterized. The previous fault remains. If ultimate reality is intended, then, because nothing characterized exists, the two types of characteristic do not exist either. Whence, then, the supposed two valid means of knowledge?

If you decline to accept that the derivation of words rests upon relations between acts and agents, that is very troublesome. For you use words whose operation depends on the relations between acts, agents and the like, while refusing to accept the meanings of those words—actions, instruments and so forth. Truly, your practice seems bound only by what you happen to wish.

Since the two kinds of objects of knowledge are thus not established, testimony and other sources of knowledge would count as additional valid means, because they are not confined to the particular and general characteristics. Moreover the proposed definition is too narrow, since it does not include worldly expressions such as “The pot is perceived directly,” and since it refuses to accept the usage of ordinary people. It is therefore untenable.

Someone may say: “Blue and other qualities which constitute the pot are directly perceived, because they are determined by direct perception. Accordingly, just as by figuratively attributing the result to the cause one says 'the Buddhas' arising is happiness,' so too the pot, whose cause is directly perceived blue and the like, is called directly perceived through figuratively attributing a cause to its result.”

Such figurative usage is not appropriate in this case. The arising of a Buddha is something observed in the world apart from happiness; and because arising is the defining characteristic of the conditioned and requires hundreds of difficult conditions, it is not itself happiness. To call it happiness is therefore an intelligible figurative attribution.

But in the expression “The pot is directly perceived,” there is no separately apprehended pot that is not perceived directly and to which 'directly perceived' could be attributed figuratively. If you say the pot does not exist apart from blue and other qualities, so its direct perception is only figurative, the figure is still less acceptable, because there is no underlying entity to which it is attributed. One cannot figuratively attribute sharpness to a donkey's horn.

Furthermore, if the pot, a constituent of worldly practice, is not distinct from blue and the other qualities, so that its direct perceptibility is figurative, then blue and the other qualities are likewise not distinct from earth and the other elements. Their direct perceptibility too ought to be figurative.

As it has been said: “Just as a pot does not exist apart from form and the other qualities, so form does not exist apart from air and the other elements.” Thus the proposed definition fails to encompass such worldly usages and remains inadequate.

For one who knows reality, direct perceptibility is not admitted either for pots and similar things or for blue and similar qualities. But within worldly convention the direct perceptibility of pots and the like must certainly be admitted.

As it has been said in the Śataka: “The whole pot appears unseen even while form is seen; who, knowing reality, would say that the pot is directly perceived?” By this very analysis a person of excellent understanding should also reject all attributions such as fragrant, sweet and soft.

Furthermore, because the word pratyakṣa (“directly perceptible”) designates an object that is not remote, an object immediately present is pratyakṣa. Since an object is before the senses, pots, blue things and the like are properly described as directly perceptible when they are immediately encountered.

The cognition that determines them is called direct perception because it is a cause of direct apprehension, just as the fire that consumes grass and husks is described in terms of what it burns. But someone who derives pratyakṣa from 'operating with reference to each sense faculty' offers an improper derivation, since cognition is not an object of a sense faculty, whereas its own objects are its objects. The appropriate expression would instead be 'with reference to each object.'

One might say: “Although the occurrence of cognition depends on both the supporting faculty and its object, it is named after its support because the acuity or dullness of that support affects the cognition. Thus we call it 'eye-consciousness.' Likewise, although cognition occurs with reference to its object, it depends on a sense faculty and can therefore be called pratyakṣa.”

“For an object is indeed named after its distinctive cause, as in 'the sound of a drum' or 'a barley sprout.'” But this is not equal to the former case.

In that former case, if cognitions were designated only by their objects—'consciousness of form' and so forth—the distinction between the six kinds of consciousness would not be indicated, because mental consciousness can occur in relation to the objects of eye-consciousness and the others.

For example, when one says merely 'consciousness' with regard to the six consciousnesses of blue and other things, one still wonders whether it arose from a material faculty or from the mind. By naming the supporting faculty, the distinction between mental consciousness and the consciousnesses of the eye and the other faculties is maintained, even where mental consciousness has the same kinds of objects.

Here, however, the intended definition is of a valid means of knowledge, and direct perception has been accepted merely as something free from conceptual construction. Since its proposed distinction from conceptual thought already lies in its freedom from thought, naming it after a distinctive cause accomplishes nothing.

Because the enumeration of the valid means of knowledge depends on the objects known, and because the two proposed means are established merely by their conformity to the forms of those objects, the reference to the sense faculty contributes nothing to explaining their intrinsic nature. Therefore naming according to the object alone is appropriate.

If one replies that pratyakṣa is the word well known in the world, while the word 'with reference to each object' is not known in the intended sense, and that the derivation based on the supporting faculty is therefore adopted, we answer: the word pratyakṣa is indeed well known in the world, and we use it exactly as the world does.

But if one derives it by dismissing worldly objects as they are conventionally established, one ends up dismissing the accepted word itself; then this is no longer pratyakṣa in its established sense.

Further, a single occurrence of eye-consciousness, depending on a single moment of a sense faculty, could not be called pratyakṣa if the derivation required reference to each of a plurality of senses. And if each individual occurrence is not pratyakṣa, a multitude of them would not be so either.

Moreover, if only cognition free of conceptual construction is accepted as direct perception, while ordinary people do not transact their affairs through that kind of cognition alone, the imagined definition of direct perception becomes useless for explaining ordinary worldly relations between valid means of knowledge and objects of knowledge.

The scriptural statement “One possessing eye-consciousness knows blue but does not know 'this is blue'” does not establish that only cognition free from conceptual construction is direct perception. Its purpose is not to give a definition of direct perception but to show the limited, non-discriminating character of the five sensory consciousnesses.

Therefore in worldly life whether one speaks of what is characterized, particular characteristics or general characteristics, whatever is apprehended directly is immediately present and is established as directly perceptible together with the cognition that takes it as its object.

Things such as two moons are not directly perceptible relative to the knowledge of someone without an eye disorder, but they certainly are directly perceptible relative to someone suffering from such an eye disorder.

Inference is the cognition whose object is not immediately present and which arises from an indicative mark that invariably accompanies what is to be proved.

Authoritative testimony is the utterance of trustworthy persons who know directly things beyond the senses.

Comparison is the cognition of something not previously experienced based upon resemblance, as in the recognition 'a wild ox is like a cow.'

Thus the apprehension of things in worldly practice is established through four valid means of knowledge.

And these are established dependently on one another: when valid means of knowledge exist, there are objects to be known; when objects to be known exist, there are valid means of knowledge. Yet there is no establishment of either by intrinsic nature. Therefore let worldly practice remain just as it is observed. Enough of this digression; let us return to explaining our subject. The Blessed Buddhas' teaching of Dharma takes its stand within the worldly standpoint.

Here members of our own community say: “The statement that things do not arise from themselves is reasonable, because self-production would be purposeless. The statement that they do not arise from both is also reasonable, because one of the two components is deficient. The thesis of causeless production is utterly inferior, so its refutation too is reasonable. But the assertion that things do not arise from another is unreasonable, for the Blessed One has stated that things are produced by conditions which are other than the things produced.”
