MMK 1.4

kriyā na pratyayavatī nāpratyayavatī kriyā
pratyayā nākriyāvantaḥ kriyāvantaś ca santy uta

There is no activity that has conditions, nor any activity without conditions. There are no conditions without activity, and none with activity.

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

'The action does not possess conditions.' If some action existed, then, possessing conditions such as the eye, it could produce consciousness. But it does not exist. Why? Is the proposed action supposed to occur when consciousness has already arisen, when it has not arisen, or when it is in the process of arising? It is not reasonable in the case of what has already arisen. An action is something that brings a thing to completion. If the thing is already complete, what need has it of the action?

This was explained in the Madhyamakāvatāra in statements such as 'Nor is it reasonable for what has already been born to be born once more.' Nor is [the action] reasonable when consciousness has not yet arisen, because of statements such as 'This birth without an agent is not reasonable.' Nor does the action occur in a thing that is presently arising, since there is no presently-arising thing apart from what has arisen and what has not arisen. As has been said: 'Because the thing that is arising is already arisen, the arising thing does not arise; otherwise everything whatsoever would have to be in the state of arising.'

Since an activity of producing is impossible at these three times, it does not exist. That is precisely why [Nāgārjuna] says 'action does not possess conditions.' It was explained in the Madhyamakāvatāra with words such as 'An attribute does not exist without something qualified by it.' A barren woman's son cannot be called the owner of cows.

If so, one might say that the action will be without conditions. That too is unreasonable, as expressed in 'nor is the action without conditions.' When an action that possesses conditions does not exist, how could there be an unconditioned and causeless action? Just because cloth made of threads is untenable, one does not accept that cloth is made of vīraṇa grass. Therefore action does not produce things.

Objection: 'If action is impossible, the conditions themselves must produce things.' Reply: 'Conditions are not without activity.' When no activity exists, how can conditions that lack activity and are devoid of an operative cause be producers? If instead conditions possessing activity are producers, he replies: 'Nor, indeed, are they possessed of activity.' The negation is to be supplied from the earlier statement. The particle uta expresses restriction. The absence of activity has already been stated; how then could conditions possess it?

As has been explained for the supposed action producing consciousness, so too other supposed processes and things are to be understood. Consequently things do not arise even from an action, and the statement of intrinsic production is empty of meaning.

Objection: 'What need is there for this examination of whether conditions possess activity? Consciousness and other phenomena do arise in dependence upon the eye and other conditions. Accordingly, the eye and the like are conditions, and consciousness and the like arise from them.' This too is unreasonable, as he says:
