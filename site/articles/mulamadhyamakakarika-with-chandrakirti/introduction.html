Candrakīrti’s Introduction to Chapter 1

Chapter One: Examination of Conditions. Homage to noble Mañjuśrī, the youthful prince.

“Having abandoned dwelling in the two extremes, born from the ocean of the wisdom of the Fully Awakened One, he compassionately taught, as he himself understood it, the profundity of the waters of the true Dharma.

“The brilliance of his insight even now burns up the fuel of opponents' views and the darkness in the minds of people.

“His arrows, the words of unequalled knowledge, destroy without remainder the army of enemies that is existence, and bestow the glory of sovereignty over the three realms upon the world of beings to be trained, together with the gods.

“Having bowed before that Nāgārjuna, I shall compose an explanation of his verses, one that is clear, woven together with sentences whose correct method is easy to understand, and undisturbed by the winds of contentious reasoning.”

Now the treatise to be explained begins with the words “Not from oneself, nor from another, nor from both,” and so forth. If someone asks what its connection, subject matter, and purpose are, its connection is as follows. According to the method laid down in the Madhyamakāvatāra, the first production of the thought of awakening, adorned with nondual knowledge and preceded by the means of great compassion, is taken as the starting point of the causes that lead to the arising of the knowledge of a Tathāgata.

From there, up to the composition of this treatise by Ācārya Nāgārjuna, who knew the unperverted system of the Perfection of Wisdom, the treatise was composed through compassion for the purpose of making others understand. Such is its connection.

“That which subdues all your enemies—the defilements—and protects you from bad destinies and from becoming is a śāstra because it possesses the qualities of instruction and protection. These two are not found in the doctrines of others.”

The master himself wishes to offer homage to the Tathāgata, the supreme teacher who is not separate from that nature, on the occasion of composing the treatise. At the same time he shows the subject matter and purpose of the entire treatise that follows, and manifests its greatness because it correctly makes these clear. He therefore says:

“Neither cessation nor arising, neither annihilation nor permanence, neither identity nor difference, neither coming nor going: I bow to that Fully Awakened One, the best among teachers, who taught dependent arising, the peaceful cessation of all conceptual proliferation.”

Here dependent arising, qualified by eight attributes beginning with non-cessation, is the subject matter of the treatise. Its purpose is said to be nirvāṇa, characterized by the auspicious cessation of all conceptual elaboration. In the words “I bow to him, the best of speakers,” homage is expressed. Such is the collective meaning of the two stanzas.

The meaning of the individual parts will now be distinguished. Nirodha (cessation) is destruction; momentary destruction is called cessation. Utpāda (arising) is production, meaning the emergence of a thing's own existence. Uccheda (annihilation) is cutting off, meaning the interruption of a continuum. Śāśvata (permanence) means eternal, remaining unmoved at all times.

Ekārtha (one in meaning) means an undivided entity, not something separate. Nānārtha (different in meaning) means a divided entity, something separate. Āgama (coming) is the arrival near at hand of things situated in a distant place. Nirgama (going away) is the passage to a distant place of things located nearby.

The verbal root i means going, while the prefix prati conveys attainment. A verbal root changes its meaning according to the prefix attached to it. As it is said: “A prefix forcibly carries the meaning of a root elsewhere, just as the sweetness of the Ganges' water is changed by the water of the ocean.”

Here the word pratītya is an indeclinable verbal derivative ending in lyap and expresses attainment or dependence. The root pad, with the prefixes sam and ut, means manifestation; consequently samutpāda denotes the manifestation of something. Thus the meaning of pratītyasamutpāda is the arising of things in dependence upon causes and conditions.

Others say that the root i means going and destruction, that the suffix applied to what is subject to such going gives the form iya, and that prati conveys repetition or distribution. They derive the term with a secondary suffix and explain pratītyasamutpāda as the arising of things that perish again and again.

In such statements as “Monks, I shall teach you dependent arising,” and “One who sees dependent arising sees the Dharma,” their derivation might be preferable, because a distributive meaning and a compound are possible in those contexts.

But consider the particular meaning accepted directly in a statement such as “In dependence on the eye and visible forms, eye-consciousness arises.” Here pratītya relates to the eye. When even the arising of a single consciousness having one eye faculty as its cause is intended, whence is there a meaning of repeated distribution?

On the other hand, the meaning 'attaining' is possible even where no particular object is specified: dependent arising is the occurrence of a thing through reaching or depending upon its factors. It is also possible when the particular factors are mentioned, since “in dependence on the eye” is explained as “having reached the eye” or “with the eye and visible forms as its conditions.”

If pratītya were a word formed with a secondary suffix, then, in “Depending on the eye and visible forms, eye-consciousness arises,” the word pratītya would not be an indeclinable and a compound would not be possible. With a case ending expressed, the words would have to be arranged as “eye, pratītya, consciousness, and forms.” But that is not so. Therefore the derivation of pratītya as an indeclinable ending in -lyap must be accepted.

Another critic repeats two interpretations, saying: “Some derive dependent arising as arising after reaching each of the various conditions, because prati signifies distribution, the root i signifies reaching, and samutpāda means occurrence. Others derive it as the arising of perishing things, one after another.” In criticizing these interpretations, he demonstrates first of all that he has not accurately repeated the opposing position.

Why? The person who explains pratītya as signifying reaching does not interpret prati as distributive, nor does he make the root i by itself mean reaching. Rather, he says that the prefix prati has the sense of reaching, and he explains the whole word pratītya in the sense of reaching.

Accordingly, when dependent arising has been derived as “arising upon reaching,” and the intention is to refer to the totality of all possible things that arise, repetition is connected with the expression: “Upon reaching this or that combination of causes and conditions, a thing arises.” But if the intention is a particular reference, “upon reaching the eye and visible forms,” then no distributive sense is involved. Thus the critic is first of all unskilled in repeating the other interpretation.

Moreover, the criticism that the statement “In dependence on the eye and visible forms, eye-consciousness arises” cannot admit the two meanings is also untenable. Why? Because without supplying reasoning, the critic merely asserts that arising through contact with the eye is impossible.

If his intention is that consciousness, being non-material, cannot reach the eye because reaching is observed only among material things, this too is unreasonable. We also accept the usage “This monk has attained the fruit,” and the word 'attaining' can be synonymous with 'depending upon.'

The revered Ācārya Nāgārjuna himself takes pratītya in the sense of attainment in his statement, “Whatever is produced after reaching this or that is not produced by intrinsic nature.” Therefore, say some, the criticism does not stand.

As for the critic's own definition: “When this is present, that occurs; with the arising of this, that arises: such conditionality is the meaning of dependent arising,” that too is inadequate, because it does not separately state the particular meanings of the words pratītya and samutpāda, whose derivation is what is being sought.

Even if the word pratītyasamutpāda is accepted as an established expression, as with araṇyetilaka and similar expressions, that too is untenable, since the master accepts dependent arising in accordance with the meanings of its parts, as in: “Whatever has arisen upon reaching this or that has not arisen by its own nature.”

If someone explains “When this is present, that occurs” with the example “When something short exists, something long exists,” does this not itself accept that the long exists by depending on, reaching, and having regard to the short? In that case one accepts precisely the interpretation one had rejected. Enough of the digression.

Thus by expounding the arising of things in dependence on causes and conditions, the Blessed One excludes their production without causes, from a single cause, or from unsuitable causes, as well as their production from themselves, from others, or from both. By excluding these possibilities he reveals the actual conventional character of conventionally designated things.

This very conventional dependent arising, because it has not arisen through intrinsic nature, is qualified from the standpoint of noble knowledge by the eight attributes from non-cessation through non-going. How dependent arising is without cessation and the other features will be established by the whole treatise.

Although dependent arising can be described by endlessly many attributes, only these eight are selected because they are the chief points at issue in the disputes. When dependent arising is seen as it actually is, the noble ones completely bring to rest the conceptual proliferation involving objects to be named and the like. Therefore that same dependent arising is called the cessation of conceptual elaboration.

And because the mind and mental factors do not operate upon it, the traffic of knower and known ceases. It is free from every affliction, including birth, old age and death; therefore it is auspicious. Since the most desired object of the action of teaching is dependent arising qualified in this way, it is put in the accusative case.

“Neither cessation nor arising, neither annihilation nor permanence, neither identity nor difference, neither coming nor going; I bow to the Fully Awakened One, the best of teachers, who taught dependent arising as the auspicious stilling of conceptual proliferation.”

Seeing that the Tathāgata alone speaks without distortion concerning reality, because he has comprehended dependent arising as described above, and perceiving every opposing doctrine as the prattle of children, the master, moved by profound faith, again qualifies the Blessed One with the words “the best among speakers.”

The negation of cessation precedes the negation of arising to show that a fixed temporal order—first arising, then cessation—cannot be established for the two. It will be said: “If birth were first and old age and death followed, there would be birth without old age and death, and one who was born would be immortal.” Therefore there is no rule that there must first be arising and then cessation.

Now, wishing to teach dependent arising qualified by non-cessation and the other characteristics, and considering that by refuting arising it will be easier to refute cessation and the rest, the master begins with the refutation of arising. For production imagined by others would have to be imagined from oneself, from another, from both, or without a cause. Having determined that none of these is tenable, he says [the first verse].
