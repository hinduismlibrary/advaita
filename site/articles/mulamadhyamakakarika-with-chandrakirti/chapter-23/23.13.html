MMK 23.13

anitye nityam ity evaṃ yadi grāho viparyayaḥ
nānityaṃ vidyate śūnye kuto grāho viparyayaḥ || 13 ||

If grasping permanence in what is impermanent is an inversion, when the empty is not impermanent, whence is the inversion in grasping permanence?

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

anitye nityam ity evaṃ yadi grāho viparyayaḥ | nānityaṃ vidyate śūnye kuto grāho viparyayaḥ || 13 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Thus, having established the absence of passion and aversion because their objects, good and bad, are absent, he now establishes the absence of delusion too by showing that the nature of mistaken views is absent. He says:

Here four inversions are stated. They are these: grasping the five aggregates, which are impermanent and perish in every moment, as “permanent”—that is an inversion. And also:

“Impermanence is certainly suffering; what is suffering is not happiness.” By this reasoning, what is impermanent is suffering, and all conditioned things are impermanent. Therefore grasping the five aggregates, whose nature is suffering, as happiness is another mistaken view. And also:

“This seed is born from the union of semen and blood, and is nourished by feces and urine. Knowing it to have an impure form, by what desire do you lust here? He who would lie with a woman lies in the hollow of her lower belly, covered by a heap of impurity, with that moist skin making it wet.” And so forth.

Thus this body is in every way, always, of an impure nature. A grasping or adherence, through delusion, to purity in it is an inversion. Likewise, the five aggregates are unlike the defining characteristic of a self; because they are unstable and subject to arising and passing, they are selfless and empty of the nature of a self. Grasping them as self, adhering to a self in what is not-self, is an inversion. These four inversions are causes of delusion.

Here it is now examined: if permanence, the perception of permanence, the grasping of permanence in things empty of own-nature, is established as an inversion, then surely impermanence too is absent in aggregates empty of own-nature. To this he says:

Impermanence is called an inversion in relation to its opposite, permanence. But impermanence is not found in what is empty. When impermanence is absent, whence would its opposite, permanence—the mistaken view of permanence—arise? This is the meaning. Therefore no inversion exists. And just as impermanence is not possible in what is empty, devoid of the nature of an existent thing and not arisen with own-nature, so suffering too is not possible; impurity too is absent; not-self too is absent. And when suffering and the rest are absent because of emptiness of own-nature, where would the mistaken views that are their opposites—permanence, happiness, purity, and self—come from? Therefore the mistaken views do not exist in their own nature. In their absence, whence would ignorance arise? There would be no cause. As the Blessed One said:

“Ignorance is never found at any time through ignorance; and how can there be the arising of conditions? This ignorance is not present in the world; therefore this has been called ignorance by me.”

And also: “Lord, how is delusion the dhāraṇī-ground?” The Blessed One said: “Mañjuśrī, delusion is completely liberated; therefore it is called delusion.” And so forth. Thus it is established as an inversion.
