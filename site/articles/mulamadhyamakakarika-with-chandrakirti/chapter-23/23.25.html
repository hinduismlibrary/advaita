MMK 23.25

yady abhūtāḥ svabhāvena kleśāḥ kecid dhi kasyacit
kathaṃ nāma prahīyeran ko 'sadbhāvaṃ prahāsyati || 25 ||

If some afflictions were unreal by own-nature for someone, how could they be abandoned? Who would abandon nonexistence?

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

yady abhūtāḥ svabhāvena kleśāḥ kecid dhi kasyacit | kathaṃ nāma prahīyeran ko 'sadbhāvaṃ prahāsyati || 25 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Suppose it is conceived that they are unreal by own-nature. Even so, abandonment is not possible, he says:

Even afflictions that are unreal, nonexistent by own-nature, cannot be abandoned. For it is not possible to remove the nonexistent coldness of fire. In the same way, if these afflictions do not exist by own-nature for someone, who will abandon them? No one will abandon them. Thus, since abandonment is not possible in either position, there is no abandonment of the afflictions. And when there is no abandonment, how could there be a search for a means of abandoning the afflictions? Therefore what was said, “Ignorance and the other afflictions certainly exist, because a means of abandoning them is sought,” is unreasonable.

As it is said in the noble Samādhirāja: “Whoever would be passionate, wherever he would be passionate, or by whatever he would be passionate; whoever would be angry, wherever he would be angry, or by whatever he would be angry; whoever would be deluded, wherever he would be deluded, or by whatever he would be deluded: he does not see that dharma; he does not apprehend that dharma. Not seeing and not apprehending that dharma, he is called passionless, without aversion, undeluded, with a mind not mistaken and concentrated. He is called one who has crossed over, one who has gone to the further shore, one who has reached safety.” And so forth at length.

And also: “On the back of a mirror, and likewise in a bowl of oil, he looks at the adorned face of a woman. Having produced passion there, the foolish one runs off, seeking a lover. When there is no transfer of the face, a face is never found in the reflection. Just as the deluded one would produce passion there, so understand all dharmas to be similar.” And so forth.

And also: “Awakening has been shown through form; form has been shown for awakening. The higher Dharma has been taught through a word that is different. The higher form, through words, is deep by own-nature; form and awakening are equal, and no difference between them is found. Just as nirvāṇa, deep, is made clear through words, nirvāṇa is not found, and that word too is not found. Neither the word nor nirvāṇa, neither of the two is found. In this way nirvāṇa is made clear among empty dharmas. Nirvāṇa is just cessation, and nirvāṇa is not found; for the non-engagement of dharmas is the same afterward as it was before.”

“All dharmas by own-nature are equal and similar to nirvāṇa, known by those joined to the essence of renunciation for the awakening of a Buddha.”

And also: “By knowledge I know the emptiness of the aggregates; having known it, I do not dwell with the afflictions. For I speak only by convention; having completely entered nirvāṇa, I move through this world.”

And also: “Those heroes are completely extinguished in the world by whom dharmas are known as having no own-nature. Unattached, they move among the qualities of sense pleasures, free from attachment, and train beings.”

Thus ends the twenty-third chapter, named “Examination of Inversions,” in the Madhyamaka Commentary, the Clear Words, composed by the teacher Candrakīrtipāda.
