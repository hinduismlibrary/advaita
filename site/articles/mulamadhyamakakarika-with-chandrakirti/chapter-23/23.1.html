MMK 23.1

saṃkalpaprabhavo rāgo dveṣo mohaś ca kathyate
śubhāśubhaviparyāsān saṃbhavanti pratītya hi || 1 ||

Passion, aversion, and delusion are said to arise from conceptual thought; for they arise in dependence on the good, the bad, and mistaken views.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

saṃkalpaprabhavo rāgo dveṣo mohaś ca kathyate | śubhāśubhaviparyāsān saṃbhavanti pratītya hi || 1 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Here an opponent says: “The continuity of existence certainly exists, because its cause exists. For, here, action proceeds from the afflictions. A succession of birth and death, whose causes are action and afflictions, arises; and that is called the continuity of existence. The principal cause of it is the afflictions, because, for those whose afflictions have been abandoned, there is no continuity of existence. And those afflictions—passion and the rest—exist. Therefore the succession of birth and death, which is their result, and the continuity of existence will also continue without interruption.”

We reply: the continuity of existence would exist if the afflictions that are its cause existed. But they do not exist. How so? Here, the Blessed Buddhas, destroyers of the enemy that is the affliction of beings throughout the whole three worlds, victors in battle over the four Māra-enemies, say:

“Passion, aversion, and delusion are said to arise from conceptual thought; for they arise in dependence on the good, the bad, and mistaken views.”

Conceptual thought is discursiveness. What arises from conceptual thought is “arising from conceptual thought.” Because a verse says: “Desire, I know your root: indeed, you are born from conceptual thought. I will not conceptualize you; therefore you will not exist for me.”

“Passion, aversion, and delusion are said to arise from conceptual thought.” Because these are the root of the other afflictions and are primary, only these three are taken up. And these three afflictions:

“Arise in dependence on the good, the bad, and mistaken views.” There, passion arises in dependence on a pleasing appearance; aversion arises in dependence on an unpleasing appearance; delusion arises in dependence on mistaken views. But conceptual thought is the common cause in the arising of all three. How, then, is delusion born of conceptual thought? We reply: in dependent arising the Blessed One said: “Monks, ignorance too has a cause, has a condition, has a basis. And what is the cause of ignorance, monks? Inappropriate attention is the cause of ignorance, monks; turbid attention, attention born from delusion, is the cause of ignorance, monks.” Therefore ignorance is born from conceptual thought.
