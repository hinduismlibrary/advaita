MMK 16.9

nirvāsyāmy anupādāno nirvāṇaṃ me bhaviṣyati
iti yeṣāṃ grahas teṣām upādānamahāgrahaḥ || 9 ||

“I shall be released without appropriation; nirvāṇa will be mine.” For those who hold this, there is a great grasping of appropriation.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

nirvāsyāmy anupādāno nirvāṇaṃ me bhaviṣyati | iti yeṣāṃ grahas teṣām upādānamahāgrahaḥ || 9 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Here it is said: If saṃsāra and nirvāṇa themselves have been denied by you, and bondage and release also denied, then for those wishing to be released from saṃsāra, this skillful, great desire for Dharma, which is the highest consolation, is produced: “When indeed shall I, without appropriation, be released? When will nirvāṇa be mine?” Surely that becomes entirely useless. And also the sequence for attaining nirvāṇa, for those who have produced such a skillful, pure, and great desire for Dharma—service to a good friend, giving, morality, hearing, thinking, and meditation—surely that too would be useless. The response:

“For whoever, among all entities that are without own-nature in this way, resembling a reflection, a mirage, water, a firebrand-circle, a dream, and a magical illusion, and devoid of the nature of self and what belongs to self, by following mere inversion, produces that very view of the existent group through adopting the activity of making ‘I’ and making ‘mine,’ and thinks:”

“I shall be released without appropriation, free from all appropriations; for me, who has proceeded in this way, nirvāṇa will come to be”—for those seekers of release who have this grasping, that very appropriation of the view of the existent group, called making ‘I’ and making ‘mine,’ becomes a great grasping. And peace cannot be expected for those settled in a great grasping of this kind.

As long as there is adherence to the grasping “I” and “mine” for the sake of attaining release through the complete abandoning of grasping; as long as there is adherence to the grasping that something called nirvāṇa exists; and as long as there is adherence to abandoning appropriation, for so long all endeavors certainly become useless for those who seek nirvāṇa by a means that is no means. Therefore all this is to be abandoned by one seeking release. As stated by the Blessed One in the noble Dhāyitamusti Sūtra:

“Then the Blessed One said this to the young Mañjuśrī: ‘Because they do not see the real meaning of the four noble truths, beings, their minds reversed by four inversions, do not pass beyond this unreal saṃsāra in this way.’ When this was said, the young Mañjuśrī said this to the Blessed One: ‘Let the Blessed One teach: through apprehending what do beings not pass beyond saṃsāra?’ The Blessed One said: ‘Mañjuśrī, beings do not pass beyond saṃsāra through apprehending self and what belongs to self. What is the reason for this? Mañjuśrī, for whoever sees self and other, constructions of karma occur. Mañjuśrī, the foolish, unlearned ordinary person, not understanding all dharmas as completely extinguished, apprehends self and other. Having apprehended, he clings. Having clung, he becomes attached, hostile, and confused. Being attached, hostile, and confused, he constructs three kinds of karma by body, speech, and mind. He imagines through false superimposition: “I am attached; I am hostile; I am confused.” For him, gone forth in the Tathāgata’s teaching, it occurs thus: “I am one of morality; I am a celibate. I shall pass beyond saṃsāra. I shall attain nirvāṇa. I shall be released from sufferings.” He imagines: “These dharmas are wholesome; these dharmas are unwholesome; these dharmas are to be abandoned; these dharmas are to be directly realized; suffering is to be fully known; arising is to be abandoned; cessation is to be directly realized; the path is to be cultivated.” He imagines: “All formations are impermanent; all formations are burning. Would that I flee from all formations!” As he observes in this way, mental attention accompanied by disenchantment arises, preceded by the signless. It occurs to him thus: “This is the full understanding of suffering; this is the full understanding of these dharmas.”’”

“It occurs to him thus: ‘Would that I abandon arising!’ He is distressed by these dharmas, ashamed, recoils, is disgusted, is frightened, is alarmed, comes to fear. It occurs to him thus: ‘This is the direct realization of these dharmas. This is abandonment of arising: this distress, disgust toward these dharmas.’ It occurs to him: ‘Cessation is to be directly realized.’ Having imagined arising, he recognizes cessation. It occurs to him thus: ‘This is the direct realization of cessation.’ It occurs to him: ‘Perhaps I should cultivate the path.’ He, having gone alone into seclusion, attending to those dharmas in mind, obtains calm. Through that mental attention accompanied by disenchantment, calm arises. His mind contracts, turns away, and withdraws with regard to all dharmas; he is distressed by them, ashamed, and a mind of non-delighting arises. It occurs to him thus: ‘I am released from all suffering. There is nothing further to be done by me. I am an Arhat.’ Thus he recognizes himself. At the time of dying, he sees the arising of himself. Doubt and uncertainty about the awakening of the Buddha arise in him. Having fallen into doubt, when he dies he falls into the great hells. What is the reason? Because, having imagined all dharmas as unborn in this way, he produces doubt and uncertainty concerning the Tathāgata.’”

“Then the young Mañjuśrī said this to the Blessed One: ‘How, Blessed One, are the four noble truths to be seen?’ The Blessed One said: ‘Mañjuśrī, by whom all formations are seen as unborn, suffering is fully known. By whom all dharmas are seen as not arisen, arising is abandoned. By whom all dharmas are seen as completely extinguished, cessation is directly realized. By whom all dharmas are seen as utterly empty, the path is cultivated. Mañjuśrī, whoever sees the four noble truths in this way does not imagine, does not conceptualize: “These dharmas are wholesome; these dharmas are unwholesome; these dharmas are to be abandoned; these dharmas are to be directly realized; suffering is to be fully known; arising is to be abandoned; cessation is to be directly realized; the path is to be cultivated.” What is the reason? For he does not see that dharma which he would imagine. But foolish ordinary people, imagining these dharmas, become attached, hostile, and confused. He does not bring forward nor set aside any dharma. As he does not bring forward or set aside in this way, his mind does not adhere in the three realms; he sees the entire three realms as unborn.’ Thus, in detail.”
