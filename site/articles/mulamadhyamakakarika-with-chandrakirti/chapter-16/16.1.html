MMK 16.1

Investigation of Bondage and Freedom

MMK 16.1

saṃskārāḥ = formations; saṃsaranti = transmigrate; cet = if; na = not; nityāḥ = permanent; saṃsaranti = transmigrate; te = they; saṃsaranti = transmigrate; ca = and; na = not; anityāḥ = impermanent; sattve = in existence; api = also; eṣaḥ = this; samaḥ = same; kramaḥ = sequence

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

saṃskārāḥ saṃsaranti cen na nityāḥ saṃsaranti te | saṃsaranti ca nānityāḥ sattve 'py eṣa samaḥ kramaḥ || 1 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

An opponent argues that own-nature exists because saṃsāra exists: if nothing had a nature, what could move from one life to another? Candrakīrti replies that if saṃsāra existed, it would have to belong either to conditioned formations or to a sentient being. If formations transmigrated, they would be either permanent or impermanent. Permanent things do not act or move; impermanent things vanish as soon as they arise, so a vanished formation cannot travel anywhere.

The opponent proposes a continuing stream of impermanent moments linked by cause and effect. Candrakīrti examines the relation between earlier and later moments: they cannot be identical, since cause and result are distinct; nor can they be wholly different, since then even an awakened person could transmigrate and a flame could pass from one lamp to another without continuity. A later moment cannot arise from a cause already destroyed, from one that has not ceased, or from a cause and result existing simultaneously. The same dilemma applies if a person, rather than formations, is said to transmigrate. Neither can be established as a saṃsārin.
