MMK 16.1

saṃskārāḥ saṃsaranti cen na nityāḥ saṃsaranti te
saṃsaranti ca nānityāḥ sattve 'py eṣa samaḥ kramaḥ || 1 ||

If formations transmigrate, they are not permanent; and impermanent ones do not transmigrate. Even for a being, this same sequence applies.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

saṃskārāḥ saṃsaranti cen na nityāḥ saṃsaranti te | saṃsaranti ca nānityāḥ sattve 'py eṣa samaḥ kramaḥ || 1 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Here it is said: Own-being of entities indeed exists because saṃsāra exists. Here, going from one course of existence to another is called saṃsāra, transmigration. If entities had no own-being, of what would going from one course to another be the saṃsāra? For transmigration of nonexistent formations, like the sons of a barren woman, is not seen. Therefore, because saṃsāra exists, own-being of entities indeed exists. The response: Own-being of entities would exist if saṃsāra itself existed. But it does not. Here, if there were saṃsāra, it would necessarily be either of formations or of a being. What follows from that? A fault in either way. He says:

If it is imagined that formations transmigrate, do they transmigrate as permanent or as impermanent? There, they do not transmigrate as permanent, because they are inactive, while activity is observed in impermanent things such as pots and the like. Or if they are impermanent, those that are inactive are destroyed immediately upon arising. And those that are destroyed, where do they go, being nonexistent like the formations of a barren woman’s sons? In this way, there is no saṃsāra even for impermanent things.

Or it may be said: Formations, though impermanent and existent, proceeding without interruption in a series of cause-and-fruit relations, and continuing as a continuum, transmigrate. This too is not reasonable. Why? The effect that arises does not transmigrate, because it does not come from somewhere and go somewhere. The cause that has perished does not transmigrate either, because it does not come from somewhere and go somewhere. Apart from formations alone, the past and future are not established, because they are nonexistent as destroyed and not yet arisen.

If it should be said: When the later moment has arisen, the earlier moment transmigrates. This would be so if the earlier and later moments were one. But they are not one, because there is a cause-and-effect relation, as with eye, form, and eye-consciousness, which are not one. Moreover, if they were one, the designations “earlier moment” and “later moment” would not exist. For one Devadatta is not called both earlier and later at one time. Likewise here, because of oneness, there would be no designation of earlier and later moments. Moreover, the earlier moment would not be destroyed, because it would not differ from the later moment. The later moment would not have arisen, because it would not differ from the earlier moment. Or, if transmigration occurred when they are different, then even Arhats would transmigrate, because another, an ordinary person, exists as a saṃsāra-producing one; and a quenched lamp would be burning in another lamp. Moreover, would the later moment arise from the earlier moment when destroyed, when not destroyed, or while being destroyed? There, if it is accepted that it arises from what is destroyed, then a sprout would arise even from a seed burned by fire, and it would then be without a cause. Or if from what is not destroyed, even so, a sprout would arise from a seed unchanged; there would be simultaneity of cause and effect, and arising would be without a cause. If it is said “while being destroyed,” there is no “being destroyed” apart from destroyed and not destroyed; and because the faults stated apply to destroyed and not destroyed, there is no arising from what is being destroyed either. From where, then, will there be an arrangement of cause and effect or an arrangement of earlier and later moments? And when there is no arrangement of earlier and later moments and no arrangement of cause and effect, there is no continuum either. Since that is absent, there is no saṃsāra for you. Thus there is no saṃsāra even for impermanent formations.

Some explain: It is true that formations do not transmigrate, because they are opposed to arising. Rather, a being transmigrates. The response: The same sequence applies even to a being. When it is examined whether a being transmigrates as permanent or impermanent, the very sequence of reasoning by which transmigration of formations is not established applies equally to a being. Therefore a being too does not transmigrate.
