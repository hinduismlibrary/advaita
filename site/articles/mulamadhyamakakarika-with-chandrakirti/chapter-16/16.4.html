MMK 16.4

saṃskārāṇāṃ na nirvāṇaṃ kathaṃcid upapadyate
sattvasyāpi na nirvāṇaṃ kathaṃcid upapadyate || 4 ||

Nirvāṇa of formations is not possible in any way. Nirvāṇa of a being also is not possible in any way.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

saṃskārāṇāṃ na nirvāṇaṃ kathaṃcid upapadyate | sattvasyāpi na nirvāṇaṃ kathaṃcid upapadyate || 4 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Here it is said: Saṃsāra indeed exists because its counteragent exists. Here, what does not exist has no counteragent, for example, a barren woman’s son. And nirvāṇa is the counteragent of saṃsāra. Therefore saṃsāra exists. The response: Saṃsāra would exist if nirvāṇa, its counteragent, existed. But it does not, he says:

If something called nirvāṇa existed, would it be imagined for formations that are permanent or for those that are impermanent? There, what would nirvāṇa do to permanent, unchanging formations? And what would nirvāṇa do to impermanent ones, which do not exist? Everything is the same as before. “In no way” means in no manner.

Or if nirvāṇa of a being is imagined, that too is not established, as before, whether the being is permanent or impermanent.

Or if it is imagined as inexpressible in terms of permanence and impermanence, then in that case the self is accepted to exist in nirvāṇa as in saṃsāra. Moreover, only the self with appropriation can properly be inexpressible. And since there is no appropriation in nirvāṇa, from where is its inexpressibility? Or let inexpressibility of identity or difference of the self be accepted. But is it existent in nirvāṇa or not existent? If existent, then because it exists even in liberation, it would be permanent. Or if non-existent, then the self would be impermanent. Thus, just as it is inexpressible in terms of identity and difference, it would not be inexpressible in terms of permanence and impermanence either. Or if it is accepted that even in nirvāṇa the self is inexpressible in terms of existence and non-existence, even so, is it knowable or not? If it is knowable, then it is not a self without appropriation, since it is knowable in nirvāṇa as in saṃsāra. Or if it is not known there, because it has a nature that cannot be known, like a sky-flower it certainly does not exist; from where, then, its inexpressibility? In this way, nirvāṇa too does not exist. Because it does not exist, saṃsāra does not exist. For this very reason it is said in the Blessed One’s Aṣṭasāhasrikā:

“Even nirvāṇa, venerable Subhūti, is like an illusion, like a dream. The qualities of a Buddha, venerable Subhūti, are like an illusion, like a dream. If, noble son, there were any dharma greater than nirvāṇa, I would say of that too: ‘It is like an illusion, like a dream.’”

And in the noble Samādhirāja:

“Ultimate truth is equal to a dream; nirvāṇa goes beyond as a dream. The wise mind that goes beyond in this way—this is said to be the highest restraint of mind.”

And likewise:

“The truth of cessation is just like a dream; and nirvāṇa is of the nature of a dream. The bodhisattva who has gone beyond by this speech is indeed called one who restrains speech.”
