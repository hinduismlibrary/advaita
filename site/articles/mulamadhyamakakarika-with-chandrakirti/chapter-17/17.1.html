MMK 17.1

ātmasaṃyamakaṃ cetaḥ parānugrāhakaṃ ca yat
maitraṃ sa dharmas tadbījaṃ phalasya pretya ceha ca || 1 ||

Whatever mind is self-restraining, helpful to others, and friendly—that is dharma, the seed of fruit both after death and here.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

ātmasaṃyamakaṃ cetaḥ parānugrāhakaṃ ca yat | maitraṃ sa dharmas tadbījaṃ phalasya pretya ceha ca || 1 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Here it is said: Saṃsāra indeed exists, because it is the basis for the connection of action and fruit. If here transmigration of formations or of the self were to take place in a sequence of unbroken continuity, in a succession of births and deaths, through the operation of cause and fruit, then there would be a connection of action and fruit. But in the absence of saṃsāra as described, because mind perishes immediately after arising and because the ripening is absent at the time when action projects it, there would only be an absence of the connection of action and fruit. But when saṃsāra exists, because there is a connection of the fruit of an action done here with its ripening even in another birth, the connection of actions with fruit is not contradicted. Therefore saṃsāra indeed exists, because it is the basis for the connection of action and fruit. Now, wishing to set out the division of which those actions are and what their fruit is, he says this:

“Whatever mind is self-restraining, helpful to others, and friendly—that is dharma, the seed of fruit both after death and here.”

There, that in which the conceit “I am” has been placed, produced, is the self. The person designated by taking up the aggregates is called the self. “Cetas” is that which accumulates, builds up wholesome and unwholesome action, and determines the capacity to give ripening. Mind, thought, and consciousness are synonyms for that same thing. Because it restrains the self, makes it not independent with respect to sense objects, and stops activity through the power of afflictions such as passion, it is self-restraining. That wholesome mind which is self-restraining, which holds back activity such as taking life and the rest, and which holds one back from going to a bad destiny, is therefore called dharma.

In this teaching the word “dharma” is established in three ways: in the sense of bearing its own characteristic; in the sense of restraining movement to a bad destiny; and in the sense of restraining movement in saṃsāra of the five destinies. There, in the sense of bearing its own characteristic, all contaminated and uncontaminated things are called dharmas. In the sense of restraining movement to a bad destiny, things such as the ten wholesome actions and the rest are called dharmas:

“One who practices dharma sleeps happily in this world and in the next.”

In the sense of restraining movement in saṃsāra of the five destinies, nirvāṇa is called dharma, as in “I go to the Dharma for refuge.” But here the word “dharma” is intended only in the sense of restraining movement to a bad destiny.

Is only self-restraining mind dharma? No, he says. What then? Whatever mind is helpful to others and friendly is also dharma. Here the word “and” with “friendly” is to be understood as stated by omission. There, the mind that greatly helps, the mind that is engaged in the four means of gathering, and the mind that is engaged in protecting from fear is also dharma. The mind that is friendly, without hostility toward beings, is friendly mind. What mind is friendly is friendly mind, or in this way, friendly. And this mind, indicated as threefold, is called dharma. By inversion, “non-dharma” is to be joined.

And this mind, whose division has been stated, is the seed of fruit. That which is the cause that is not shared in the production of fruit is called seed alone, just as the seed of rice for the sprout of rice. But earth and the rest, which are shared, are not the seed; they are causes. As in this case, here too, for the production of a desired ripening, mind of three kinds is the seed. But effort and the rest are only causes.

At what time does the fruit of the seed ripen? He says: “After death and here.” “After death” means in an unseen birth; “here” means in a seen birth. And this is to be understood at length from authoritative text.
