MMK 17.33

Investigation of Actions and Fruits

← MMK 17.32Chapter 17MMK 18.1 →

MMK 17.33

kleśāḥ karmāṇi dehāś ca kartāraś ca phalāni ca
gandharvanagarākārā marīcisvapnasaṃnibhāḥ || 33 ||

Afflictions, actions, bodies, agents, and fruits have the form of a city of gandharvas and resemble mirages and dreams.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

kleśāḥ karmāṇi dehāś ca kartāraś ca phalāni ca | gandharvanagarākārā marīcisvapnasaṃnibhāḥ || 33 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

And not only does seeing the absence of own-nature become possible through the example of emanation; but, further, showing that the absence of own-nature of entities is clearly to be understood from these examples, he says:

“Afflictions, actions, bodies, agents, and fruits have the form of a city of gandharvas and resemble mirages and dreams.”

There, afflictions are passion and the rest, because they afflict the continuum of the minds of beings. Actions are wholesome, unwholesome, and imperturbable. Bodies are bodies. Agents are selves. Fruits are ripening, sovereign, effluent, and the rest. These things, afflictions and the rest, are to be understood as without own-nature, like a city of gandharvas and the rest. Therefore it should be understood that, because the Madhyamikas alone do not accept own-nature in entities, the consequence of the two views of permanence and annihilation does not follow.

And here, in examining the connection of action and fruit, the remaining refutation of false objections is to be understood at length from the Madhyamakāvatāra.

As stated in the noble Ratnakūṭa Sūtra:

“Five hundred monks, who had attained meditation, rose from their seats and went away, not understanding, not entering, not penetrating, and not becoming convinced of this profound teaching of Dharma. The Blessed One said: ‘Kāśyapa, these monks are conceited. They do not enter, penetrate, or become convinced of this undefiled purity of discipline; they are frightened, terrified, and seized by terror. Kāśyapa, the production of verses is profound, and the awakening of the Buddhas, the Blessed Ones, is profound. It cannot be understood by beings who have not planted wholesome roots, who are taken in hand by bad friends, and who are much given to lack of conviction.

“‘Moreover, Kāśyapa, these five hundred monks were hearers of other sects in the teaching of the Tathāgata Kāśyapa. They heard this teaching of Dharma from the presence of that Tathāgata Kāśyapa with the intention of objecting. Having heard it, they obtained clarity of mind for a single moment. Their speech was thus: “Marvelous indeed is the Tathāgata Kāśyapa, the Arhat, the Fully Awakened One, who speaks sweetly and pleasantly.” Having obtained that clarity of mind for a single moment, they died and were born among the Thirty-three gods. Having passed away from there, they were born here. And for that very reason they went forth in my teaching. Kāśyapa, these five hundred monks are those who have become established in views. They do not enter, penetrate, or become convinced of this profound teaching of Dharma; they are frightened, terrified, and seized by terror. But this preparation has been made for them by this teaching of Dharma. They will not again go to a fall into a bad destiny, and with these very aggregates they will become completely extinguished.’

“Then the Blessed One addressed the venerable Subhūti: ‘Go, Subhūti, summon these monks.’ Subhūti said: ‘They contradict what has been spoken by the Blessed One himself; what then of me?’ Then, at that time, the Blessed One created two emanated monks on the road along which those monks were going. Those five hundred monks approached by the road along which those two emanated monks had gone. Having approached, they said: ‘Where are you going, venerables?’ The two said: ‘We are going to forest dwellings. There we shall dwell in the experiences of the bliss of meditation.’ …

“Those five hundred monks too said this: ‘Venerables, we too do not enter, penetrate, or become convinced of the Blessed One’s teaching of Dharma; we are frightened, terrified, and seized by terror. Therefore we shall dwell in forest dwellings in the experiences of the bliss of meditation.’ The emanated monks said: ‘Then, venerables, let us discuss; let us not dispute. For non-dispute is the supreme dharma of a monk. When you venerables speak of complete nirvāṇa, what is that dharma that will become completely extinguished? Is there here some body, self, being, living being, creature, person, individual, human, or man that will become completely extinguished? Or through the destruction of what is there complete nirvāṇa?’ They said: ‘Complete nirvāṇa is through the destruction of passion, hatred, and confusion.’ The emanated monks said: ‘Do passion, hatred, and confusion exist for you, which you will destroy?’ They said: ‘They are not found inwardly, outwardly, or between both; nor do they arise when not imagined.’ …”

Thus ends the seventeenth chapter, called “Investigation of Action and Fruit,” in the Madhyamaka commentary, the Prasannapadā, composed by the Teacher Candrakīrti.
