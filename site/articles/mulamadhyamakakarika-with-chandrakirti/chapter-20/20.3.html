MMK 20.3

hetośca pratyayānāṃ ca sāmagryāmasti cetphalam
gṛhyeta nanu sāmagryāṃ sāmagryāṃ ca na gṛhyate || 3 ||

If the fruit exists in the collection of cause and conditions, it should indeed be apprehended in the collection; but it is not apprehended in the collection.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

hetośca pratyayānāṃ ca sāmagryāmasti cetphalam | gṛhyeta nanu sāmagryāṃ sāmagryāṃ ca na gṛhyate || 3 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

And further:

“If the fruit exists in the collection of cause and conditions, it should indeed be apprehended in the collection; but it is not apprehended in the collection.” || 3 ||

Whatever exists in a place is apprehended there, as yogurt in a pot. And whatever does not exist in a place is not apprehended there, as oil in grains of sand, or a crest-jewel on a frog’s hair.

An opponent might say: “Even existing objects are not apprehended because of extreme subtlety, extreme closeness, extreme distance, damage to the sense faculty, obstruction by a material object, disappearance within a material object, the mind’s not being engaged, as with an atom, a collyrium applicator at the eye, the movement of the sun, each single hair seen by one with eye-disease, forms and sounds for the blind and deaf, a pot and the like obstructed by a wall and the like, the bodies of accomplished beings, gods, and ghosts, and an object not being apprehended by someone whose mind is engaged with another object.” What, then, is the sign of the existence of these things that are not apprehended, by which there would be non-apprehension even though they existed? If it is said that their existence is grasped by inference, analogy, and scripture, then it must not be said that they are not apprehended, since they are apprehended by inference and the like. If it is said, “What is apprehensible by the visual sense, even while existing, is not apprehended through these causes,” it is replied: Did we say this—that what exists would be apprehended by the visual senses? What, then? We say only this generally: “It should indeed be apprehended in the collection.”

And further: “If a thing does not exist in a place, it does not arise from that”—like oil from grains of sand. But a result arises from a collection; therefore, by inference, the fruit exists in the collection. It is replied: “What exists in a place does not arise from that”—as yogurt from a pot. Since by this inference too its existence is not reasonable, why is it not understood that the fruit does not exist in the collection?

An opponent might say: “Because inference contradicts both positions, just as its existence is not reasonable, so its non-existence too is not reasonable.” It is replied: We do not establish its non-existence; rather, we refute the existence of it imagined by the others. In this way, we do not establish its existence; rather, we remove its non-existence imagined by the others. For what we wish to demonstrate is the middle way, having avoided both extremes. And it was said in the Śataka composed by the noble Āryadeva:

“The adornment of pillars and the like is useless for the purpose of a house, for one who accepts only an already-existing effect, and for one who accepts only a non-existing effect.”

Thus it is established that a fruit is not produced from a collection, because the consequence would be that what exists is apprehended. || 3 ||
