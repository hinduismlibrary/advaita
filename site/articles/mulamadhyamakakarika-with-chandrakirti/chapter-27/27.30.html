MMK 27.30

I bow down to Gautama, whose kindness holds one close, who revealed the sublime dharma in order to let go of all views.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

sarvadṛṣṭiprahāṇāya yaḥ saddharmam adeśayat | anukampām upādāya taṃ namasyāmi gautamam || 30 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Out of compassion, Gautama taught the true Dharma in order to abandon every view. “Dharma” is that which sustains and protects; the “true Dharma” is the teaching of the noble ones who have accomplished their task, or, more simply, the excellent teaching praised because it brings the suffering of saṃsāra to an end.

Gautama taught dependent arising as unborn and unceased, not annihilated and not eternal, neither one thing nor many, neither coming nor going: the auspicious quelling of conceptual proliferation. He did so through great compassion, wishing to free beings from all views, as one cherishes beings of the three realms even more than an only child. He sought no gain, honor, or repayment. I bow to that unsurpassed, incomparable teacher, Gautama, born into the lineage of the great seers.

Candrakīrti closes by citing the Ārya Śālistamba Sūtra: whoever continually sees dependent arising with correct wisdom—as it is, without distortion, without a living self, unborn, unmade, unconditioned, unobstructed, unimpeded, peaceful, fearless, unassailable, imperishable, and not subject to cessation—sees it as empty, insubstantial, impermanent, suffering, and without self. Such a person does not turn back toward questions about the past, the future, or the present, nor toward views about a self, a living being, or ritual observances. These views are abandoned, understood, and cut off at the root, like a palm tree whose crown has been severed, so that they will not arise again.

The sūtra’s narrative then concludes: venerable Śāriputra rejoiced in Maitreya’s words, rose from his seat, and departed; the monks departed as well.
