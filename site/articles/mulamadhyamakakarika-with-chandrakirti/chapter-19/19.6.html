MMK 19.6

bhāvaṃ pratītya kālaścetkālo bhāvādṛte kutaḥ
na ca kaścana bhāvo 'sti kutaḥ kālo bhaviṣyati || 6 ||

If time exists depending on an entity, where is time without an entity? And no entity at all exists; whence will time come to be?

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

bhāvaṃ pratītya kālaścetkālo bhāvādṛte kutaḥ | na ca kaścana bhāvo 'sti kutaḥ kālo bhaviṣyati || 6 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

An opponent says: “It is true that no permanent time exists with an own-nature established independently of form and the like. Rather, time is designated in dependence on conditioned formations such as form themselves, and is expressed as a moment and so forth. Therefore there is no fault.” It is replied: Even so:

“If time exists depending on an entity, where is time without an entity? And no entity at all exists; whence will time come to be?” || 6 ||

If you establish that time exists depending on an entity, then, when an entity is absent, time that has that as its cause is certainly absent too. And because “no entity at all exists” has been explained at length before, and because of the denials that will be stated, when no entity exists in reality, then:

“Whence will time come to be?” || 6 ||

And because time is absent, the divisions of time—moments, instants, muhūrtas, and so on—which are transformations of it, do not exist. Therefore how will time be established through having transformations? Thus it is that entities have no own-nature. For it was said by the Blessed One in the Ārya-Hastikakṣya Sūtra:

“If any dharma had an own-nature, the Victor together with his disciples would go there. If dharmas were fixed, there would be no nirvāṇa; the wise would never become free of proliferation.”

And further:

“Hundreds and thousands of Buddhas have passed; having spoken hundreds and thousands of dharmas, neither the Dharma nor a syllable has been exhausted; from that, there is no arising of an unexhausted syllable.”

And further:

“At the arising-time of the Tathāgata, one named Maitreya will be here in the future; this earth will be covered with gold. From where does that coming of his come now?”

“The five strands of sensual pleasure are intensifications, wandering, deluding, and of a thieving nature. As water in a mirage at noontime in summer, so are sensual pleasures.”

“In one eon the world would be sky-bodied, of the nature of the sky; the terrifying ones undergo burning and destruction. From where did they come, and where is their going?”

And just as follows: “These five are merely designation, merely assertion, merely convention, merely worldly covering,” namely, the past time, the future time, space, nirvāṇa, and the person. ||

Thus ends the nineteenth chapter, called “Examination of Time,” in the Madhyamaka Commentary, the Clear Words, composed by the venerable Ācārya Candrakīrti.
