MMK 18.7

nivṛttamabhidhātavyaṃ nivṛtte cittagocare
anutpannāniruddhā hi nirvāṇamiva dharmatā || 7 ||

What is to be spoken has ceased when the range of mind has ceased; the nature of dharmas, like nirvāṇa, is unborn and unceasing.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

nivṛttamabhidhātavyaṃ nivṛtte cittagocare | anutpannāniruddhā hi nirvāṇamiva dharmatā || 7 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Here it is said: If the Buddhas, the Blessed Ones, did not teach “self” and did not teach “non-self,” what then did they teach? The response:

“What is to be spoken has ceased when the range of mind has ceased; the nature of dharmas, like nirvāṇa, is unborn and unceasing.”

Here, if there were some entity to be spoken, it would be taught. But when what is to be spoken has ceased, there is no object of speech. Then nothing at all is taught by the Buddhas. Why is there nothing to be spoken? He says: “When the range of mind has ceased.” The range of mind is the object of mind; “range” means object, that is, support. If there were some range of mind, then, having superimposed some sign there, speech might proceed. But when the object of mind itself is impossible, where would there be superimposition of a sign by which speech could proceed? Why, then, is there no object of mind? He says:

“For the nature of dharmas, like nirvāṇa, is unborn and unceasing.”

Because the nature of dharmas, the own-nature of dharmas, the nature of dharmas, is established as unborn and unceasing, like nirvāṇa, mind does not proceed there. And when mind does not proceed, where is superimposition of a sign? In its absence, where is speech? Therefore it is perfectly established that nothing at all is taught by the Buddhas, the Blessed Ones. For this very reason he will say:

“The calming of all apprehension, the calming of proliferation, is auspicious. No dharma whatsoever was taught by the Buddha to anyone anywhere.”

So be it.

Or this is another prior position: When it was said that proliferation ceases in emptiness, how does proliferation cease in emptiness? The response: “What is to be spoken has ceased,” and the rest is explained as before.

Or alternatively, when this was said before—“Through not finding internal and external entities, the complete exhaustion of making ‘I’ and ‘mine’ internally and externally is reality here”—what is that like? Is it possible to say what it is? Therefore:

“What is to be spoken has ceased when the range of mind has ceased.”

Here the words “in reality” are to be supplied. Why is what is to be spoken ceased in that reality, and why has the range of mind ceased? He says:

“For the nature of dharmas, like nirvāṇa, is unborn and unceasing.”

The earlier explanation alone is to be applied. For this reason it is said in the noble Tathāgataguhya Sūtra:

“Śāntamati, from the night when the Tathāgata awakened to unsurpassed, complete awakening until the night when he will enter final nirvāṇa, in that interval not even one syllable was uttered by the Tathāgata, was spoken, or will be spoken. How then did the Blessed One teach the teaching of Dharma in diverse ways to all the various beings to be tamed—gods, asuras, humans, kinnaras, siddhas, vidyādharas, serpents, and the rest? With only an utterance of speech in a single moment, it removes the darkness of the minds of each of those people, awakens the forest of many kinds of intelligence-lotuses, dries up the ocean-river of birth and death, and burns up the spread of rays of the seven suns of the fire at the end of the eon; it is a great radiance like the autumn sun.”

Thus in the sūtra:

“Just as a musical instrument made by a mechanism is played when moved by wind, and there is no player here, yet sounds emerge,

“in this way the speech of the Buddha, moved by the intentions of all beings because of prior complete purity, emerges, and there is no conception of it here. Sounds such as echoes are neither situated internally nor externally; in just this way the speech of the King of Men is neither situated internally nor externally.”

And likewise:

“A divine drum, unstruck, when the divine ripening of karma for the gods has ceased, releases its sound from the sky, knowing that the gods dwell heedlessly.

“All desires are impermanent, not permanent, fleeting, unstable, of the nature of foam. All existences are like illusion, mirage, the moon in water, and the nature of dreams.

“The drum, sounded by Śakra together with the Maruts, moves to the Dharma assembly. It proclaims a discourse of Dharma to the gods, a discourse that accords with peace and dispassion.”

And likewise in the noble Samādhirāja:

“When the Buddha becomes the Dharma King, the sage who illuminates all dharmas, the sound of the dharmas of non-existence will arise for grass, shrubs, trees, herbs, mountains, and rocks.

“As many sounds as there are in that world-realm, all are non-existents; there is not a single existent. Such is the sound that comes forth from that Tathāgata, the guide of the world.”

And so on, in detail.

And likewise:

“Your one voice, for the benefit of the world, comes forth as a different voice according to different inclinations. The Jina speaks to each one, speaks with a smile for the sake of the ordinary person.”

Here some raise an objection: The Madhyamikas are no different from nihilists, because they say that wholesome and unwholesome action, the agent, the fruit, and the entire world are empty of own-nature. For nihilists too say, “This does not exist.” Therefore the Madhyamikas are no different from nihilists. It is not so. Why? For the Madhyamikas teach dependent arising. Because everything, having reached causes and conditions, arises in dependence, they explain that everything, this world and the next, is without own-nature. The nihilists do not speak in this way. They do not accept the non-existence of another world and the rest because things are empty of own-nature through dependent arising. Rather, having apprehended the things of this life, they deny another world, seeing the passage from this world to the next as intrinsically real, and deny another thing similar to the thing apprehended in this world.

But, one might say, they too accept that what does not exist in its own form is non-existent; so is there not equality in this view? No. Why? Because the Madhyamikas accept existence conventionally, there is no equality. If one says there is equality in reality, although there is no establishment in reality for either, they are still unequal because of the difference in the one who understands.

For example, one man, not having correctly understood a man who committed theft, and urged by that man’s enemy, falsely declares, “This man committed theft.” Another, having seen it directly, censures him. There, although there is no difference in reality, because of the difference in understanding, the first is called a speaker of falsehood and the other a speaker of truth. And when examined correctly, disgrace and demerit fit one, not the other.

In just this way, although there is no difference in the own-nature of the thing, there is no equality in knowledge and speech between Madhyamikas, who know and explain the own-nature of things correctly, and nihilists, who do not know the own-nature of things correctly. Just as there is a great difference between one who is indifferent without reflection and one who is indifferent after reflection, between an ordinary person and an Arhat, and between a blind person and one with sight, although they are alike in their determination of a dangerous precipice, so there will be a difference between nihilists and Madhyamikas. So much for this digression; we shall explain the subject at hand.
