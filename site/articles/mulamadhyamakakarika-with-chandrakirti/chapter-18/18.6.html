MMK 18.6

ātmetyapi prajñapitamanātmetyapi deśitam
buddhairnātmā na cānātmā kaścidityapi deśitam || 6 ||

“Self” too was designated; “not-self” too was taught. By the Buddhas, “neither self nor non-self exists” was also taught.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

ātmetyapi prajñapitamanātmetyapi deśitam | buddhairnātmā na cānātmā kaścidityapi deśitam || 6 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Here it is said: If the truth has been established as the non-arising, internally and externally, of the nets of conceptualizing “I” and “mine” through not finding internal and external entities, then what of this which was said by the Blessed One:

“Self is indeed the protector of self; who else would be its protector? Through a well-tamed self, the wise one reaches heaven.”

And:

“Self is indeed the witness of self, of what has been done and what has not been done.”

And likewise in the noble Samādhirāja:

“Black and white action does not perish. Action done by oneself is to be experienced by oneself. There is no transfer of the fruit of action, and one does not experience it without a cause.”

And so on. How is that not contradictory? The response: Was this also not said by the Blessed One?

“There is no being or self here; these dharmas are with causes.”

And likewise: “Form is not self; self is not one possessing form; self is not in form; form is not in self.” In this way, up to: “Consciousness is not self; self is not one possessing consciousness; self is not in consciousness; consciousness is not in self.” And likewise: “All dharmas are not-self.” How, then, would the authoritative text not contradict the earlier authoritative text? Therefore the intention of the Blessed One must be sought.

But in general, in the teaching of the Blessed Buddhas, who are like suns, intent on awakening the lotus lake of the understanding of all the world’s beings to be tamed, with the extensive rays of great compassion, skillful means, and wisdom, in the many divisions of teaching whose meanings are to be drawn out and definitive, the intention is this:

“Self” too was designated; “not-self” too was taught. By the Buddhas, “neither self nor non-self exists” was also taught.

Here the intention is this: Those whose whole eye of understanding is covered by the dense darkness of bad views, the inversion about the absence of self, do not see the whole range of entities, even those not beyond the object of clear worldly vision. Remaining established in conventional truth, they devote themselves to describing merely the named elements earth, water, fire, and wind as reality. They describe cognitions as arising merely from the ripening of the great elements beginning with the embryo, like the special powers of drunkenness, fainting, and the like, which arise through the mere ripening of specific substances such as rice, water, and yeast, and which are observed in drinking liquor.

Engaged in denial of the past and future, they deny another world and self, saying: “This world does not exist; another world does not exist; there is no ripened fruit of actions, good or bad; there is no spontaneously arisen being,” and so on. Because they deny that, they turn away from the particular desired fruits distinguished as heaven and liberation; continually they undertake unwholesome actions and head toward the great precipice of hell and the rest.

To bring to an end their wrong view, the Blessed Buddhas, intent on fulfilling the vow undertaken to carry across the entire realm of beings, follow the intentions of beings divided into thousands of kinds of mental conduct, preceded by the accumulations of wisdom, skillful means, and great compassion. They are incomparable friends of the whole world and great physician-kings, healers of every affliction, the great disease. Wishing to guide trainees who are inferior, middling, and superior, in order to turn inferior trainees who perform unwholesome actions away from unwholesome things and the rest, the Blessed Buddhas have sometimes designated and established “self” in the world.

The reasoning that refutes the doctrine of causelessness is to be understood at length from the examination of action and agent, and from the Madhyamakāvatāra. Therefore no further effort is undertaken here for that refutation.

But those who, through a special prior practice, have ripened the seed obtained by faith in the profound Dharma, who are near nirvāṇa, who are superior trainees, free from affection for self, and able to penetrate the real meaning of the profound teaching of the Sage of Silence—having ascertained the special degree of their receptivity, the Buddhas taught:

“Neither self nor non-self exists.”

For just as seeing self is not reality, in the same way seeing non-self, which is its opposite, is also not reality. Thus it has been taught: “There is no self, and there is no non-self.” As stated in the noble Ratnakūṭa:

“‘Self,’ Kāśyapa, is one extreme; ‘non-self’ is the second extreme. That which is in the middle of these two extremes is formless, without indication, without support, without appearance, and not made known. Kāśyapa, this is called the middle way, the true examination of dharmas.”

And it is said in the noble Ratnāvalī:

“Neither self nor non-self is found according to reality. The Great Sage has removed from us the views made about self and non-self.

“What is seen and heard, the Sage has not declared true or false. For from a position there would be an opposite position, and neither of these two is real.”

Since the teaching of Dharma by the Blessed Buddhas proceeds through denying self, non-self, and both, according to the dispositions of trainees who are inferior, middling, and superior, there is no contradiction in the authoritative text of the Madhyamikas. For this reason noble Deva said:

“At first, the prevention of non-virtue; in the middle, the prevention of self; at the end, the prevention of everything. Whoever knows this is wise.”

And it is said by the Teacher:

“Just as a grammarian teaches the alphabet, so the Buddha taught Dharma to trainees according to their capacity.

“To some he taught Dharma for turning away from evils; to some for accomplishing merit; to some he taught what is determined as both.

“For some, Dharma is dependent on both; for some it is profound and terrifying; for some it is the means of awakening, with emptiness and compassion as its womb.”

Or alternatively, this is another meaning: “Self” was designated by the Sāṃkhyas and others, considering that, because formations perish at every moment, there is no connection between action and fruit. “Not-self” was designated by the Lokāyatas, who, not seeing a self that transmigrates, reason:

“A person is only as much as what is within the range of the senses. Good man, this is the wolf’s footprint, as those of much learning say.”

And so on. Those who entirely fail to see the nature of things such as self and non-self, conceived by foolish people as if they were hairs, gnats, and the like perceived by one whose eyes are diseased, were taught by the Buddhas:

“Neither self nor non-self exists.”

As stated in the noble Tathāgataguhya Sūtra:

“Then the bodhisattva Śāntamati said this to the Blessed One: ‘Lord, it is said “quieting, quieting.” What is this called quieting? Through the quieting of what is it called quieting?’ The Blessed One said: ‘Son of good family, “quieting” is a designation for the quieting of afflictions. “Quieting of afflictions” is a designation for the quieting of conception, conceptual distinction, and conceptualization. “Quieting of conception, conceptual distinction, and conceptualization” is a designation for the quieting of perception and mental attention. “Quieting of perception and mental attention” is a designation for the quieting of inversion. “Quieting of inversion” is a designation for the quieting of causes and objects. “Quieting of causes and objects” is a designation for the quieting of ignorance, becoming, and craving. “Quieting of ignorance, becoming, and craving” is a designation for the quieting of making “I” and making “mine.” “Quieting of making “I” and making “mine” is a designation for the quieting of the views of annihilation and permanence. “Quieting of the views of annihilation and permanence” is a designation for the quieting of the view of the existent group.’

“‘Śāntamati, all the defilements that are connected with an object, a cause, and a view arise from the view of the existent group. When the view of the existent group is quieted, all views are quieted. When all views are quieted, all aspirations are quieted. When all aspirations are quieted, all afflictions are quieted. Just as, Śāntamati, when the root of a tree is cut, all its branches, leaves, flowers, and fruits dry up, in the same way, Śāntamati, when the view of the existent group is quieted, all afflictions are quieted. When the view of the existent group is not fully understood, all the afflictions of appropriation arise. When the view of the existent group is fully understood, all the afflictions of appropriation do not arise and do not afflict.’”
