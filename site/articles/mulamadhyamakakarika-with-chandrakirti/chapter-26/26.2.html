MMK 26.2

vijñānaṃ saṃniviśate saṃskārapratyayaṃ gatau
saṃniviṣṭe 'tha vijñāne nāmarūpaṃ niṣicyate || 2 ||

Consciousness enters into the course of rebirth, conditioned by formations; when consciousness has entered, name-and-form is planted.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

vijñānaṃ saṃniviśate saṃskārapratyayaṃ gatau | saṃniviṣṭe 'tha vijñāne nāmarūpaṃ niṣicyate || 2 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Then, for him:

“Consciousness enters into the course of rebirth, conditioned by formations.”

For this person, who has made and accumulated formations, consciousness—having formations as its cause—enters, enters into, and arises in the course of existence that accords with the formations, the realm such as the body of a god. It is the seed of the harm of saṃsāra.

Then, later:

“When consciousness has entered, name-and-form is planted.”

There, that which names in this or that place of rebirth, pierced by karma and afflictions, is “name”; or that which names objects by the power of recognition is “name.” The four formless aggregates are designated “name.” “Form” is that which is formed, that is, obstructed. This form and the previously explained name, both together and in brief, are established as “name-and-form.”

There, when the aggregates at the time of death are ceasing, the aggregates belonging to rebirth arise, impelled according to karma, in that very same moment, just as an image and its reflection, or as a recitation, a lamp, a seal and its impression, and the like; and likewise by the example of the rising and falling of a balance beam. Thus dependent arising is established by the example of an image and its reflection, a seal and its impression, and the like. But the statement that consciousness “enters” by the example of the rising and falling of a balance beam is made in accordance with the understanding of childish people: the connection with a new existence occurs at the same time.

And likewise:

“May there always be the power, at the same time, to remove the world’s suffering and to produce happiness, just as the sun [has power] to remove darkness and produce light.”

But again, for those discerning the nature of dependent arising, it is not proper to speak in this way, by the example of association and the like, and by the example of the rising and falling of a balance beam in a single moment. For it is said:

“If this, approaching birth, is not existent, and what is arising, though existent, is approaching destruction and ceasing, then how is this like a balance? Birth without an agent is not reasonable.”

And so forth. Just as momentariness is not accepted by you on the basis of the example of an image and reflection, a seal and its impression, and the like, so too momentariness is not reasonable for any other existent thing that is destroyed immediately after arising.

For the Abhidharma text says that the four characteristics of the conditioned—birth, aging, abiding, and impermanence—occur in the very same moment in an existent thing arising, whether external or internal. But the wise do not accept that birth and aging, which have mutually different characteristics, or abiding and impermanence, occur together in a single existent thing.

For it is said in the text of the Madhyamaka doctrine:

“If [things are] momentary in every respect, how could there be any oldness? But if they are non-momentary because of stability, how could there be any oldness?

“Just as there is an end of a moment, so too let a beginning and a middle be conceived; because a moment has an end, the world does not abide for a moment.

“Beginning, middle, and end are to be considered in the same way as a moment; being beginning, middle, and end is not from itself or from another.”

From the recitation of this Madhyamaka doctrine, it is to be understood that momentary entities are not established.

Nor could birth and death, which have mutually different characteristics, occur in a single moment—just as cognition that doubts and cognition that determines, light and darkness, knowledge and ignorance, a seed and a sprout, and the state of dying and the state of rebirth have different characteristics. Only things established through their own causes and conditions, independent of one another, can reasonably coexist, like a cow’s right and left horns, the breasts of a young woman, or a man’s two ears; but never destruction and arising, which are mutually opposed.

As was said in the Examination of Passion and the Passionate Person:

“Co-arising of passion and the passionate person is not reasonable; for passion and the passionate person would be independent of one another.

“There is no coexistence in oneness, for that would not be together with itself; if they are separate, from where would coexistence come?

“In this way, passion is established with the passionate person neither together nor apart; like passion, all dharmas are established neither together nor apart.”

If this is refuted, how could the things, the dying state and the state of rebirth, be simultaneous? Therefore it is seen that their coexistence proceeds only in accordance with the understanding of those to be trained.

For this reason the rising and falling of a balance do not occur in precisely the same moment, since the rising and falling occur at different times. For the text says that sixty-five moments pass in just the snap of a strong man’s fingers, and gives the example of piercing one hundred thousand lotus leaves with the tip of a needle. Even there, the piercing of one hundred thousand lotus leaves with a needle is to be understood as occurring sequentially, because moments are extremely subtle, just as the syllables and words of a verse are recited in a single moment.

And further:

“Without cessation, without arising, without annihilation, not eternal; not one in meaning, not different in meaning, not coming, not going: dependent arising is the calming of proliferation, peace.”

From this recitation, the treatise Madhyamaka has itself shown that arising and cessation do not occur. And in the sūtras of the collections:

“The world is indestructible, unborn, equal to the dharma-element; and he teaches the realm of beings—this is conformity to the world.

“He does not apprehend the nature of beings in the three times; and he teaches the realm of beings—this is conformity to the world.”

And likewise:

“Form is like a mass of foam; feeling is like a bubble; perception is like a mirage; formations are like the trunk of a plantain tree; consciousness is said by the Kinsman of the Sun to be like an illusion.

“A monk who examines dharmas in this way, energetic, whether by day or by night, clearly knowing and mindful, would penetrate the peaceful state, the calming of formations, the peace.”

These verses are recited in all the sūtras and treatises of the collections. Since masses of foam and the rest, having met a collection of causes and conditions, have arisen dependently and are devoid of an essential thing, how could there be a consideration of momentary or non-momentary?

And in the Mahāyāna sūtras:

“All the realms of existence are like a dream; no one is born, nor does anyone die. Karma is never lost; when done, it gives its fruit, black or white, for one who wanders in saṃsāra.

“There is neither permanence nor annihilation; there is no accumulation of karma, nor any abiding. Nor does that very one, having acted, experience it again; nor does another, having acted, experience it again.

“As a young woman in a dream sees her son born and dead, overjoyed at his birth and distressed at his death, so should you know all dharmas to be like that.

“Just as separate wholesome and unwholesome actions proceed because a written mark is seen in another village, there is no transfer of the writing: know all dharmas to be like that.

“An impression is seen from a seal, but no transfer of the seal is apprehended; it is neither there nor elsewhere: thus formations are neither annihilated nor eternal.

“As a sprout comes from an existent seed, yet is not that seed; it is neither another from it nor that very thing: such is the dharma-nature, without annihilation and without permanence.

“Just as a rope is twisted with the strength of effort, depending on muñja and balbaja grass, and a waterwheel with its wheel revolves, while no single one of them revolves, so all the limbs of existence proceed, resting on one another’s increase; in each one of them no prior or later limit of the revolving is apprehended.”

For this very reason the venerable Ācārya Nāgārjuna said:

“The linking of aggregates, without transmigration, is to be understood by the wise through recitation, lamp, seal, mirror, echo, sunstone, seed, and sour fruit.”

And in the Śataka Treatise, the Noble Āryadeva, standing in the course of the stability of the great conduct of awakening, said:

“Existence is like a magic fire-circle, a construction, a dream, an illusion, a reflection in water, smoke, an echo, a mirage, and a cloud.”

Thus, by the example of an image and reflection and the like, when consciousness has coalesced in the mother’s womb, name-and-form is planted with consciousness as its condition; that is, it flows forth and appears. If consciousness had not coalesced in this course of rebirth, the appearance of name-and-form would not occur.

For it is said: “If, Ānanda, consciousness were not to enter the mother’s womb, that embryo would not develop into an embryo.”
