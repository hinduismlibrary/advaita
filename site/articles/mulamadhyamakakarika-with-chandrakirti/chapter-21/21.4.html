MMK 21.4

bhaviṣyati kathaṃ nāma saṃbhavo vibhavaṃ vinā
anityatā hi bhāveṣu na kadācinna vidyate || 4 ||

How could arising exist without cessation? Impermanence is never absent from entities.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

bhaviṣyati kathaṃ nāma saṃbhavo vibhavaṃ vinā | anityatā hi bhāveṣu na kadācinna vidyate || 4 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

And since in this way passing away is not established either without arising or together with arising, he shows that arising too is not established either without passing away or together with passing away:

“How could arising exist without cessation? Impermanence is never absent from entities.” || 4 ||

For arising indeed is not reasonable without passing away, because impermanence is never absent from entities that have the character of coming to be, that have the character of arising; rather, it is always present. For it was said:

“All entities, in every way subject to aging and death, always remain; which entities are there that are without aging and death?”

And when all entities are always accompanied by impermanence in this way, what state could there be that would be free from destruction? Therefore arising does not exist without passing away. Thus, for now, arising is not without passing away. The remainder is not examined here, since it was examined in the Examination of the Conditioned. ||

One raises a proof: “Destruction has a cause, because it has the character of the conditioned, like arising.” He says that it is inconclusive because of the final moments of mind and mental factors. This is not rightly said, because their destruction too has a cause, since birth is its condition; the matter to be established is the same, and therefore there is no inconclusiveness.

And what was stated—“The arising of an entity is called its coming to be after not having been; therefore the arising of a real substance is established, and it is an example in conventional usage”—that too is not right, because arising from causes is accepted even for things that are not real substances, such as reflections. As the venerable Ācārya said:

“Those whose arising is from a cause, and who are absent when that cause is absent, why are they not clearly held to be like reflections?”

From this authoritative statement, how is the conventional example unestablished? If it is because of an entity, that which cannot be spoken of as either identical or different is said not to exist even conventionally; blue and the like too are said not to exist. As it was said in the Ratnāvalī:

“Because form is only the absence of form, space is only a name. Without the elements, whence is form? Therefore it too is only a name.”

Moreover, where is an own-nature, having established existence, for the Madhyamikas, whose particular state would be arising? Therefore it is indeed not right to raise the objection that the example is unestablished.

And what was said—“Destruction is not with a cause, because it is like the unconditioned, which is without destruction”—the reason of one speaking in this way gives rise to a great contradiction. For just as this reason establishes the causelessness of destruction, so too it establishes the absence of the character of the conditioned. And likewise it contradicts all things such as inclusion in the aggregate of formations and inclusion among the factors of dependent arising. Further, from all negations such as “consciousness does not cut off the nature of its object, because it is non-conscious, like the unconditioned,” a great undesirable consequence follows for him. Therefore this view is not to be relied upon. ||
