MMK 22.11

śūnyamiti na vaktavyamaśūnyamiti vā bhavet
ubhayaṃ nobhayaṃ ceti prajñaptyarthaṃ tu kathyate || 11 ||

It should not be said “empty,” nor should it be “nonempty”; nor both, nor neither. All this is not to be said by us. But it is spoken for the sake of designation.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

śūnyamiti na vaktavyamaśūnyamiti vā bhavet | ubhayaṃ nobhayaṃ ceti prajñaptyarthaṃ tu kathyate || 11 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Moreover, the yogin who explains the Tathāgata as without own-nature, wishing to state the meaning without error, should not say in every way, “It is empty,” or “It is nonempty,” or “It is both,” or “It is neither.” All this is not to be said by us. But, when it is not said, the one who apprehends is not able to apprehend the own-nature as it actually stands. Therefore we too, standing in conventional truth, which is an imputation, and for the sake of conventional usage, in accordance with the wishes of those to be trained, say “empty,” say “nonempty,” say “both empty and nonempty,” and say “neither empty nor nonempty.” For this reason he says:

“But it is spoken for the sake of designation.”

As the Blessed One said: “All dharmas are empty through the connection with having no own-nature; all dharmas are signless, by taking up signlessness; all dharmas are without aspiration, by the connection with being without aspiration; all dharmas are naturally luminous through purification by the perfection of wisdom.”

Elsewhere, what is nonempty has been stated: “Monks, if past form did not exist, the learned noble disciple would not delight in past form. Since, monks, past form exists, the learned noble disciple delights in past form. If future form did not exist, monks”—and so forth. In this way, as far as: “If past consciousness did not exist, monks”—and so forth, as before.

Likewise, according to the Sautrāntika view, past and future are empty, and the other [things] are nonempty. Cognitions dissociated [from mind] are empty. Also in the doctrine of consciousness-only, the imagined nature is empty, because it has not arisen in dependence, like the perception of two moons by someone whose eyes are diseased.

“Neither empty nor nonempty; therefore everything is declared. And the middle way is from existence and nonexistence.”

The intention with which emptiness and the like are taught is to be understood from the analysis of the self. As it is said in a sūtra:

“This world has been declared by you to be like an illusion, like a stage-performance, like a dream. There is no self, no being, and no life-course; dharmas are like a mirage and a reflected moon. Empty and peaceful and free from appropriation, the world wanders about not knowing. Through hundreds of methods of approach and reasonings, out of compassion, you lead them in. I saw the whole world, alarmed by hundreds of diseases such as passion and the rest. Like an incomparable physician you move about, Sugata, releasing hundreds of beings. Like a chariot wheel the whole world revolves; beings have gone to the animal realms, the pretas, and hells. Confused, without a guide, gone without refuge, for them you show the excellent path.”

All these conceptions do not occur in the Tathāgata, who is free from elaboration.
