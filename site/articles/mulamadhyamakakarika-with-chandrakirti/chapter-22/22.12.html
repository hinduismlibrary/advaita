MMK 22.12

śāśvatāśāśvatādyā kutaḥ śānte catuṣṭayam
antānantādi cāpyatra kutaḥ śānte catuṣṭayam || 12 ||

How could there be the four alternatives beginning with permanence and impermanence in the peaceful one? And how could there be the four alternatives beginning with finitude and infinitude here, in the peaceful one?

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

śāśvatāśāśvatādyā kutaḥ śānte catuṣṭayam | antānantādi cāpyatra kutaḥ śānte catuṣṭayam || 12 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

And not only do the four alternatives beginning with emptiness and so forth fail to occur in the Tathāgata; further:

The Blessed One has stated fourteen indeterminate matters. They are these: “The world is eternal”; “the world is noneternal”; “the world is both eternal and noneternal”; “the world is neither eternal nor noneternal”—this is the first fourfold set. “The world is finite”; “the world is infinite”; “the world is both finite and infinite”; “the world is neither finite nor infinite”—this is the second. “The Tathāgata exists after death”; “the Tathāgata does not exist after death”; “the Tathāgata both exists and does not exist after death”; “the Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist after death”—this is the third. “The life is the body”; “the life is one thing and the body another.” These fourteen matters are called indeterminate matters because they have not been determined.

There, by the reasoning described above, just as the four alternatives beginning with emptiness and so forth do not occur in the Tathāgata, who is naturally peaceful and without own-nature, so too the four alternatives beginning with permanence and impermanence do not occur in him. And because they do not occur, the Blessed One did not determine the four alternatives concerning the world, just as he did not determine whether a barren woman’s son is dark or fair. And just as this fourfold set does not occur in the Tathāgata, so too the alternatives beginning with finitude and infinitude do not occur in the peaceful Tathāgata.
