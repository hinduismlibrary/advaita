MMK 22.1

skandhā na nānyaḥ skandhebhyo nāsmin skandhā na teṣu saḥ
tathāgataḥ skandhavānna katamo 'tra tathāgataḥ || 1 ||

The aggregates are not [the Tathāgata]; he is not other than the aggregates. The aggregates are not in him; he is not in them. The Tathāgata is not one who possesses the aggregates. Which one here is the Tathāgata?

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

skandhā na nānyaḥ skandhebhyo nāsmin skandhā na teṣu saḥ | tathāgataḥ skandhavānna katamo 'tra tathāgataḥ || 1 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Here, an opponent says: “The continuity of existence certainly exists, because the Tathāgata exists. For, the Blessed One, bearing the pair of great compassion and wisdom in means, with his mind set solely on pacifying the sufferings of birth and so forth of all beings without remainder throughout the three realms, having exerted himself continuously and in sequence for three immeasurable eons, or seven, through the beginnings of those various, unsurpassed, and exceedingly diverse meritorious actions, characterized by the single activity of bringing welfare and benefit to the whole world; being intent, under the power of great compassion, on assisting the whole world without remainder more than even a beloved only son; and, in the various places of rebirth, having made himself available for people to enjoy at their own will, like a common medicinal great tree of earth, water, fire, and wind, attained over a great length of time the station of omniscience, which comprehends every aspect. That Blessed One, having thus attained omniscient knowledge, is called Tathāgata because he has gone completely, without remainder, just as the reality of dharmas is established, and because he has awakened. If the continuity of existence did not exist, then the Tathāgata would not exist either. For Tathāgataship cannot be attained in a single birth. Therefore the continuity of existence certainly exists, because the Tathāgata exists.”

To this we reply: this very great ignorance of yours explains the unbroken continuation and extremely long duration of the continuum of existence. For that very great mass of darkness that is your ignorance, although being destroyed by the lights of knowledge that are like the various autumn moons of births, still today is neither destroyed nor brought to an end, having been increased by an extensive habituation of dispositions over an exceedingly long time. If some Tathāgata existed by own-nature, then there would be a continuity of existence, because he would be brought to completion after a long time. But no entity called a Tathāgata is found to have the nature of an existent thing by own-nature. Only you, whose eye of understanding has been afflicted by the darkness of ignorance, falsely perceive something called a Tathāgata as having own-nature, like two moons, hairs, gnats, and the like. Indicating how the Tathāgata does not exist by own-nature, he says:

If some entity called a Tathāgata, stainless and free from elaboration, existed, would it have the nature of the aggregates? It would have the nature of the five aggregates called form, feeling, recognition, formations, and consciousness; or it would have the nature of the five aggregates called ethical conduct, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and knowledge and vision of liberation; or it would be different from them. The first five aggregates are taken up in this analysis because they are the basis for designating a being; the latter are not taken up, because they are not all-inclusive, being included within the former.

Or, if it were different from the five aggregates, then the aggregates would be in the Tathāgata, or he would be in the aggregates, or the Tathāgata would possess the aggregates, as Devadatta possesses wealth. Examined in every way, none of these is possible. First, the aggregates themselves are not the Tathāgata. Why? It has been said, “If the fuel were the fire, there would be identity of agent and action.” This is also to be applied here: “If the Buddha were the appropriated aggregates, there would be identity of appropriator and appropriated.” And it has been said, “If the self were the aggregates, it would be subject to arising and passing away.” This too is to be applied here: “If the Buddha were the aggregates, he would be subject to arising and passing away.” Thus, for now, the aggregates are not the Tathāgata.

Now, the Tathāgata is not other than the aggregates. Why? It has been said, “If fire were other than fuel, it could exist without fuel.” Also: “Because it would be independent of what is elsewhere, it would have no cause for being kindled; rekindling would then be pointless, and, if so, it would be without activity.” This too is to be applied here: “If the Buddha were other than the appropriated aggregates, he could exist without the aggregates being appropriated.” Also: “If he were other than the aggregates, he would have the characteristic of being without aggregates.” Because there is no otherness between the aggregates and the Tathāgata, the aggregates do not properly exist in the Tathāgata.

Nor is it proper that the Tathāgata is in the aggregates. The explanation of the two positions has been stated in the Madhyamakāvatāra: “There is no self at all in the aggregates, nor are these aggregates in the self. If there were difference, this conception could apply; but that difference does not exist, therefore this is a conception.”

The Tathāgata is not a possessor of aggregates either. It has been said there: “A self possessing form is not accepted, because a self is not accepted. For, where there is difference, the expressions ‘possessor of cows’ and ‘possessor of form’ would be used; but, in the absence of difference, neither identity nor difference from the self exists, because [the self] is without form.” In reality, all five positions are included within the positions of identity and difference; but the Teacher sets out five positions with regard to the operation of the view of personal identity. Understand this. And the Tathāgata who, when examined in these five ways in relation to the aggregates, does not exist: by what other self would he exist? Therefore, seeing that a Tathāgata having the nature of an existent thing is in no way possible, the Teacher’s venerable feet say, “Which one here is the Tathāgata?” The meaning is: no such thing exists that, by its own nature as an existent thing, cognizes all the objects in the three worlds. And, because the Tathāgata does not exist, it is established that the continuity of existence too, as a continuity of substance, does not exist.
