MMK 25.3

aprahīṇam asaṃprāptam anucchinnam aśāśvatam
aniruddham anutpannam etan nirvāṇam ucyate || 3 ||

Unabandoned, unattained, unannihilated, not eternal, not ceased, not arisen—this is called nirvāṇa.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

aprahīṇam asaṃprāptam anucchinnam aśāśvatam | aniruddham anutpannam etan nirvāṇam ucyate || 3 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

If, indeed, those who hold emptiness do not accept nirvāṇa characterized by the cessation of the afflictions or the aggregates, then with what characteristic do they accept it? It is said:

“Unabandoned, unattained, unannihilated, not eternal, not ceased, not arisen—this is called nirvāṇa.”

For that which is not abandoned, as passion and the like are; not attained, as the fruits of ascetic practice are; not annihilated, as the aggregates are; and also not eternal, as what is non-empty is: that is said to be nirvāṇa, by own-being not ceased and not arisen, characterized by the calming of all proliferation.

Then, in such a nirvāṇa, which is without proliferation, whence would there be a conception of afflictions, by abandoning which nirvāṇa would occur? Or whence would there be a conception of aggregates there, through whose cessation it would occur? For as long as these conceptions proceed, there is no attainment of nirvāṇa; it is attained precisely through the exhaustion of all proliferation.

Someone might say: “Although afflictions and aggregates do not exist in nirvāṇa, they exist before nirvāṇa; therefore nirvāṇa will occur through their exhaustion.”

It is said: Let this grasping be relinquished. For it is not possible to make nonexistent again those afflictions and aggregates that exist by own-being before nirvāṇa. Therefore one who desires nirvāṇa should relinquish this conception. For he will say:

“Whatever is the limit of nirvāṇa, that is the limit of saṃsāra; between them not the slightest thing is found.”

Thus, it is to be understood that in nirvāṇa no one’s abandoning and no one’s cessation occur. Therefore nirvāṇa itself is the form of the exhaustion of every conception without remainder. And this was said by the Blessed One:

“‘The dharmas have ceased; the dharmas that are not here do not ever exist. For those who act with the conceptions “it exists” and “it does not exist,” suffering does not become calm.’”

The meaning of this verse is as follows. In cessation, in the element of nirvāṇa without remainder, because the dharmas characterized by afflictions, action, and birth, or the aggregates, have completely disappeared, there is no existence; this is accepted by all proponents. But those dharmas that are not present here, in cessation, like the rope-snake fear perceived when a lamp appears and the darkness is gone, those dharmas characterized by afflictions, action, birth, and so forth, never exist as such at any time, even in the state of saṃsāra.

For a rope, in the state of darkness, is not by its own nature a snake, as an actually existing snake is: it is not apprehended by body or eye either in darkness or in light. If it is asked, “Then how is there saṃsāra?” it is said: for foolish ordinary people seized by the false apprehension of self and what belongs to self, entities whose nature is unreal appear real, like the unreal hair, gnats, and the like to those whose vision is impaired.

He says:

“‘For those who act with the conceptions “it exists” and “it does not exist,” suffering does not become calm.’”

“‘It exists’” is the conception of the existence of entities held by the Jaiminīyas, Kāṇādas, followers of Kapila, and others, down to the Vaibhāṣikas. “It does not exist” is the conception of the nihilists, who end in a destiny among the lower realms. Other proponents of nonexistence hold that past and future are forms, and that formations dissociated from mental activity do not exist; their opponents hold that these do exist. Some hold that the imagined nature does not exist; others hold that the dependent and perfected natures do exist. For those who act in these ways, as proponents of existence and nonexistence, saṃsāric suffering does not become calm.

And likewise:

“‘Just as one who suspects [something] does not accept the notion of poison, nor does one possessed [by it] go into the chest, so likewise the fool, overcome [by it] ... is born and dies, always unreal.’”

Thus, it is to be understood that in nirvāṇa no one’s abandoning and no one’s cessation occur. Therefore nirvāṇa itself is the exhaustion of all conceptualization. As it is said in the Noble Garland:

“‘Even non-existence is not nirvāṇa; how much less its cultivation? The exhaustion of grasping at existence and non-existence is called nirvāṇa.’”
