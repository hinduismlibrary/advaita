MMK 25.8

yady abhāvaś ca nirvāṇam anupādāya tat katham
nirvāṇaṃ na hy abhāvo 'sti yo 'nupādāya vidyate || 8 ||

If nirvāṇa were non-existence, how could it be without appropriation? For no non-existent thing is found that exists without appropriation.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

yady abhāvaś ca nirvāṇam anupādāya tat katham | nirvāṇaṃ na hy abhāvo 'sti yo 'nupādāya vidyate || 8 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

And further:

“If nirvāṇa were non-existence, how could it be without appropriation? For no non-existent thing is found that exists without appropriation.”

Here, nonexistence—or impermanence—is designated in dependence on an existent thing, because impermanence is not observed in a donkey’s horn and the like. The characterized thing is designated in dependence on its characteristic, and the characteristic in dependence on the characterized thing. Therefore, when the characteristic and the characterized thing operate in dependence on one another, how could impermanence exist independently of the characterized existent thing? Thus nonexistence too is designated in dependence.

Therefore, if nirvāṇa were nonexistence, how could nirvāṇa exist without dependence? It would exist only in dependence, because it is nonexistence, like destruction.

Making this very point clear, he says: “For no non-existent thing is found that exists without appropriation.”

If, then, nonexistence does not exist without dependence, would things such as the son of a barren woman be non-existent things in dependence? Who said that sons of barren women and the like are non-existent things? For it was said earlier:

“‘If an existent thing is not established, a non-existent thing is not established; people call the alteration of an existent thing “non-existence.”’”

Therefore the son of a barren woman and the like are not non-existent things.

And what is also said:

“‘Space, a hare’s horn, and a barren woman’s son are spoken of as nonexistent; so too are conceptions about existents.’”

Here too, it is to be understood that this is only the refutation of the conception of existence, not a conception of nonexistence, precisely because existence is not established. “Son of a barren woman” is only a word; its referent is not apprehended, such that it could be existence or nonexistence. How could a conception of existence or nonexistence be appropriate for something whose own-nature is not apprehended? Therefore, it is to be understood that a son of a barren woman is not a non-existent thing. Thus it stands established: “For no non-existent thing is found that exists without appropriation.”
