MMK 3.9

vyākhyātaṃ śravaṇaṃ ghrāṇaṃ rasanaṃ sparśanaṃ manaḥ
darśanenaiva jānīyācchrotṛśrotavyakādi ca || 9 ||

Hearing, smelling, tasting, touching, and mind are explained by seeing alone; through seeing alone one should understand the hearer, the heard, and the like.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

vyākhyātaṃ śravaṇaṃ ghrāṇaṃ rasanaṃ sparśanaṃ manaḥ | darśanenaiva jānīyācchrotṛśrotavyakādi ca || 9 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Now, in order to extend the explanation to the remaining sense bases in the same way as seeing, he says:

“Hearing, smelling, tasting, touching, and mind are explained by seeing alone; through seeing alone one should understand the hearer, the heard, and the like.”

For the Blessed One has said:

“The eye does not perceive form, nor does the mind know phenomena. This is the highest truth into which the world does not enter. Where the Guide makes seeing manifest through a collection [of conditions], the wise call that the conventional level of ultimate reality.”

Likewise:

“Depending on the eye and form, visual consciousness arises here. Form is not supported in the eye, nor is there a transfer of form into the eye. These phenomena are selfless and impure, but self and purity are imagined in them. From that inverted, unreal conception, visual consciousness also arises. One sees the arising and cessation of consciousness, its arising and passing away; it has gone nowhere and come from nowhere. The yogin sees it as empty, like an illusion.”

And in the Inquiry of Ācārya Upāli:

“The eye sees what is joined with conditions; there it does not see what is without conditions. The eye itself certainly does not see form; hence there is the conception of conjunction and separation. Supported by light, the eye sees form, pleasing, varied, and distinguished; but an eye supported by conjunction never sees it. Even the pleasant sound that is heard has never entered within; no transition of it is found. Rather, the sound is raised up by the power of conception.”

Likewise:

“Song, dance, and the sound of instruments are not to be grasped, for pleasures are like dreams and they delude the unwise. The ignorant, eager for conceptions, go to destruction here; why should I become a childish person, like a slave of afflictions?”

Thus ends the third chapter, called “Investigation of the Sense Faculties,” in the Madhyamaka commentary, the Prasannapadā, composed by Ācārya Candrakīrti.
