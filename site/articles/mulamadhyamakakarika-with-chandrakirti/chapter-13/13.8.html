MMK 13.8

śūnyatā sarvadṛṣṭīnāṃ proktā niḥsaraṇaṃ jinaiḥ
yeṣāṃ tu śūnyatā dṛṣṭis tān asādhyān babhāṣire || 8 ||

The Victorious Ones taught emptiness as the relinquishing of all views. Those who make emptiness itself a view, they declared incurable.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

śūnyatā sarvadṛṣṭīnāṃ proktā niḥsaraṇaṃ jinaiḥ | yeṣāṃ tu śūnyatā dṛṣṭis tān asādhyān babhāṣire || 8 ||

Commentary note on Candrakīrti

An opponent protests that the Buddha taught three liberating gateways—emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness—and that Candrakīrti seems to reject them. Candrakīrti says this reverses the path: the Buddha taught emptiness as the release from every view and grasping. Emptiness is not a new entity or a position to hold. Those who turn emptiness itself into a fixed view are beyond the reach of this medicine.

The teaching does not make things empty; things are empty in themselves. A sūtra warns that those who grasp emptiness as something to possess are lost. It is like someone told that there is no merchandise for sale, who then demands to buy “nothing” as though it were an item. If a person clings to emptiness as a real object, how could that very grasping be removed by teaching emptiness? The instruction is meant to end all conceptual fixation.

The Buddha says that it is better to hold even a vast belief in a personal self than to cling to a view of emptiness as mere non-existence. A doctor’s medicine should remove illness; if it remains inside and aggravates every disorder, it has failed. In the same way, emptiness is the way out of all views, but when treated as a view in its own right it deepens confusion. That is why the Buddhas call such a person incurable.
