MMK 13.8

śūnyatā sarvadṛṣṭīnāṃ proktā niḥsaraṇaṃ jinaiḥ
yeṣāṃ tu śūnyatā dṛṣṭis tān asādhyān babhāṣire || 8 ||

Emptiness was taught by the Victors as the relinquishing of all views. Those for whom emptiness is a view were called incurable.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

śūnyatā sarvadṛṣṭīnāṃ proktā niḥsaraṇaṃ jinaiḥ | yeṣāṃ tu śūnyatā dṛṣṭis tān asādhyān babhāṣire || 8 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Here it is said: The three gateways to liberation, called emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness, were taught by the Blessed One for the liberation of those to be trained. They are found in the Buddha’s teaching alone, not shared with the doctrines of all other teachers. For the sake of teaching these, Buddhas, Blessed Ones, the one lamp of the world, whose flame of the teaching of no-self is uninterrupted, arise in a world followed by the great darkness of delusion of the doctrines of all other teachers. You have now begun to refute this very emptiness under the pretext of explaining the Tathāgata’s teaching. Enough of you, destroyer of the collection of the path to heaven and liberation. The response: Ah, indeed, you seem exceedingly eager. Through extreme inversion, having abandoned the auspicious, straight, supreme path leading to the city of nirvāṇa, you have taken up, as the path leading to the city of liberation, only the path that follows the wilderness of saṃsāra, disturbed by clinging to entities. Though desiring release, and indeed reproached by the good as following the wilderness of saṃsāra, you reproach those very ones with hundreds of nets of pride, clinging, and grasping. Indeed, by the great physician-kings who cure the disease of all afflictions:

“Emptiness was declared by the Victors as the relinquishing of all views. But those for whom emptiness is a view, they declared incurable.”

Here, the relinquishing, the non-occurrence, of all view-made things, of all clinging and adherence, is emptiness. And emptiness is not an entity that is merely the cessation of view-made things. But we are inexpressible to those who cling to existence even in that emptiness. How, then, will liberation arise for them through our teaching, by the removal of all conceptual construction? Someone who has been told, “I shall give you no merchandise at all,” if he should say, “Give me just that merchandise called ‘nothing,’ sir,” by what means could he be made to understand the absence of merchandise? In the same way, for those whose clinging to existence is even in emptiness, by what means is their clinging to existence in that emptiness to be denied? Therefore those people, regarding even the great medicine as a fault, have indeed been rejected by the Tathāgatas, the supreme physicians, the great physician-kings. As is said in the Blessed One’s noble Ratnakūṭa Sūtra:

“It is not that one makes dharmas empty by emptiness; rather, dharmas themselves are empty. It is not that one makes dharmas signless by signlessness; rather, dharmas themselves are signless. It is not that one makes dharmas wishless by wishlessness; rather, dharmas themselves are wishless. This examination, Kāśyapa, is called the middle way, the true examination of dharmas. For those who resort to emptiness by apprehending emptiness, Kāśyapa, I say that they are lost, utterly lost.”

Thus is it taught.

And likewise:

“It is better, Kāśyapa, to have a personal-identity view as great as Mount Sumeru than to have a view of emptiness that clings to nonexistence. For what reason? For emptiness, Kāśyapa, is the relinquishing of all views. But whoever has emptiness itself as a view, him I say to be incurable. Suppose, Kāśyapa, there were a sick man. A physician would give him medicine. What do you think, Kāśyapa? If that medicine did not expel all the humors but remained in his stomach, would that man be freed from his illness? No, Blessed One. The illness of that man would become more severe, if that medicine, not expelling all the humors, remained in his stomach. The Blessed One said: In exactly the same way, Kāśyapa, emptiness is the relinquishing of all views. But whoever has emptiness itself as a view, him I say to be incurable.”

Thus ends the thirteenth chapter, called “Examination of Formations,” in the Madhyamaka commentary Prasannapadā composed by Ācārya Candrakīrti.
