Investigation of Extremes of Before and After

MMK 11.8

pūrvā na vidyate koṭiḥ saṃsārasya na kevalam
sarveṣāmapi bhāvānāṃ pūrvā koṭirna vidyate || 8 ||

The first limit of saṃsāra alone is not found; the first limit of all things too is not found.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

pūrvā na vidyate koṭiḥ saṃsārasya na kevalam | sarveṣāmapi bhāvānāṃ pūrvā koṭirna vidyate || 8 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Just as the first limit of saṃsāra does not exist, so too of other things, he says:

“Cause and effect, characterized and characteristic, feeling and feeler, and whatever things there are.”

The first limit of saṃsāra alone is not found; the first limit of all things too is not found.

Not only is the first limit of saṃsāra absent; the first limit of all things is absent. If a cause were first and an effect afterward, the cause would be without an effect and without a cause. Or if an effect were first and a cause afterward, even so the effect before the cause would indeed be without a cause. Or if cause and effect were together, both would be without causes. The same is to be applied to characterized and characteristic, and to feeling and feeler. And it is not to be understood that cause and effect and the like have been explained only through the explanation of saṃsāra. Moreover, it is to be applied also that the first limit of those entities that are at the end of the series—knowledge and knowable, means of valid knowledge and object of valid knowledge, means and what is to be accomplished, part and whole, quality and possessor of quality, and the like—is absent. For this very reason, in the noble Ratnamegha Sūtra, the Blessed One was praised by the noble great bodhisattva Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin:

“Those dharmas are peaceful from the beginning, unborn, and by their very nature extinguished. O Lord, they were revealed at the turning of the wheel of Dharma.”

And likewise:

“From the beginning, empty, not arrived, not gone, not abiding, free from a place: all are always without substance, of the nature of illusion, pure, completely pure, like the sky.

“He who speaks the Victor’s Dharma does not see it as imperishable. These dharmas are without beginning, without self, without beings; he speaks them and does not destroy them.

“One says ‘constructed,’ merely constructed; but no end is found for one who is transmigrating. The first limit has no characteristic; formerly it was thus through causal dependence.

“Action and activity proceed thus; the low and the high arise. Know all conditioned phenomena as always, by nature, empty and without self.”

And so forth.

Thus ends the eleventh chapter, called “Examination of the Former and Later Limits,” in the Madhyamaka commentary Prasannapadā composed by Ācārya Candrakīrti.
