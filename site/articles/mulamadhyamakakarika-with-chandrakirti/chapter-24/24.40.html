MMK 24.40

Investigation of the Ennobling Truths

← MMK 24.39Chapter 24MMK 25.1 →

MMK 24.40

yaḥ pratītyasamutpādaṃ paśyatīdaṃ sa paśyati
duḥkhaṃ samudayaṃ caiva nirodhaṃ mārgam eva ca || 40 ||

Whoever sees dependent arising, that one sees this: suffering, its arising, its cessation, and the path.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

yaḥ pratītyasamutpādaṃ paśyatīdaṃ sa paśyati | duḥkhaṃ samudayaṃ caiva nirodhaṃ mārgam eva ca || 40 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

In this way, because, when the doctrine of own-being is accepted, all this does not obtain, therefore:

“Whoever sees dependent arising, that one sees this: suffering, its arising, its cessation, and the path.”

For whoever correctly sees own-being-emptiness, characterized by the dependent arising of all dharmas, sees the four ennobling truths as they are, in reality.

As it is said in the Noble Mañjuśrī’s Questions:

“Mañjuśrī, by whom the non-arising of all dharmas has been seen, by him suffering has been fully known. By whom the non-existence of all dharmas has been seen, by him arising has been abandoned. By whom all dharmas have been seen as utterly extinguished, by him cessation has been directly realized. Mañjuśrī, by whom the absence of all dharmas has been seen, by him the path has been cultivated.” Thus, at length.

And it is said in the Noble Fist of Meditation Sūtra:

Then the Blessed One said this to the youthful Mañjuśrī: “Mañjuśrī, because they do not see the four ennobling truths as they are, sentient beings, their minds distorted by the four inversions, do not cross over this saṃsāra, devoid of essence.”

When this had been said, the youthful Mañjuśrī said this to the Blessed One: “May the Blessed One explain: through apprehension of what do sentient beings not cross over saṃsāra?”

The Blessed One said: “Mañjuśrī, sentient beings do not cross over saṃsāra through apprehension of self and what belongs to self. Why is that? Mañjuśrī, for whoever sees self and other, formations of karma occur for him.

“Mañjuśrī, an ignorant, unlearned ordinary person, not knowing all dharmas as utterly extinguished, apprehends self and other. Having apprehended, he adheres; having adhered, he is attached, repelled, and confused. Being attached, repelled, and confused, he forms three kinds of karma by body, speech, and mind.

“Through the superimposition of what is unreal, he thinks: ‘I am attached; I am repelled; I am confused.’ For him, having gone forth in the Tathāgata’s teaching, this occurs: ‘I am virtuous; I am a brahmacārin; I will pass beyond saṃsāra; I will attain nirvāṇa; I will be freed from sufferings.’

“He conceptualizes: ‘These dharmas are wholesome; these dharmas are unwholesome. These dharmas are to be abandoned; these dharmas are to be directly realized. Suffering is to be fully known; arising is to be abandoned; cessation is to be directly realized; the path is to be cultivated.’

“He conceptualizes: ‘All formations are impermanent; all formations are burning. Would that I might flee from all formations!’

“As he observes that very thing, an attention accompanied by disenchantment arises for him, with signlessness at its forefront. It occurs to him: ‘This is the full knowledge of suffering: this knowing of these dharmas.’

“It occurs to him: ‘Would that I might abandon arising!’ He recoils from all dharmas, feels revulsion, turns away, is disgusted, is alarmed, is terrified, and falls into terror. It occurs to him: ‘This is the direct realization of these dharmas; this is the abandonment of arising, namely, this recoiling from these dharmas.’

“It occurs to him: ‘Cessation is to be directly realized.’ Having conceptualized arising, he recognizes cessation. It occurs to him: ‘This is the direct realization of cessation.’

“It occurs to him: ‘Surely I should cultivate the path.’ Alone, gone into seclusion, attending to those dharmas, he attains calm. Through that attention accompanied by disenchantment, calm arises for him. His mind does not sink into all dharmas; it flows onward and turns back. He recoils from them, feels revulsion, and a mind that does not delight arises.

“It occurs to him: ‘I am freed from all sufferings; there is nothing further that I have to do; I am an arhat.’ Thus he recognizes himself. At the time of death, he sees his own rebirth among the gods. Doubt and uncertainty about the Buddha’s awakening arise for him. Having fallen into doubt and uncertainty, at death he falls into the great hells. Why is that? Having conceptualized all dharmas as unarisen, he produces doubt and uncertainty about the Tathāgata.”

Then the youthful Mañjuśrī said this to the Blessed One: “But, Blessed One, how are the four ennobling truths to be seen?”

The Blessed One said: “Mañjuśrī, by whom all dharmas are seen as unarisen, by him suffering has been fully known. By whom all dharmas are seen as unproduced, by him arising has been abandoned. By whom all dharmas are seen as utterly extinguished, by him cessation has been directly realized. By whom all dharmas are seen as utterly empty, by him the path has been cultivated.

“Mañjuśrī, one who sees the four ennobling truths in this way does not conceptualize: ‘These dharmas are wholesome; these dharmas are unwholesome; these dharmas are to be abandoned; these dharmas are to be directly realized; suffering is to be fully known; arising is to be abandoned; cessation is to be directly realized; the path is to be cultivated.’ Why is that? For he does not see or apprehend that dharma which he would conceptualize.

“But foolish ordinary people, conceptualizing these dharmas, become attached, hate, and become confused. He neither raises up nor lays down any dharma. Because he neither raises up nor lays down anything in this way, his mind does not adhere to the three realms. He sees the entire three realms as unborn, like an illusion, like a dream, like an echo.

“Seeing all dharmas as having such a nature, he is free of attraction and aversion toward all sentient beings. Why is that? For he does not apprehend those dharmas toward which he would be attracted or opposed.

“With a mind equal to space, he does not regard the Buddha, nor does he regard the Dharma, nor does he regard the Saṅgha. Seeing all dharmas as empty, he does not produce doubt about any dharma. Being without doubt, he becomes without appropriation; without appropriating, he is completely liberated without appropriating.” Thus, at length.
