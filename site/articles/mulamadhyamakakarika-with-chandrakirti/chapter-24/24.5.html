MMK 24.5

dharme cāsati saṃghe ca kathaṃ buddho bhaviṣyati
evaṃ trīṇy api ratnāni bruvāṇaḥ pratibādhase || 5 ||

And when there is no Dharma and no Sangha, how will a Buddha exist?

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

dharme cāsati saṃghe ca kathaṃ buddho bhaviṣyati | evaṃ trīṇy api ratnāni bruvāṇaḥ pratibādhase || 5 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

If, when the noble truths of suffering and the rest do not exist, full understanding and the rest do not exist, what fault follows? We reply:

When full understanding of suffering and the rest do not exist, then the fourfold set of fruits called stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, and arhatship is not possible. How so? Here, the collected abandonment of afflictions obtains the designation “fruit.” For example, when the three fetters have been abandoned, the abandonment of afflictions that occurs at the moment of subsequent knowledge on the sixteenth path is the fruit of stream-entry.

The afflictions in the desire realm that are to be abandoned by cultivation have three grades: strong, middling, and mild. Each of those is again divided into strong, middling, and mild, so there are nine grades. There, the abandonment on the path of liberation when the sixth grade of affliction in the desire realm has been exhausted is the fruit of once-returning. The abandonment of afflictions on the path of liberation when the ninth grade of those same desire-realm afflictions has been exhausted is the fruit of non-returning.

The afflictions in the realms of form and the formless, which are to be abandoned by cultivation and are divided into nine grades in each plane, are abandoned on the path of liberation when the ninth grade of affliction in the plane of neither perception nor non-perception, the highest plane, has been exhausted. That is the fruit of arhatship. These are the four fruits. How are these four fruits reasonable? When full understanding of suffering occurs, abandonment of origin occurs, cessation is directly realized, and the noble path is cultivated. But when, because the noble truths of suffering and the rest do not exist, full understanding of suffering and the rest do not exist, then those four fruits do not exist. And when the four fruits do not exist, the four noble persons established in them, abiding in the fruits, do not exist. For this very reason, the four noble persons who are proceeding toward them are not found either.

Here, before the sixteenth moment of subsequent knowledge on the path, there are fifteen moments of acceptance and knowledge. They are as follows. In realizing the suffering of the three realms, there are four moments of acceptance and knowledge that take the truth of suffering as their object. Which are the four moments of acceptance and knowledge in realizing the suffering of the three realms?

The first is the moment of acceptance of Dharma-knowledge concerning suffering. It has the aspect of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and not-self; it takes as its object the truth of suffering in the desire realm; it is characterized as the uninterrupted path; and it is opposed to the ten latent tendencies abandoned by seeing the suffering of the desire realm: belief in a personal body, grasping at an end, wrong view, adherence to views, adherence to rules and observances, doubt, passion, aversion, conceit, and ignorance. The second is the moment of Dharma-knowledge concerning suffering, with the same object and aspect, characterized as the path of liberation.

In the same way, the third is the moment of acceptance of subsequent-knowledge concerning suffering, taking the truth of suffering in the realms of form and the formless as its object. It arises with the aspect of suffering and the rest, is characterized as the uninterrupted path, and is opposed to the eighteen latent tendencies just mentioned, except for aversion. The fourth is the moment of subsequent knowledge concerning suffering, with the same object and aspect, characterized as the path of liberation.

Just as these are four moments in realizing the truth of suffering throughout the three realms, so, in the desire realm, the first is the moment of acceptance of Dharma-knowledge concerning origin. It takes the truth of origin as its object, arises with the aspect of cause, arising, source, and condition, is characterized as the uninterrupted path, and is opposed to the seven latent tendencies abandoned by seeing origin: wrong view, adherence to views, doubt, passion, aversion, conceit, and ignorance. The second is the moment of Dharma-knowledge concerning origin, with the same object and aspect, characterized as the path of liberation.

In the same way, the third is the moment of acceptance of subsequent knowledge concerning origin, taking the truth of origin in the realms of form and the formless as its object. It arises with the aspect of the truth of origin and is characterized as the uninterrupted path; it is opposed to the twelve latent tendencies mentioned above, except for aversion. The fourth is the moment of subsequent knowledge concerning origin, with the same object and aspect, characterized as the path of liberation. These are the four moments in realizing the truths of suffering and origin throughout the three realms.

Just as these are four moments in realizing the truths of suffering and origin throughout the three realms, so, in the desire realm, the first moment of acceptance of Dharma-knowledge concerning cessation takes the truth of the cessation of suffering as its object. It arises with the aspect of cessation, peace, excellence, and escape; is characterized as the uninterrupted path; and is opposed to the seven latent tendencies stated under origin. The second is the moment of Dharma-knowledge concerning cessation, with the same object and aspect, characterized as the path of liberation.

With these same aspects, the third is the moment of acceptance of subsequent knowledge concerning cessation, taking the truth of cessation of suffering in the realms of form and the formless as its object. It is characterized as the uninterrupted path and opposed to the twelve latent tendencies, except for aversion. The fourth is the moment of subsequent knowledge concerning cessation, with the same object and aspect, characterized as the path of liberation. These are the four moments in realizing the truth of the cessation of suffering throughout the three realms.

In the same way, in the latent tendencies stated under cessation that are to be abandoned by seeing the path that leads to the cessation of suffering in the desire realm, with adherence to rules and observances added as the eighth, the first is the moment of acceptance of Dharma-knowledge concerning the path. It takes as its object the path that leads to the cessation of suffering in the desire realm, arises with the aspect of the path, reason, practice, and going forth, is characterized as the uninterrupted path, and is opposed to those eight latent tendencies. The second is the moment of Dharma-knowledge concerning the path, with the same object and aspect, characterized as the path of liberation.

With these same aspects, the third is the moment of acceptance of subsequent knowledge concerning the path, taking as its object the path that leads to the cessation of suffering in the realms of form and the formless. It is characterized as the uninterrupted path and opposed to the fourteen latent tendencies, except for aversion. These are the fifteen moments called the path of seeing.

The noble person who is thus established, proceeding toward the direct realization of the fruit of stream-entry, is called one who is proceeding toward the fruit of stream-entry. But, established in subsequent knowledge on the sixteenth path, that person is called a stream-enterer.

These eighty-eight latent tendencies are abandoned merely through seeing the truths, without depending on cultivation; for this reason they are called “to be abandoned by seeing.” Those that are abandoned later through cultivation of the aspects of the truths as seen are “to be abandoned by cultivation.” They are ten latent tendencies: in the desire realm, passion, aversion, conceit, and ignorance; in the realm of form, these same [tendencies] except aversion; and in the formless realm, these same three. Thus they are ten.

By the reasoning stated above, each of these is divided into nine grades in each plane: in the desire realm, in each of the four absorptions, and in each of the four formless planes. For the abandonment of each grade of affliction, two moments of knowledge are set out, differing as the uninterrupted path and the path of liberation, in the reverse order of the grades of affliction. For an extremely intense grade of affliction is abandoned by the two moments of the mildest grade, the uninterrupted path and the path of liberation, down to the very mild grade of affliction, which is abandoned by the two moments of the most intense grade. A coarse stain is removed with little effort, whereas a subtle stain is removed with great effort; this is to be understood by the analogy of a washerman washing cloth.

There, after the path of seeing and before the moment of knowledge on the path of liberation that opposes the sixth grade of affliction in the desire realm to be abandoned by cultivation, the noble person abiding in that moment of knowledge is called one who is proceeding toward the fruit of once-returning. Because that person comes back to this world once and then passes completely into nirvāṇa, the person is called a once-returner. One who is on the preparatory practice, proceeding for the sake of that fruit, is called one who is proceeding toward the fruit of once-returning. In the sixth moment, that person is called a once-returner.

From the sixth moment onward and before the moment of knowledge on the path of liberation that abandons the ninth grade of affliction, the noble person abiding in those moments of knowledge is called one who is proceeding toward the fruit of non-returning. Because that person does not come back to this world and passes completely into nirvāṇa there, that person is called a non-returner. One who is on the preparatory practice for the sake of that fruit is called one who is proceeding toward the fruit of non-returning. In the ninth moment, that person is called a non-returner.

After the ninth moment of the path of liberation for the desire realm and before the moment of knowledge on the path of liberation that abandons the ninth grade of affliction in the plane of neither perception nor non-perception, the noble person abiding in those moments of knowledge is called one who is proceeding toward the fruit of arhatship. Because that person is worthy of reverence by the world together with its gods, humans, and asuras, the person is called an arhat. One who is on the preparatory practice for the sake of that fruit is called one who is proceeding toward the fruit of arhatship. When, on the ninth path of liberation, the ninth grade of affliction in the summit of existence is abandoned, the person becomes an arhat.

These four persons proceeding [toward the fruits] and four persons established in the fruits make eight great person-individuals. The Blessed One said that they are worthy of the highest offering. As it is said in a sūtra:

“Asked by the lord of gods, Śakra, who is in control: ‘For beings who toil and sacrifice, who desire merit, who always make the material merit of those who give with faith, I shall tell you the good field where a great fruit comes from what is given. Four are proceeding, and four are established in the fruits; this is the Sangha, worthy of offerings through its perfection of knowledge and conduct.’”

If the four noble truths and their full knowledges do not exist, then, because the fruits obtained through seeing and cultivating the truths do not exist, the persons proceeding toward the fruits and established in the fruits do not exist. Therefore the Sangha does not exist. Through the Dharma of realization, the direct presence of Dharma, and the arising of confidence from knowing that it cannot be shaken even by all the Māras, there would be no Sangha. And if those eight person-individuals do not exist, there is no Sangha.

Because the noble truths do not exist, the true Dharma too is not possible. The Dharma of the noble ones is the true Dharma. There, the truth of cessation is the Dharma that is the fruit; the truth of the path is the Dharma that leads into the fruit. This is the Dharma of realization. The teaching that makes that clear is the Dharma of scripture. All this does not exist when the noble truths do not exist. Thus: “Because the noble truths do not exist, the true Dharma too is not present. And when there is no Dharma and no Sangha, how will a Buddha exist?”

If the Dharma stated above existed, then through following that Dharma, complete awakening to every dharma in every aspect, “Buddha” would be reasonable. And if the Sangha existed, then the collection of knowledge, increased through their instructions, and the collection of merit, increased through giving, honor, taking refuge, and the rest, would gradually bring one to Buddhahood.

Or alternatively: if there is no Sangha, there would be no persons proceeding toward the fruit of stream-entry and the rest. And Buddhahood is not attained without first attaining the state of one who is proceeding toward the fruit and the rest. For the Blessed One must necessarily first be established in some fruit. The Blessed One established in that fruit would be included in the Sangha. If there is no Sangha, the Blessed One, the Buddha, certainly does not exist.

Or, because the Blessed One is included among those beyond further training, the Blessed One too is included in the Sangha. Some describe him as included in the Sangha because it is said, “the community of monks headed by the Buddha.” According to their view, this is plainly so: “And when there is no Dharma and no Sangha, how will a Buddha exist?”

The Madhyoddeśikas too, explaining the Bodhisattva who has reached the first stage and produced the path of seeing according to the arrangement of stages taught in the Mahāvastu, explain that he is included in the Sangha. Then, if there is no Sangha, it is plain that the Bodhisattva too does not exist. How then will there be a Buddha?

Thus, by saying that things are empty, you deny all three Jewels—the Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha—which are difficult to obtain, which come into being only once in a long while, which people of little merit do not attain, and which are of very great value.
