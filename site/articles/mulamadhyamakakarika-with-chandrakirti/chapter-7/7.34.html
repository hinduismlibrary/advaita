MMK 7.34

Like a dream, like a magician's illusion, like a city of gandharvas, likewise birth and likewise remaining, likewise perishing are taught.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

yathā māyā yathā svapno gandharvanagaraṃ yathā | tathotpādastathā sthānaṃ tathā bhaṅga udāhṛtam || 34 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

The opponent cites the Buddha’s statement that arising, passing away and alteration of abiding are discerned in conditioned things. Candrakīrti explains that these are taught as conventional descriptions, not as things existing by their own nature—just as people speak of an illusion, a dream or a city of gandharvas, though none is independently real. The teaching is offered to guide those who need it.

The commentary illustrates this with verses. A deluded person calls a body of skin, sinews, flesh and bones “my wife” and becomes attached, though the object of that attachment is like an illusion. A young woman dreams that a child is born and dies, rejoicing at the birth and grieving at the death; understand all phenomena through that comparison. Another verse compares phenomena to a gandharva city, a mirage, an illusion and a dream: their signs are empty of own-nature. The final verses describe sages as free from fixed views about conditioned and unconditioned things, and from the paths of mistaken views; their minds are steady, without passion, hatred or delusion, because they know the empty nature of phenomena.
