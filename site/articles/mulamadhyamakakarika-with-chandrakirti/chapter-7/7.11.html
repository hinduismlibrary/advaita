MMK 7.11

aprāpyaiva pradīpena yadi vā nihataṃ tamaḥ
ihasthaḥ sarvalokasthaṃ sa tamo nihaniṣyati || 11 ||

If darkness is destroyed by a lamp without reaching it, then the lamp standing here will destroy the darkness in the whole world.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

aprāpyaiva pradīpena yadi vā nihataṃ tamaḥ | ihasthaḥ sarvalokasthaṃ sa tamo nihaniṣyati || 11 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Someone might think: “Just as knowledge destroys ignorance without reaching it, the eye sees form without reaching it, and a lodestone attracts iron without reaching it, so a lamp will destroy darkness without reaching it.” He says that this too is insubstantial:

“If darkness is destroyed by a lamp without reaching it, then the lamp standing here will destroy the darkness in the whole world.”

If darkness is destroyed by a lamp without reaching it, then, in that case, a lamp standing here will destroy the darkness situated in the whole world, since, without reaching it, it is just like what is nearby. By this reasoning, the dispelling of ignorance by knowledge, seeing form by the eye, attraction of iron by a lodestone, and the like are to be understood as equal to what is to be established.

Someone may say: “Even though there is no reaching, perhaps lodestones and the rest perform their own function only when situated in a suitable place.” This too is not reasonable. When there is no reaching, because it is like being situated in a distant place elsewhere and like being situated far away without an intervening barrier, even things situated in a suitable place cannot reasonably be situated in a suitable place. How, then, will it be established that things situated in a suitable place perform their own functions?

Someone may say: “This is seen in the world.” This is not so. It is not seen in the world as you conceive it. The world does not introduce this kind of thought about reaching and not reaching into these matters, beginning with light and the rest, and then conceive their illuminating and the like. Rather, without introducing the analysis stated above, it accepts that darkness is destroyed by a lamp, that form is seen by the eye, and that iron is attracted by a lodestone. Let the world see it in this way. But at the time of examining reality, the world is not an authority, and it cannot be used to refute. Thus illumination without reaching is unreasonable; and even when there is reaching, grasping an object and the rest is unreasonable. For reaching occurs when there is oneness; and when there is oneness, seeing, attraction, and the like of something with its own nature do not exist.

Although this consideration of reaching and not reaching and the like does not enter ordinary worldly practice, because this [ordinary practice] is groundless and false, nevertheless it must be introduced in examining reality, lest an unreasonable position be accepted as ultimate reality. Enough of this digression.
