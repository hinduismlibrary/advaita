MMK 15.7

kātyāyanāvavāde cāstīti nāstīti cobhayam
pratiṣiddhaṃ bhagavatā bhāvābhāvavibhāvinā || 7 ||

And in the instruction to Kātyāyana, the Blessed One, who makes being and non-being clear, denied both “it exists” and “it does not exist.”

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

kātyāyanāvavāde cāstīti nāstīti cobhayam | pratiṣiddhaṃ bhagavatā bhāvābhāvavibhāvinā || 7 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

And because these views of own-being, other-being, being, and non-being are not truths, since they are devoid of reasoning, for those who seek liberation and those to be trained:

“In the instruction to Kātyāyana, the Blessed One, who makes being and non-being clear, denied both ‘it exists’ and ‘it does not exist.’”

For the Blessed One said in the noble Kātyāyana Instruction Sūtra:

“For the most part, Kātyāyana, this world is attached either to existence or to non-existence. Because of this, it is not fully released from birth, aging, disease, death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, unhappiness, and distress; it is not released from the bondage of the prison-house of saṃsāra with its five destinies; it is not released from the suffering of the death of a mother; it is not released from the suffering of the death of a father,” and so forth.

And this sūtra is recited in all the collections. Therefore, on account of this scriptural authority and the reasoning described, a wise person should not adopt the view of own-being, other-being, being, or non-being, which is utterly contrary to the Tathāgata’s word, because it was denied by the Blessed One. By what characteristic Blessed One? By being one who makes being and non-being clear. One whose habit is to make being and non-being clear is one who makes being and non-being clear. Because of knowing without error the own-nature of being and non-being as they stand, the Blessed One alone is called one who makes being and non-being clear. Since being and non-being, both of them, were denied by that Blessed One who makes being and non-being clear, it is not reasonable to accept the view of being and non-being as truth.

And likewise:

“‘It exists,’ Kāśyapa, is one extreme. ‘It does not exist,’ Kāśyapa, is one extreme. Whatever is the middle between these two extremes is unformed, invisible, unestablished, unmanifest, without abode, and not indicated. This, Kāśyapa, is called the middle way, the true examination of dharmas.”

And likewise:

“‘It exists’ and ‘it does not exist’ are both extremes; ‘pure’ and ‘impure’ are also these extremes. Therefore, having abandoned both extremes, the wise person does not take a position even in the middle.

“‘It exists’ and ‘it does not exist’—this is a dispute; ‘pure’ and ‘impure’—this is a dispute. By reaching a dispute, suffering does not subside; by not reaching a dispute, suffering ceases.”

Here it is said: If the own-being of fire and the like exists by its own nature, what fault would there be? The fault stated:

“An own-being arisen from causes and conditions would be made,” and so forth.
