Investigation of Essences

MMK 15.11

asti yad dhi svabhāvena na tan nāstīti śāśvatam
nāstīdānīm abhūt pūrvam ity ucchedaḥ prasajyate || 11 ||

Whatever exists by own-being, that is not non-existent: this is permanence. “It did not exist before; now it does not exist”: this entails annihilation.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

asti yad dhi svabhāvena na tan nāstīti śāśvatam | nāstīdānīm abhūt pūrvam ity ucchedaḥ prasajyate || 11 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Why again, when there is a view of being and non-being, does the consequence of a view of permanence and annihilation follow? Because:

Whatever is said to exist by own-being, because own-being does not depart, that is never at any time non-existent. Thus, when the existence of an entity is accepted, the view of permanence follows. And for one who first accepts the own-form of an entity in a former present state and later accepts that it does not exist now because it has been destroyed, the view of annihilation follows. But for one for whom own-being of an entity is not established, there is no consequence of a view of permanence or annihilation, because own-being of the entity is not perceived.

But, surely, for one who accepts that own-being of entities does not exist, while the view of permanence does not arise because there is no view of an entity, the view of annihilation necessarily follows. It is not so: there is no view of non-being. For one who first accepts own-being of an entity and afterward takes hold of its cessation has a view of non-being, because he denies own-being perceived earlier. But one who perceives nothing, like a person without an eye-disease who does not perceive the hairs perceived by one with an eye-disease, if he says “it does not exist,” says that something does not exist though there is nothing to be denied. But for the sake of removing the false clinging of those who are reversed, we say, as do those without eye-disease: “All entities do not exist.” And when we, who are intent on acting for the benefit of others, speak thus, the consequence of the view of annihilation does not follow. As is said in a sūtra:

“Whoever, Blessed One, having first accepted the existence of passion, hatred, and delusion, afterward says, ‘Passion, hatred, and delusion do not exist,’ that one, Blessed One, becomes a nihilist.” And so forth.

But one who accepts only mind and mental factors as dependent on another, and avoids a view of existence because their imagined nature does not exist, and avoids a view of non-existence because the mere dependent reality, which is the basis of defilement and purification, exists—for him, since the imagined does not exist and the dependent does exist, how is there not the arising of both views of existence and non-existence? And because it has been shown that the own-being of things produced by causes and conditions is unreasonable, his explanation too is unreasonable. Thus it is to be understood that the two views of existence and non-existence do not occur only in the Madhyamaka view, but also in views such as the doctrine of consciousness-only.

For this very reason, it is said in the noble Ratnāvalī:

“Ask the world that speaks of Sāṃkhya, Ulūka, Nirgrantha, person, and aggregates whether it says that it has gone beyond existence and non-existence.

“Because of the combination of dharmas, know the profound transcending of existence and non-existence; this nectar of the Buddhas’ teaching is said.”

And, in accordance with the understanding of trainees of this kind, because the vision of ultimate truth is a means, the doctrine of consciousness and the like was taught by the Blessed One, dependent on great compassion, as having an interpretable meaning, like the doctrine of the person held by the Sāṃmitīyas; it is to be understood that it is not of definitive meaning. As is said in the noble Samādhirāja:

“He knows the distinction of sūtras of definitive meaning, emptiness as taught by the Sugata. But that in which person, being, and man know all dharmas is of interpretable meaning.”

This is to be understood at length in the noble Akṣayamatinirdeśa and the like. Thus, understanding that the consequence of the two views of being and non-being is saṃsāra, those seeking liberation should cultivate the middle way, having rejected these two views, as it is, by means of the good. And this was said by the Blessed One:

“The knowledge that makes clear being and non-being is wholly inconceivable, wholly unreal. But the fools who follow the power of thought suffer in hundreds of limits of existence.

“Whoever knows things as being and non-being is never attached to all things. Whoever is never attached to all things touches the signless concentration.”

And likewise:

“I remember, in a former, past age, in an inconceivable aeon, the best of men arose, a great sage who acted for the benefit of the world. By name, he was Abhāvasamudgata.

“As soon as he was born, standing in the sky, he taught all dharmas as non-being. A name was made in accordance with that; by the word, all the three thousand were made known.

“All the gods too uttered the sound, ‘The Victor will be named Non-being.’ He, who as soon as born took seven steps, revealed all dharmas as equal in non-being.

“When the Buddha, the king of Dharma, the sage who reveals all dharmas, becomes [Buddha], in grasses, bushes, trees, herbs, rocks, and mountains there will be the sound of non-being of dharmas.

“As many sounds as there are in that world-element, all indeed are non-beings, for there is not one being. So many indeed are the sounds that issue from that Tathāgata, the guide of the world.”

“‘Being’ means existence. Since the own-being of all entities as existence does not exist, all dharmas are non-beings, because the own-being of entities is not established, and because the text of the Prajñāpāramitā says, ‘All dharmas are empty through being joined with absence of own-being.’

“The meaning of the sūtra is to be understood by the passage beginning, ‘There will be the sound of non-being of dharmas,’ and so on.

“‘As many sounds as there are in that world-element, all indeed are non-beings, for there is not one being,’ and so on.

“Because, having become, what is intended is the denial of non-being, the meaning of being and non-being is precisely the meaning of absence of own-being.”

Thus ends the fifteenth chapter, called “Examination of Own-Being,” in the Madhyamaka commentary Prasannapadā composed by Ācārya Candrakīrti.
