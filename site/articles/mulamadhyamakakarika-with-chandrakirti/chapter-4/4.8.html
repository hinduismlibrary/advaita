MMK 4.8

vigrahe yaḥ parīhāraṃ kṛte śūnyatayā vadet
sarvaṃ tasyāparihṛtaṃ samaṃ sādhyena jāyate || 8 ||

If, in a debate, someone should state a reply when [the refutation is] made by emptiness, all of that reply of his remains unanswered and becomes equal to what is to be established.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

vigrahe yaḥ parīhāraṃ kṛte śūnyatayā vadet | sarvaṃ tasyāparihṛtaṃ samaṃ sādhyena jāyate || 8 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Therefore:

“If, in a debate, someone should state a reply when [the refutation is] made by emptiness, all of that reply of his remains unanswered and becomes equal to what is to be established.”

Here, a debate is the refutation of the opposing position. When, by emptiness as the reason, the view that form has own-nature has been refuted with the statement “form is without own-nature,” if the opponent should state a reply—“Feelings and the rest at least exist; form too exists in the same way as they”—then all of this reply of his remains unrefuted. For the existence of feelings and the rest is to be understood as equal to the existence of form, which is what is to be established. Just as form is non-existent when examined as identical with or different from its own cause, so too feeling, conditioned by contact; conception, co-arisen with consciousness; formations, conditioned by ignorance; and consciousness, conditioned by formations, are not existent when examined as identical with or different from their own cause, contact and the rest. Therefore all of this is equal to what is to be established.

Just as feelings and the rest are equal to what is to be established, so too all entities, such as the characterized and the characteristic, effect and cause, whole and parts, and the rest, are equal to the thing to be established, form. How, then, could a reply of the opponent be possible? Every statement of his becomes equal to what is to be established. The Ācārya teaches that in every treatise a Madhyamika should understand that the opponent’s reply is equal to the thesis.
