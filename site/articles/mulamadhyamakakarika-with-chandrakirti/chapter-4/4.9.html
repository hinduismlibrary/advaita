MMK 4.9

When having explained by means of emptiness, everything of that one who finds fault is not a fault; it is similar to what is to be established.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

vyākhyāne ya upālambhaṃ kṛte śūnyatayā vadet | sarvaṃ tasyānupālabdhaṃ samaṃ sādhyena jāyate || 9 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

The same rule applies when the teaching is being explained. A student may raise an objection to the explanation of emptiness, but that objection too is on the same footing as the point being established; it has not independently proved the existence of the things it invokes.

Candrakīrti cites a verse: “Whoever sees the emptiness of one thing is remembered as seeing the emptiness of all. Whatever emptiness belongs to one thing is the emptiness of all.” The argument is not limited to form: the same analysis applies to every phenomenon.

He also quotes the *Gaganagañjasamādhisūtra*: through one phenomenon, a practitioner understands all phenomena as like illusions and mirages, ungraspable, insubstantial, and not eternal; such a person soon reaches awakening. The *Samādhirājasūtra* adds that just as one has understood the designation “self,” one should extend that understanding everywhere: all phenomena are pure and like space. Knowing all through one, and seeing all through one, does not give rise to pride, however much has been cultivated. Candrakīrti then closes Chapter 4, “Examination of the Aggregates.”
