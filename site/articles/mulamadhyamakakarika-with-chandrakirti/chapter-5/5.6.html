MMK 5.6

avidyamāne bhāve ca kasyābhāvo bhaviṣyati
bhāvābhāvavidharmā ca bhāvābhāvamavaiti kaḥ || 6 ||

When no entity exists, whose nonexistence could there be? And who could understand an existence or nonexistence that is contrary to both?

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

avidyamāne bhāve ca kasyābhāvo bhaviṣyati | bhāvābhāvavidharmā ca bhāvābhāvamavaiti kaḥ || 6 ||

Commentary note on Candrakīrti

Could space be a non-thing instead? Candrakīrti says no: where no thing exists, what could be absent? Space is conventionally described as the absence of obstruction or as the absence of form. But if form itself has not been established, there is no independently existing thing whose absence could constitute space. The non-thing depends on the thing whose absence it is said to be.

An opponent appeals to the person who examines being and non-being: since an examiner exists, the things examined must exist too. Candrakīrti asks what kind of examiner this could be. If it is a being, its existence has already been rejected; if it is a non-being, the problem remains that non-being cannot be established without a being. No third entity, unlike both being and non-being, is found to know them. Therefore neither the examiner nor the two opposites is established in its own nature.

Candrakīrti cites the Buddha: one who understands being and non-being does not cling to any thing and enters the signless concentration. He then cites verses warning that even thoughts about “empty things” can become a wrong path. The words that teach emptiness are themselves conventional signs; the peaceful and subtle nature of phenomena is not captured by conceptual thought. All proliferation of thought is a construction, so phenomena should be understood as subtle and inconceivable.
