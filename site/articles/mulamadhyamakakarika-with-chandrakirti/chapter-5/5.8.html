MMK 5.8

astitvaṃ ye tu paśyanti nāstitvaṃ cālpabuddhayaḥ
bhāvānāṃ te na paśyanti draṣṭavyopaśamaṃ śivam || 8 ||

Those of little understanding who see existence and nonexistence do not see the auspicious calming of what is to be seen among entities.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

astitvaṃ ye tu paśyanti nāstitvaṃ cālpabuddhayaḥ | bhāvānāṃ te na paśyanti draṣṭavyopaśamaṃ śivam || 8 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Thus, when the own-nature of entities has been established in this way, because the eye of the mind has been struck by the darkness of ignorance, because of having practiced beginningless cyclic existence, and because of seeing entities and non-entities and the rest in inverted ways, those who have turned away from the true path of seeing the non-inverted absence of own-nature that leads to nirvāṇa:

“Those of little understanding who see existence and nonexistence do not see the auspicious calming of what is to be seen among entities.”

They do not see the calming of what is to be seen, whose characteristic is auspiciousness, free from every net of conceptual construction, with the nature of the cessation of knowledge and knowable, auspicious, with the nature of ultimate reality: the ultimate, ageless, deathless, non-proliferating nirvāṇa, whose nature is emptiness. Because they are of dull understanding, they are attached to existence and nonexistence. As has been said in the Noble Ratnāvalī:

“A nihilist goes to a bad destination; a non-nihilist goes to a good destination. Through knowing things as they are, one goes to liberation grounded in nonduality.”

And in the Noble Samādhirāja, the Blessed One said:

“‘It exists’ and ‘it does not exist’ are both extremes; ‘pure’ and ‘impure’ are also extremes. Therefore, having abandoned both extremes, the wise person does not take a position even in the middle.

“This is the dispute: ‘It exists’ and ‘it does not exist’; this is the dispute: ‘pure’ and ‘impure.’ Through engaging in dispute, suffering is not calmed; through engaging in non-dispute, suffering ceases.”

Therefore, it is impossible that nirvāṇa is attained by the cyclic path. Thus, in the Prasannapadā, the Madhyamaka commentary composed by the venerable Ācārya Candrakīrti, the fifth chapter, called “Examination of the Elements,” is complete.
