VP 1.2.1

अविकाराय शुद्धाय नित्याय परमात्मने । सदैकरूपरूपाय विष्णवे सर्वजिष्णवे ॥१॥

To the changeless, pure, eternal Supreme Self; to Viṣṇu, whose form is always one, the conqueror of all.

Śrīdhara translation

After saluting Viṣṇu, whose higher and lower forms are the subject of this Purāṇa, the meaning is explained at length, beginning with nine verses headed by “avikārāya.” At the end of the preceding chapter, the Purāṇa’s meaning was stated in brief, including Viṣṇu’s being the material and efficient cause of the world; this could suggest that he undergoes change, so that implication is removed here. In ordinary experience an agent is changeable because he is the locus of action, and is associated with attachment and other conditions; the words “avikārāya” and the following terms remove those implications. A material cause is also seen to take many forms through transformation, as clay or gold becomes pots and ornaments; “sadaikarūparūpāya” removes that implication, meaning “to him whose form is always one.” “Sada” is to be understood with all the terms. “Sarvajiṣṇu” means one who, through inconceivable sovereignty, surpasses all agents, causes, and the rest. To such Viṣṇu the salutation is offered.

VP 1.2.2

नमो हिरण्यगर्भाय हरये शङ्कराय च । वासुदेवाय ताराय सर्गस्थित्यन्तकारिणे ॥२॥

Salutation to Hiraṇyagarbha, Hari, and Śaṅkara; to Vāsudeva, the deliverer, who effects creation, preservation, and dissolution.

Śrīdhara translation

Vāsudeva is saluted in the three forms beginning with Hiraṇyagarbha—Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śaṅkara—which perform creation, preservation, and dissolution. Having set worldly existence in motion, Vāsudeva is now saluted as the one who brings it to an end. “Tārāya” means “to the deliverer,” the one who carries devotees across saṃsāra.

Śrīdhara translation

The Supreme is free from the modifications of being, beginning with decline and destruction. The cited śruti says, in effect, “He becomes as though non-existent if one knows Brahman as non-existent; but if one knows Brahman as existent, they know him as existent,” and another passage says that he is to be apprehended as existence alone. This might seem to introduce the second modification of being, but “he can be described as always existing” means that his existence is not occasional; it is always present and so is not a modification produced by birth.

VP 1.2.13

तद‍्ब्रह्म परमं नित्यमजमक्षयमव्ययम् । एकस्वरूपं तु सदा हेयाभावाच्च निर्मलम् ॥१३॥

That is supreme Brahman: eternal, unborn, imperishable, immutable, ever of one nature, and stainless through the absence of what is to be relinquished.

Śrīdhara translation

The same Supreme Self is called Brahman by the Upaniṣads. The one characterized by the absence of form, colour, birth, and the rest is Vāsudeva and is also called Brahman; it is not another entity, because there is no difference in the defining characteristics. “Supreme,” “eternal,” and the remaining terms repeat those characteristics: what is to be rejected, namely ignorance and its effects, is absent, and therefore he is pure.

परस्य ब्रह्मणो रूपं पुरुषः प्रथमं द्विज । व्यक्ताव्यक्ते तथैवान‍्ये रूपे कालस्तथा परम् ॥१५॥

O dvija, Puruṣa is the first form of the Supreme Brahman; the manifest and unmanifest are likewise other forms, and Time is yet another.

Śrīdhara translation

The manner in which the four forms arise is as follows. The śruti passages “He saw” and “He desired” describe Puruṣa as the agent of seeing and the other acts; Puruṣa is therefore the first form of the Supreme Brahman. Pradhāna and Vyakta are the other two forms, and Kāla is the final form.

प्रधानपुरुषव्यक्तकालानां परमं हि यत् । पश्यन्ति सूरयः शुद्धं तद्विष्णोः परमं पदम् ॥१६॥

That which is supreme among Pradhāna, Puruṣa, the manifest, and Time, the seers behold as Viṣṇu’s pure, supreme state.

Śrīdhara translation

The statement that Pradhāna, Puruṣa, Vyakta, and Kāla are forms of Brahman might suggest that they are effects to be accepted as ultimate objects. That implication is avoided: the wise behold the pure supreme station of Viṣṇu itself, the highest reality, not a dependent product among those four.

The meaning of “without differentiation” is explained by stating their distinctive condition: “not śānta,” and so on. “Without differentiation” means without the distinctions produced by the three guṇas called śānta, ghora, and mūḍha. “This” concludes the creation of elements: the elements and their tanmātras arise from tāmasa Ahaṅkāra in the order stated, not immediately one after another. Some, on the basis of Śruti passages such as “From the Self arose ether; from ether, wind,” hold that the specific gross elements beginning with ether are successively the material causes of the later ones. That conflicts with the Śruti, “Eight are prakṛtis, and sixteen are modifications.” If those [gross elements] were also prakṛtis, there would be more prakṛtis and fewer modifications. Therefore the passage “From the Self arose ether” must be interpreted so as not to conflict with that Śruti. Śrīdhara translation

“Taijasa senses”: some say the senses are effects of rājasa Ahaṅkāra. Others take “the devas” to mean the ten senses together with mind, and “Vaikārika” to mean products of sāttvika Ahaṅkāra. Taijasa Ahaṅkāra, however, impels Vaikārika and tāmasa [Ahaṅkāra] in producing their effects; it alone impels sattva and tamas, as the Vāyu [Purāṇa] states, “Rajas is the impeller there.” Some say Ahaṅkāra is only the instrumental cause of the senses, while the elements alone are their material cause, on the basis of the Śruti, “Mind is made of food, breath of water, speech of fire.” That is not correct: even if Ahaṅkāra is their material cause, the elements can still be named because they nourish [the senses]. VP 1.2.66

Śrīdhara translation

Having indicated Brahmā and the rest, and abiding in them, he himself performs creation and the rest. “Effecting creation, maintenance, and dissolution, consisting of Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva”: the Bhagavān assumes the designation “Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva” in the respective forms. The feminine form karaṇīm, where karaṇam would be expected, is correct as an archaic usage. Or, by a passive derivation, the state of being designated is meant: he assumes the state consisting of Brahmā and the rest, which serves as the means for creation and the rest. Karaṇī is derived from √kṛ with the suffix lyuṭ in the sense of an instrument.

Śrīdhara translation

He exists under different designations among the countless other things that are to be created, protected, and withdrawn. The creator is Brahmā; the created are the groups beginning with Dakṣa; the destroyer is Rudra; the destroyed are the three worlds. By naming them together and declaring them to be his forms, the verse indicates that the creator and destroyer do not differ from the created and destroyed. “The Lord himself” means that he controls the guṇas in accordance with the actions of the Kṣetrajñas; he is not their experiencer.
