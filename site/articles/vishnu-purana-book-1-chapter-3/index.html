VP 1.3.1

निर्गुणस्याप्रमेयस्य शुद्धस्याप्यमलात्मनः । कथं सर्गादिकर्तृत्वं ब्रह्मणोऽभ्युपगम्यते ॥१॥

How is agency in creation and the rest accepted for Brahman, who is without guṇas, immeasurable, pure, and of stainless Self?

Śrīdhara translation

In the world, agency is seen only in a potter and the like: embodied, limited, subject to karma, and fit for connection with it. How, then, is agency in creating the world and the rest ascribed to Brahman, who differs from that?

VP 1.3.2

शक्तयः सर्वभावानाम् अचिन्त्यज्ञानगोचराः । यतोऽतो ब्रह्मणस् तास् तु सर्गाद्या भावशक्तयः । भवन्ति तपसां श्रेष्ठ पावकस्य यथोष्णता ॥२॥

The powers of all entities are within the range of inconceivable knowledge. Therefore, O best among those who practice austerity, Brahman’s inherent powers that bring about creation and the rest exist, as heat exists in fire.

Śrīdhara translation

Powers belonging intrinsically to all entities, like heat to fire, are beyond reasoning but within the reach of the means of knowledge that establish them. Brahman too has natural powers that cause the creation of the world and the rest, known from Śruti: “His supreme power is manifold and natural, consisting of knowledge, strength, and action.” Thus agency in creation and the rest is fitting, though Brahman differs from ordinary agents. “Creation and the rest” means the acts of creating and the rest; “O best among those who practice austerity” is a vocative. Agency is stated here on the assumption that Brahman is bodiless but endowed with power; ultimately, he has a divine auspicious form. Or the objection concerns both material and efficient causation: in the world, the material cause, such as clay, is qualified and limited, while the potter and the like, the efficient cause, has a body and is stained by attachment. How, then, can Brahman, who differs from these, be both causes? Ordinary experience has no such universal rule. Non-conscious substance, though without qualities, is seen as the material cause of qualities; unlimited ether is the material cause of sound and the rest; the bodiless Self is an efficient cause by impelling its own body; one free from attachment is a cause of stumbling, and a magnet, without effort, causes iron to move. Thus the proposed universal rule is broken. The powers of entities that produce their own effects are established by their own means of knowledge and cannot be judged by alternatives such as difference and non-difference, just as fire’s power to burn cannot be questioned. “There is no ground for questioning the power of a thing: fire burns, not space; what is to be questioned here?” “One who examines the teaching of the Ṛṣis and dharma by reasoning that does not oppose the Veda and Śāstras knows dharma; another does not.”

VP 1.3.3

तन् निबोध यथा सर्गे भगवान् सम्प्रवर्तते । नारायणाख्यो भगवान् ब्रह्मा लोकपितामहः ॥३॥

VP 1.3.4

उत्पन्नः प्रोच्यते विद्वन् नित्यम् एवोपचारतः ॥४॥

He is said to be born only by figurative usage, O learned one; he is eternal.

Śrīdhara translation

“Born” is said by figurative usage; he is eternal. Nārāyaṇa-hood is the reason: by his own will, he is of the nature of all. Proper birth is the taking up of a body subject to karma. Or, because Brahmā, called Nārāyaṇa, enters Nārāyaṇa at night and arises in each kalpa, he is figuratively said to be born. Since he lasts for two Parārdhas, he is eternal; though his body is taken by his own will, “born” is figurative because it occurs at a particular time. “Then the universal Self descended, entered the body of the wielder of the discus, and, becoming one with Viṣṇu, attained Vaiṣṇava sleep.”

Word-for-word
Viṣṇu Purāṇa
उत्पन्नः utpannaḥ — born
प्रोच्यते procyate — is said
विद्वन् vidvan — O learned one
नित्यम् nityam — eternal
एवोपचारतः evopacārataḥ — only figuratively

VP 1.3.5

निजेन तस्य मानेन आयुर् वर्षशतं स्मृतम् । तत्पराख्यं तद्अर्धं च परार्धम् अभिधीयते ॥५॥

By his own measure, his lifespan is remembered as one hundred years. That is called Parā, and half of that is called a Parārdha.

Śrīdhara translation

“By his own”: by the Brahmic measure; “measure”: measure of time. Since his lifespan exceeds that of all beings in saṃsāra, it is called the highest.

VP 1.3.9

तावत्सङ्ख्यैर् अहोरात्रं मुहूर्तैर् मानुषं स्मृतम् । अहोरात्राणि तावन्ति मासः पक्षद्वयात्मकः ॥९॥

A human day and night are reckoned as that many muhūrtas; that many day-and-nights make a month consisting of two fortnights.

VP 1.3.24

एकार्णवे तु त्रैलोक्ये ब्रह्मा नारायणात्मकः । भोगिशय्यां गतः शेते त्रैलोक्यग्रासबृंहितः ॥२४॥

When the three worlds are one ocean, Brahmā, whose nature is Nārāyaṇa, lies on the serpent couch, enlarged by the swallowing of the three worlds.

Śrīdhara translation

“Enlarged by swallowing”: at the time of swallowing the three worlds, he is enlarged by the Lord’s exceptional entry [into him]. In another reading, “enlarged by knowledge” means enlarged by the knowledge of the residual impressions of the actions of those beings. The Śrījanmarahasya calls Brahmā an avatāra, like Paraśurāma and the rest; since Brahmā’s acts are the Lord’s own acts, his lying on the serpent couch is not contradictory. His being an avatāra fits his being contemplated by Yogis. Here too, as with Balabhadra and the rest, “Viṣṇu himself abides in the form of Brahmā.” Or the phrase “the Lord alone slept on the serpent couch” permits the couch of enjoyment to be distinct from the serpent couch. Likewise, “the Lord rests in the milk ocean” permits the milk ocean to be distinct from the auspicious abode, divine pavilion, yoga seat, and the rest. In the same way, “Brahmā has gone to the couch of enjoyment” permits the lotus from the navel to be the place of his lying. Viṣṇudharma: “In this world, become one ocean, in the lotus born from Kṛṣṇa’s navel, lying in its interior … having withdrawn the universe, lying on the waters … to the Lord lying in the lotus of his own navel, who has assumed Brahmā’s form … lying in the interior of the lotus arisen from his own navel.” Thus Bhagavān is said to lie on the waters, and Brahmā to lie within the lotus. Harivaṃśa: at the end of the kalpa the devas, together with Brahmā, enter Bhagavān; then the thousand-headed, golden-hued, supersensuous Puruṣa sleeps in those waters. Brahmā, however, moves about in those waters as a light, like a firefly in the rainy season, and lies in the lotus marked by the waters.
