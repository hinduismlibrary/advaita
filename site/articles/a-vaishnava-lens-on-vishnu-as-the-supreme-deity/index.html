1. Preface

1.1. Introduction

The most important aspect of Hinduism is perhaps its deities, and thereby the most important debate is about the status of said deities, especially their relative superiority and subordination. In general the supreme being in Hinduism is called Brahman and different traditions identify different deities with Brahman, the Smartas (at least in their modern form) say Brahman is devoid of forms, qualities or names at a Paramarthika (absolute) level, but at a Vyavaharika (experiential level) is manifest in the form of 5 deities, Vishnu, Shiva, Devi, Surya and Ganesha. The Shaivas say that it is Lord Shiva alone who is supreme, both in Paramartha and in Vyavahara. Similarly the Vaishnavas say Vishnu alone is supreme Brahman at all levels, and all other deities are subordinate to him. These are the three biggest groups, though there are others like the Shaktas who consider Devi to be supreme. This paper will primarily focus on establishing the Vaishnava position of the absolute supremacy of Vishnu over all other deities using a reconciliatory scriptural approach, and will address the Shaiva and Smarta arguments against the same.

This paper is NOT meant to be an attack on the Shaivas, Smartas or any other sect within Hinduism. As fellow Vaidika Hindus they and their theologies are always respectable to us. This paper is meant purely to convey the Vaishnava view, with scripture as the basis, in the spirit of respectful debate as is the long-standing Hindu tradition.

1.2. Methodology

As stated, the paper will treat scriptures as the criterion by which all doctrines regarding Brahman are to be validated (or invalidated). This is because, of the 3 valid pramanas or means of knowledge in Hindu epistemology, viz., direct observation (Pratyaksha), logical inference (Anumana) and scripture (Shabda), the former two are inadmissible in the case of Brahman. It is not something which is seen, heard, touched, smelled or tasted. In the absence of any pratyaksha evidence whatsoever, logical inference also becomes impossible. Thus we can know nothing about Brahman except by scripture. This is clearly established by the third sutra of Vyasa's Brahma Sutras:

"Due to scripture being the yoni (pramana)"

Commentary by Shankaracarya

"As stated, the scriptures, beginning with Rigveda, are the Yoni, i.e., cause (of knowledge) or pramana of Brahman. By the scriptural pramana alone Brahman, the cause of the creation, preservation and destruction of the world, is known. This is the meaning."

This is accepted by all aforementioned schools, who all allege that their doctrine is backed by all the scriptures. Thus we will argue only based on what is acceptable by all of them as Pramana.

Having established scripture as the basis, the paper next defines what counts as scripture. The highest scriptures are the Vedas, or Shruti. "Veda" here means not merely the four Samhitas, as is common in modern discussion, but also their associated Brahmanas, Aranyakas, and Upanishads.

The Samhitas and Brahmanas together form the karma-kanda, the ritual division. Their praises of particular deities occur within ritual contexts and are therefore treated by the source as doctrinally indecisive. The Aranyakas and Upanishads together form the jnana-kanda, the knowledge division concerned with Brahman, the self, and the universe.

Smriti

Aside from the Vedas, there are other scriptures within Hinduism, usually called Smritis. These derive their authority directly from the Vedas and are mentioned in them. They are:

1. Dharmashastras

Primarily featuring the Manusmrti, these are texts dealing largely with social laws.

2. Itihasas and Puranas

Made up of 2 Itihasas, viz Ramayana and Mahabharata, and 18 Puranas, they explain the Vedic teachings through the historic narratives and conversations of the realised people.

3. Vedangas

Made of 6 auxiliary sciences, viz., pronunciation, prosody, etymology, grammar, astronomy/astrology and ritual performance, they are required for the practice of the Vedas.

These scriptures are acceptable to all sects, except perhaps the Puranas, which have clear sectarian divisions within them.

It should be understood that these texts are not merely secondary literature which can be ignored in favour of using the Vedas alone, but are essential for properly understanding the Vedas themselves. The Mahabharata states in its first chapter:

"One should understand the Vedas using the Itihasas and Puranas (for) the Vedas fear the one who has studied little, saying "this one shall misinterpret me"

A note on reconciliation

This topic is of course not anything new, and this debate has been going on for thousands of years. However, in most modern takes on this topic I have seen, I have noticed that, if they even invoke scriptures in the first place, they are largely dismissive or exclusionary rather than reconciliatory. 2. Vedas

2.1. Karma-kanda

As explained earlier, the Vedas have two divisions, the Karma-kanda being ritualistic and eulogistic and hence doctrinally indecisive, and the Jnana Kanda being doctrinal. Hence our focus will be on Jnana-kanda. There is however a common misconception that Indra or Agni or any other such deities are "supreme" in the Vedas or that Vishnu is a

"minor deity", merely because he has less hymns in the karma-kanda, is rejected on its very premise, as they are praised in the Vedas for their importance in ritual, and not for their actual superiority. However, even aside from that, Vishnu is no "Minor deity" in the karma-kanda merely because he has less hymns. It is the quality of the Hymns that matter, what they say, and not their quantity.

Samhita

The Rigveda (10.113.2) clarifies that Vishnu is worshipped due to his own intrinsic nature, whereas Indra is worshipped circumstantially:

"That Vishnu is great by his own vigour and greatness, whereas Indra, lord of wealth, became deserving of honour only after slaying Vrtra with the deities."

"Narayana is the supreme effulgence, Narayana is the supreme self. Narayana is the supreme Brahman, Narayana is the supreme tattva. Narayana is the supreme meditator and Narayana is the supreme meditation."

3. Smritis

We come now to the Smritis, which not only further the conclusion derived from Shruti but also validate it, since Shruti is interpreted in light of Smriti. Here "Smriti" means the Dharmashastras, Vedangas, and Itihasas. The Puranas, though technically also Smriti, are considered separately.

3.1. Dharmashastras and Vedangas

In the first chapter of the Manusmrti (1.10-11), the creation of the world is described, and the identity of its cause is stated to be Narayana:

"Water is called nara, water being the offspring of Nara. Since water was the original residence (ayana) of that being, he is therefore called Narayana. That unmanifest and eternal cause, partaking of the nature of both the existent and non-existent, produced the being called Brahma among people."

"If it be said that the acceptance of Vishnu as superior arose from bias or ignorance, that is not so, because the text says 'along with the rshis.' The rshis perceive what lies beyond the senses; their conclusion can therefore be neither biased nor erroneous."

3.3. Mahabharata

One does not need to look very far in the Mahabharata to find the praise of Vishnu, in fact the very first word of the entire hundred thousand verse epic is "Narayana", the first one saluted in the opening benedictions. In that same opening chapter (1.1.22-24) he is praised further, and identified as Brahman:

"Having saluted Sri Hari Vishnu, the primal purusha, the deity, the one worshipped and offered to by men, the one true imperishable eternal Brahman, who is both manifest and unmanifest, asat and sat, yet also beyond sat and asat, who is the whole world, creator of all, the ancient, the supreme unending one, the auspicious and the cause of auspiciousness, the worshippable, the flawless, the pure, the master of his senses, the master of the mobile and immobile beings, I shall narrate the entire view of the great Rshi, the venerable high-souled Vyasa, of unmatched brilliance."

The Bhagavad Gita

We come now to the crest-jewel of the Mahabharata, the Bhagavad Gita, where Krishna elaborately describes his own supremacy. Before analysing Krishna/Vishnu's supremacy in the Gita, we must address a claim circulating on the internet, that it is in fact Shiva who is speaking through Krishna in the Gita. Though it is sometimes claimed that Abhinavagupta or some other Shaiva Acarya has claimed this, they have not. This claim is entirely a modern imagination. The claim, lacking any support from scripture or even from the Shaiva Acaryas, may be discarded there and then. Even still I shall highlight some points on how it is impossible.

1. As seen above, the Gita is not the only place where Krishna states His supremacy; He does so in the Shanti and Ashvamedhika Parvans as well. The Bhagavad Gita is therefore not an exceptional case requiring the claim that another deity speaks through Him.

"Thus have I heard this wondrous and thrilling dialogue between Vasudeva and the great-souled Partha. Through Vyasa's grace I heard this supreme and secret yoga directly from Krishna, the Lord of Yoga, as He Himself declared it."

4.1. Meta-analysis of the Puranas

The final category of scripture is the Puranas. Unlike the Vedas, Dharmashastras, Vedangas, Ramayana, and Mahabharata, they have clear sectarian divisions. There are Shaiva Puranas such as the Linga, Skanda, and Shiva Puranas; Vaishnava Puranas such as the Bhagavata, Garuda, and Vishnu Puranas; and texts devoted to other deities, each praising its own subject as supreme. The source presents several Vaishnava ways of reconciling this diversity.

I. Difference of scope

Each Purana begins with sages approaching a wise narrator-such as Shuka, Parashara, or Romaharshana-and asking questions. The Purana is narrated as an answer. The source compares their opening questions:

Vaishnava Puranas

(i) Garuda Purana 1.1. The sages ask: "Who among the deities is the supreme Lord? Who alone is worshippable? Who is to be meditated upon? Who creates the world, who preserves it, and who destroys it?"

(ii) Padma Purana 1.1. They ask how the primordial lotus arose, how Brahma was born from it, and how he performed creation.

(iii) Vishnu Purana 1.1. The question concerns how the world arose and will arise again, in whom the moving and unmoving universe depends, from whom it came, and in whom and how it dissolves.

These questions are general: they concern the nature, creation, preservation, and dissolution of the world, and the identity of the supreme deity or being.

Shaiva Puranas

(i) Linga Purana 1.1. The sages specifically request the divine Purana "endowed with praise of the Shiva-linga."

(ii) Skanda Purana 1.1. The rshis ask for the greatness of the trident-bearing Devadeva, including his meditation and worship.

(iii) Shiva Purana 2.1. They request the auspicious forms and divine deeds of Shiva and Shiva (Parvati).

The opening questions of the Shaiva Puranas are specific: Shiva or the linga is expressly named. The source therefore argues that statements identifying Shiva with Brahman function as praise of the stated subject, while Vaishnava Puranas answer general questions about Brahman and the origin of the world by identifying the supreme with Vishnu.

Thus identification of Brahman with Vishnu is primary and taken as a matter of fact, whereas for Shiva and others it is only for praise as subject matter. This is in fact stated in the Agni Purana, which is neither Shaiva nor Vaishnava (Chapter 379.9-12):

"Even the knowledge of Vedas is said to be 'lower' and the imperishable Brahman is 'higher' than it. This word 'Brahman' is principally used only for Bhagavan (the supreme lord), and for other deities as a courtesy or in worship."

brahmashabdo hi mukhyo bhagavatyeva kevalam | anyeshu tuupachaaraartham puujaartham vaa prayujyate ||

Then it describes what "Bhagavan"-one who has bhaga-means: "Bha" means one who destroys or sustains; "Ga" means one who leads or creates. "Bhaga" also denotes the six glories: majesty, valour, success, wealth, knowledge, renunciation, and fame.

The passage then asks who this "Bhagavan" is and identifies the name principally with Vishnu:

"In Vishnu all beings are situated; He is their support and is three-natured. Thus this word 'Bhagavan' belongs principally to Hari Vishnu alone."

Thus, "Brahman" or "Bhagavan" are used primarily and actually for Vishnu, and for others as a courtesy. Accordingly, when a general question is asked about Brahman, the answer glorifies Vishnu. These terms may be used as courtesy or for worship for other deities, as when such questions about specific deities are asked in non-Vaishnava Puranas. Thus the statements of Vaishnava Puranas are treated as plain doctrinal facts, while those of others glorify the subject matter.

II. Guna-classification of Puranas

Another approach to understanding the contradictory nature of the Puranas with regard to the supremacy of a particular deity is the guna-classification of Puranas, argued by Ramanuja and others. The complete classification is given in Padma Purana 6.236.18-20:

"Matsya, Kurma, Linga, Shiva, Skanda, and Agni-these are known as tamasa Puranas. Vishnu, Narada, Bhagavata, Garuda, Padma, and Varaha are known to be sattvika and auspicious, O beautiful one. The Brahma Puranas are known as rajasa. The sattvika grant moksha, whereas the rajasa are generally auspicious."

4.2. Statements of the Puranas

I. Tamasa Puranas

The Shaiva Puranas praise Shiva because He is their subject, yet in many passages they also acknowledge Vishnu as ultimately supreme, even over Shiva.

The Linga Purana acknowledges Vishnu as the supreme lord and creator of Brahma and Shiva, stating:

"The one who is the supreme Purusha, the supreme soul, is Janardana. From him came Brahma, from him Rudra, and in that manner the entire world."

The source notes that many modern recensions read "from whom Brahma and all others," while the reading explicitly mentioning Rudra is cited by Yamunacarya in the Agama-pramanya.

Objection VII: From an Advaitic perspective, all deities are the same being, so hierarchy does not matter.

Answer: the work does not dispute Paramarthika oneness, but addresses hierarchy within Vyavaharika reality.

The paper does not dispute the Advaitin teaching of absolute (pāramārthika) oneness. It disputes the claim that five deities share equal supremacy within practical (vyāvahārika) reality. It argues that Advaitins still recognize social, political, and family hierarchies at the practical level: father and son are not equal in age, nor king and subject in authority, simply because of absolute oneness. On that analogy, the paper argues that scripturally affirmed hierarchies among deities cannot be dismissed on the basis of absolute oneness alone.

The paper says it was written with Smārtas in mind and sought to present evidence for both Viṣṇu’s supremacy and the subordinate status of Śiva and other deities. It says Śaṅkara interprets three Upaniṣadic passages that refer to a supreme being without naming Viṣṇu as referring to Viṣṇu, not to Śiva, Sūrya, or Gaṇeśa. It reads Śaṅkara’s Bhagavad Gītā commentary as placing worship of Śiva, Gaṇeśa, goddesses, and Sūrya below worship of Kṛṣṇa, and as explicitly describing Vaiṣṇavas as supreme. It contrasts Śaṅkara’s commentary on the Viṣṇu-sahasranāma with the absence, as stated by the paper, of commentaries by him on the Śiva- or Lalitā-sahasranāma. It further says that this commentary establishes Viṣṇu alone as supreme in the Vedas and calls Rudra a vibhūti of Viṣṇu at name 233. The paper presents these commentaries as making Śaṅkara’s core commentarial work Vaiṣṇava in character at the vyāvahārika level.

The paper addresses stotras attributed to “Śaṅkarācārya,” arguing that the title was also used by later heads of successor lineages and that attribution to a Śaṅkarācārya does not establish authorship by Ādi Śaṅkara. Because it considers the authorship of this stotra literature uncertain, it says those works should not be used to dispute the position it finds in Śaṅkara’s core commentaries.

It cites devotion to Viṣṇu among Śaṅkara’s successors and later Smārta teachers. The paper says Sureśvarācārya begins and ends his Vārttika on the Yājñavalkya–Maitreya dialogue with salutations to Viṣṇu; it also names Madhusūdana Sarasvatī and Śrīdhara Svāmī as Vaiṣṇava in devotion. It further claims that Appayya Dīkṣita, about seven centuries after Śaṅkara, was the first prominent Smārta Ācārya with Śaiva devotion, and that Smārtas later shifted from Vaiṣṇava marks to the Śaiva tripuṇḍra to distinguish themselves from Vaiṣṇava groups. These historical statements are presented as claims made by the paper. It concludes that Smārta-accepted scriptures and Śaṅkara’s core commentaries support Viṣṇu and his worship as superior within the practical hierarchy.

Tables are not required here; summary:

  • The beginning questions of Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas (Viṣṇu, Garuḍa, Varāha, etc.) are very general and ask who the supreme god is without naming any deity.
  • The beginning questions of Śaiva Purāṇas (Śiva, Liṅga, Skanda, etc.) are very specific and ask about Śiva or his worship by name.
  • Padma 6.236, Skanda 7.1.2.87, and Matsya 56.68: Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas are sāttvika, Śaiva Purāṇas tāmasa, and others rājasā.

Objections

Table not required. Summary:

Why tāmasic texts exist: for gradual upliftment (Kūrma 1.16).

What about worship of other deities: also valid, though inferior, unless done with right knowledge of Viṣṇu as antaryāmin; only worship of Viṣṇu grants mokṣa (Bhāgavata 10.63.28 and 5.5.6).

Why Viṣṇu worships Śiva: he does not worship Śiva but himself as his antaryāmin; he does so to inspire others (Mahābhārata 12.350).

What about Śiva supremacy in some Vedic passages: Rudra is itself a name (115th) in the Viṣṇu-sahasranāma. Śaṅkara says on name 848 that only Viṣṇu is stated to be supreme in the Vedas, as per Gītā 15.15, Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.2.15, and the Mahābhārata.

What about the Śiva-saṅkalpa Sūkta verse: it is reordered using Mīmāṃsā 5.4.1 to say Viṣṇu is supreme.

What about verses of equality between Śiva and Viṣṇu: explained by Gītā 17.18 as hyperbole by a god to his devotee, also seen for Arjuna (Mahābhārata 3.13.40) and Nandi (213.45).

What about oneness as per Advaita: it does not affect vyāvahārika reality; Śaṅkara affirms Viṣṇu’s exclusive supremacy in vyavahāra.

Conclusion

Thus, using close to fifty scriptural citations across Śruti, Smṛti, and Purāṇas, the supremacy of Viṣṇu, his primary status as Brahman, as creator of Śiva, Brahmā and everything else and his superiority over them, has been established. The supremacy of his worship, which is the only way to mokṣa, over that of other deities has also been established. The status of Śiva as having Viṣṇu as his antaryāmin, being his devotee, and being neither fully the same as nor fully different from him, as curd to milk, has been explained. In line with the stated methodology of samanvaya, no pramāṇa has been ignored or disregarded, and all objections have been addressed. Aside from scriptural pramāṇas, non-Vaiṣṇava Ācāryas such as Sāyaṇa, Śaṅkara, Madhusūdana Sarasvatī, and Paṇḍitācārya have been cited as supporting evidence where required.

Though I have intentionally avoided citing Vaiṣṇava Ācāryas in the document, they deserve mention here. The final Padma Purāṇa verse invokes not just the scriptures but also the consensus of the Ācāryas as evidence for Viṣṇu’s supremacy. Indeed, most Ācāryas throughout history have been Vaiṣṇava, belonging to sects such as Śrī, Mādhva, Gauḍīya, Puṣṭimārgī, Rāmānand, and Vārakarī.

I would also restate the disclaimer at the beginning: this work is not an attack on the Smārtas, Śaivas, or any other sect, but presents the Vaiṣṇava stance from a scriptural perspective.
