1. Preface

1.1. Introduction

The most important aspect of Hinduism is perhaps its deities, and thereby the most important debate is about the status of said deities, especially their relative superiority and subordination. In general the supreme being in Hinduism is called Brahman and different traditions identify different deities with Brahman, the Smartas (at least in their modern form) say Brahman is devoid of forms, qualities or names at a Paramarthika (absolute) level, but at a Vyavaharika (experiential level) is manifest in the form of 5 deities, Vishnu, Shiva, Devi, Surya and Ganesha. The Shaivas say that it is Lord Shiva alone who is supreme, both in Paramartha and in Vyavahara. Similarly the Vaishnavas say Vishnu alone is supreme Brahman at all levels, and all other deities are subordinate to him. These are the three biggest groups, though there are others like the Shaktas who consider Devi to be supreme. This paper will primarily focus on establishing the Vaishnava position of the absolute supremacy of Vishnu over all other deities using a reconciliatory scriptural approach, and will address the Shaiva and Smarta arguments against the same.

This paper is NOT meant to be an attack on the Shaivas, Smartas or any other sect within Hinduism. As fellow Vaidika Hindus they and their theologies are always respectable to us. This paper is meant purely to convey the Vaishnava view, with scripture as the basis, in the spirit of respectful debate as is the long-standing Hindu tradition.

1.2. Methodology

As stated, the paper will treat scriptures as the criterion by which all doctrines regarding Brahman are to be validated (or invalidated). This is because, of the 3 valid pramanas or means of knowledge in Hindu epistemology, viz., direct observation (Pratyaksha), logical inference (Anumana) and scripture (Shabda), the former two are inadmissible in the case of Brahman. It is not something which is seen, heard, touched, smelled or tasted. In the absence of any pratyaksha evidence whatsoever, logical inference also becomes impossible. Thus we can know nothing about Brahman except by scripture. This is clearly established by the third sutra of Vyasa's Brahma Sutras:

Brahma Sūtra 1.1.3.

This is accepted by all aforementioned schools, who all allege that their doctrine is backed by all the scriptures. Thus we will argue only based on what is acceptable by all of them as Pramana.

Having established scripture as the basis, the paper next defines what counts as scripture. The highest scriptures are the Vedas, or Shruti. "Veda" here means not merely the four Samhitas, as is common in modern discussion, but also their associated Brahmanas, Aranyakas, and Upanishads.

The Samhitas and Brahmanas together form the karma-kanda, the ritual division. Their praises of particular deities occur in ritual contexts and are therefore not decisive for doctrinal purposes. The Aranyakas and Upanishads together form the jnana-kanda, the knowledge division concerned with Brahman, the self, and the universe.

Smriti

Aside from the Vedas, there are other scriptures within Hinduism, usually called Smritis. These derive their authority directly from the Vedas and are mentioned in them. They are:

1. Dharmashastras

Primarily featuring the Manusmrti, these are texts dealing largely with social laws.

2. Itihasas and Puranas

Made up of 2 Itihasas, viz Ramayana and Mahabharata, and 18 Puranas, they explain the Vedic teachings through the historic narratives and conversations of the realised people.

3. Vedangas

Made of 6 auxiliary sciences, viz., pronunciation, prosody, etymology, grammar, astronomy/astrology and ritual performance, they are required for the practice of the Vedas.

These scriptures are acceptable to all sects, except perhaps the Puranas, which have clear sectarian divisions within them.

It should be understood that these texts are not merely secondary literature which can be ignored in favour of using the Vedas alone, but are essential for properly understanding the Vedas themselves. The Mahabharata states in its first chapter:

Mahabharata 1.1.204.

"One should understand the Vedas using the Itihasas and Puranas (for) the Vedas fear the one who has studied little, saying "this one shall misinterpret me"

A note on reconciliation

This topic is of course not anything new, and this debate has been going on for thousands of years. However, in most modern takes on this topic I have seen, I have noticed that, if they even invoke scriptures in the first place, they are largely dismissive or exclusionary rather than reconciliatory. 2. Vedas

2.1. Karma-kanda

As explained earlier, the Vedas have two divisions, the Karma-kanda being ritualistic and eulogistic and hence doctrinally indecisive, and the Jnana Kanda being doctrinal. Hence our focus will be on Jnana-kanda. There is however a common misconception that Indra or Agni or any other such deities are "supreme" in the Vedas or that Vishnu is a "minor deity", merely because he has less hymns in the karma-kanda, is rejected on its very premise, as they are praised in the Vedas for their importance in ritual, and not for their actual superiority. However, even aside from that, Vishnu is no "Minor deity" in the karma-kanda merely because he has less hymns. It is the quality of the Hymns that matter, what they say, and not their quantity.

The Rigveda (10.113.2) clarifies that Vishnu is worshipped due to his own intrinsic nature, whereas Indra is worshipped circumstantially:

"That Vishnu is great by his own vigour and greatness, whereas Indra, lord of wealth, became deserving of honour only after slaying Vrtra with the deities."

Nārāyaṇa Sūkta, Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 10.13.4

“Narayana is the supreme effulgence, Narayana is the supreme self. Narayana is the supreme Brahman, Narayana is the supreme tattva. Narayana is the supreme meditator and Narayana is the supreme meditation.”

Its best-known recension has sixteen verses in the Rigveda Samhita; the Taittiriya Aranyaka preserves a longer twenty-four-verse recension in the jnana-kanda.

Purusha Sukta, Taittiriya Aranyaka 3.12.1.

"The Purusha has a thousand faces, a thousand eyes and a thousand feet. He pervades the entire world, extending in the ten directions."

Purusha Sukta, Taittiriya Aranyaka 3.12.2

3. Smritis

We come now to the Smritis, which not only further the conclusion derived from Shruti but also validate it, since Shruti is interpreted in light of Smriti. Here "Smriti" means the Dharmashastras, Vedangas, and Itihasas. The Puranas, though technically also Smriti, are considered separately.

Narayana here is identified with Lord Vishnu, the eternal cause and creator of Brahma.

Even in the Vedangas, Vishnu is described as the original and supreme deity. For example, the Brhat Parashara Hora Shastra, attributed to Parashara, father of Vyasa, opens by answering how the universe is created and dissolved (1.9-11):

Bṛhat-Parāśara-Horā-Śāstra 1.9–11

"Vishnu is the unmanifest one, the lord and deity, master of the world, of the nature of pure sattva, ultimately devoid of gunas yet endowed with them (as per his will), the creator of the universe, the husband of Lakshmi, the cause. By a single fraction of his he creates and preserves the world as a lila, his three padas are the nectar of those who truly see."

Show Sanskrit

कथं सृष्टिरियं जाता जगतश्च लयः कथम् ।
एकोऽव्यक्तात्मको विष्णुरनादिः प्रभुरीश्वरः । शुद्धसत्त्वो जगत्स्वामी निर्गुणस्त्रिगुणान्वितः ॥
संसारकारकः श्रीमान्निमित्तात्मा प्रतापवान् । एकांशेन जगत्सर्वं सृजत्यवति लीलया ॥
त्रिपादं तस्य देवस्य ह्यमृतं तत्त्वदर्शिनः ॥

Shiva and Brahma are described separately in the subsequent verses (1.20), without such absolute language.

Bṛhat-Parāśara-Horā-Śāstra 1.20

"Endowed with Bhushakti, Brahma creates, endowed with Nilashakti, Shiva destroys."

Show Sanskrit

भूशक्त्या सृजते ब्रह्मा नीलशक्त्या शिवोऽत्ति हि ॥

Thus, even in the Dharmashastras and Vedangas, which are focused on very different topics from doctrinal theology, Vishnu is still consistently upheld as the supreme, while Brahma and Shiva are mentioned as subservient.

3.2. Ramayana

Near the very beginning of the Ramayana, the supremacy of Vishnu is shown when all the deities, including Shiva, approach Him in prayer (1.15.32):

Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bālakāṇḍa 1.15.32

"Then all the Devas Rishis and gandharvas, including Shiva, along with all the apsaras and respective followers, began to worship Vishnu with divine eulogies."

Show Sanskrit

तदा देवर्षिगन्धर्वाः सरुद्राः साप्सरोगणाः । स्तुतिभिर्दिव्यरूपाभिस्तुष्टुवुर्मधुसूदनम् ॥

Another instance occurs near the end of the first kanda, where Parashurama-himself a great devotee of Shiva-narrates how the deities once disputed whether Shiva or Vishnu was supreme (1.74.14-16):

Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bālakāṇḍa 1.74.14–16

"then all the deities began to ask Brahma to test the relative superiority of the blue throated Shiva and Vishnu. Having known this purpose of the deities, he created a dispute between them. From the dispute a great battle commenced between the blue throated Shiva and Vishnu, each desirous of victory over the other."

Show Sanskrit

तदा तु देवताः सर्वाः पृच्छन्ति स्म पितामहम् । शितिकण्ठस्य विष्णोश्च बलाबलनिरीक्षया ॥
अभिप्रायं तु विज्ञाय देवतानां पितामहः । विरोधं जनयामास तयोः सत्यवतां वरः ॥
विरोधे तु महद्युद्धमभवद्रोमहर्षणम् । शितिकण्ठस्य विष्णोश्च परस्परजयैषिणोः ॥

"then, Shiva's bow, of terrifying exploits, was broken by the mere roaring of Vishnu, and the three-eyed Mahadeva himself was stunned. Then all the deities along with the Rishis and caranas came and beseeched both of them for peace, whereby they both, best of the deities, left. Having seen the bow of Shiva broken by the valour of Vishnu, the deities along with all the Rishis accepted Vishnu as superior."

Show Sanskrit

तदा तु जृम्भितं शैवं धनुरभीमपराक्रमम् । हुंकारेण महादेवः स्तम्भितोऽथ त्रिलोचनः ॥
देवैस्तदा समागम्य सर्षिसङ्घः सचारणैः । याचितौ प्रशमं तत्र जग्मतुस्तौ सुरोत्तमौ ॥
जृम्भितं तद्धनुर्दृष्ट्वा शैवं विष्णुपराक्रमैः । अधिकं मेनिरे विष्णुं देवाः सर्षिगणास्तदा ॥

Thus, the deities and Rishis themselves concluded regarding their doubt on the superiority of the two deities, that it is Vishnu who is supreme. The commentaries on this passage should also be seen:

Commentary by Maheshvara Tirtha Tattvadipika

Maheshvara Tirtha Tattvadipika on the Valmiki Ramayana Balakanda 1.74

Show Sanskrit

जृम्भितमिति । जृम्भितं विष्णुपराक्रमैर्जडीकृतम्, धनुः शितिकण्ठं च दृष्ट्वा सकलजगद्व्यापकं विष्णुं शितिकण्ठपराक्रमैः ज्ञानशक्त्यादिभिश्च अधिकं सर्वोत्तमं मेनिरे

“Seeing the bow and Śiva himself made inert by the valour of Viṣṇu, they accepted the all-pervading Viṣṇu as superior to Śiva in both power and knowledge, i.e., the supreme amongst all (sarvottama).”

A more detailed commentary, addressing an objection of the Shaivas and Smartas, is by Govindaraja:

Commentary by Govindaraja (Bhushana)

Govindarāja, Bhūṣaṇa commentary on Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bālakāṇḍa 1.74

"If it be said that the acceptance of Vishnu as superior arose from bias or ignorance, that is not so, because the text says 'along with the rshis.' The rshis perceive what lies beyond the senses; their conclusion can therefore be neither biased nor erroneous."

It is not actually the narrative itself which is the most important point of this passage, but the fact that the Rishis, who can never err nor be biased, concluded based on this that Vishnu is superior.

Govindarāja, Bhūṣaṇa commentary on Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bālakāṇḍa 1.74

"By this it should be understood that shruti passages such as 'Rudra is one; there is no second to him' are to be read like 'O Indra, there is none superior to you': they state superiority over others within the deity's own domain. By contrast, the shruti 'Narayana alone existed' establishes the supreme reality as Vishnu, another name of Narayana."

Show Sanskrit

एतेन “एक एव रुद्रो न द्वितीयाय तस्थे” इत्यादिश्रुतिः “नकिरिन्द्र त्वदुत्तरः” इत्यादिवदितरापेक्षया श्रेष्ठत्वाभिप्राया । “एको ह वै नारायण आसीत्” इत्यादिश्रुत्युक्तो नारायणापरपर्यायो विष्णुरेव परं तत्त्वमित्यभिहितम् ॥

The Shvetashvatara Upanishad passage is examined more fully in the objections section. Here Govindaraja explains that praises of Shiva and Indra express relative superiority within their respective domains, whereas the Mahopanishad establishes Vishnu's absolute superiority by excluding Shiva and the other deities. The Ramayana then confirms Vishnu's superiority over Shiva.

The Rāmāyaṇa further states Rāma's (i.e., Viṣṇu's) superiority in terms of power:

Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Sundarakāṇḍa 5.51.45

“Neither the four-faced self-born Brahma, nor the three-eyed Rudra, the killer of the demon tripura, nor Indra, the great king of the deities, can save the one who Rama wishes to slay in battle”

Show Sanskrit

ब्रह्मा स्वयंभूश्चतुराननो वा रुद्रस्त्रिणेत्रस्त्रिपुरान्तको वा । इन्द्रो महेन्द्रः सुरनायको वा त्रातुं न शक्तो युधि रामवध्यम् ॥

Near the end of the Ramayana, when Rama slays Ravana, all the deities approach him in prayer again and eulogise him as Vishnu, where he is specifically stated to be Brahman (6.105.12-14):

Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddhakāṇḍa 6.105.12–14

"You are the glorious deity Narayana, the husband of Lakshmi, having the Cakra as your weapon. You are the one-tusked Varaha, victor over your past, present and future foes. You are the imperishable Brahman, the truly real. The greatest dharma in all the realms, you are the four-armed Vishvaksena, wielder of the bow, master of your senses, you are Purusha and Purushottama."

Show Sanskrit

भवान्नारायणो देवः श्रीमांश्चक्रायुधो विभुः । एकशृङ्गो वराहस्त्वं भूतभव्यसपत्नजित् ॥
अक्षरं ब्रह्म सत्यं च मध्ये चान्ते च राघव । लोकानां त्वं परो धर्मो विष्वक्सेनश्चतुर्भुजः ॥
शार्ङ्गधन्वा हृषीकेशः पुरुषः पुरुषोत्तमः ॥

Thus, the Ramayana consistently describes Vishnu as being the supreme deity worshipped by the other deities, being recognised as superior to Shiva by the Rishis and deities, more powerful than Shiva, Brahma and Indra, and directly identified as the supreme Brahman.

3.3. Mahabharata

One does not need to look very far in the Mahabharata to find the praise of Vishnu, in fact the very first word of the entire hundred thousand verse epic is "Narayana", the first one saluted in the opening benedictions. In that same opening chapter (1.1.22-24) he is praised further, and identified as Brahman:

Mahabharata 1.1.22-24.

"Having saluted Sri Hari Vishnu, the primal purusha, the deity, the one worshipped and offered to by men, the one true imperishable eternal Brahman, who is both manifest and unmanifest, asat and sat, yet also beyond sat and asat, who is the whole world, creator of all, the ancient, the supreme unending one, the auspicious and the cause of auspiciousness, the worshippable, the flawless, the pure, the master of his senses, the master of the mobile and immobile beings, I shall narrate the entire view of the great Rshi, the venerable high-souled Vyasa, of unmatched brilliance."

Show Sanskrit

आद्यं पुरुषमीशानं पुरुहूतं पुरुष्टुतम् । ऋतमेकाक्षरं ब्रह्म व्यक्ताव्यक्तं सनातनम् ॥
असच्च सच्चैव च यद्विश्वं सदसतः परम् । परावराणां स्रष्टारं पुराणं परमव्ययम् ॥
मङ्गल्यं मङ्गलं विष्णुं वरेण्यमनघं शुचिम् । नमस्कृत्य हृषीकेशं चराचरगुरुं हरिम् ॥

Conversation between Brahma and Rudra

It is generally understood that of the 3 deities of the Trimurti, it is either Shiva or Vishnu or both who are supreme, and Brahma is lower. However, in the Mahabharata, Brahma is actually superior to Shiva and is stated to be his father, Mahabharata 12.338.11-12

"Earlier, while the intelligent four-headed Brahma was seated on the mountain, his forehead-born son-the three-eyed Shiva, lord of yogins-came from heaven before him and lovingly saluted his feet."

Show Sanskrit

अथ तत्रासतस्तस्य चतुर्वक्त्रस्य धीमतः । ललाटप्रभवः पुत्रः शिव आगाद्यदृच्छया ॥

What follows is a conversation between the two, where Shiva enquires Brahma regarding who the supreme being is (12.338.22-25)

Śiva enquired

Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 12.338.22–23.

Show Sanskrit

रुद्र उवाच । बहवः पुरुषा ब्रह्मंस्त्वया सृष्टाः स्वयम्भुवा । सृज्यन्ते चापरे ब्रह्मन् स चैकः पुरुषो विराट् ॥
को ह्यसौ चिन्त्यते ब्रह्मंस्त्वया वै पुरुषोत्तमः । एतन्मे संशयं ब्रूहि महत्कौतूहलं हि मे ॥

"There are many beings, O Brahma, which are created by you, who are self born. But there is also the supreme Purusa. Who is that one, that even you meditate upon, that purusottama? This is my doubt, answer me this, for it is my great curiosity."

Brahma answered

Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 12.338.24–25.

Show Sanskrit

ब्रह्मोवाच ॥
बहवः पुरुषाः पुत्र ये त्वया समुदाहृताः । एवमेतदतिक्रान्तं दृश्यं नैवमित्यपि ॥
आधारं तु प्रवक्ष्यामि एकस्य पुरुषस्य ते । बहूनां पुरुषाणां स यथैका योनिरुच्यते ॥

"There are many beings, son, as you have said. But there is something beyond this and it is not limited to this, thus should be understood. I shall narrate unto you the basis of that singular Purusa, who is said to be the origin of the many beings."

Thus it leads into the next chapter:

Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 12.339.

Show Sanskrit

शृणु पुत्र यथा ह्येष पुरुषः शाश्वतोऽव्ययः । अक्षयश्चाप्रमेयश्च सर्वगश्च निरुच्यते ॥
न स शक्यस्त्वया द्रष्टुं मयान्यैर्वापि सत्तम । सगुणो निर्गुणो विश्वो ज्ञानदृश्यो ह्यसौ स्मृतः ॥
अशरीरः शरीरेषु सर्वेषु निवसत्यसौ । वसन्नपि शरीरेषु न स लिप्यति कर्मभिः ॥
ममात्मा तव च ये चान्ये देहसंस्थिताः । सर्वेषां साक्षिभूतश्च न ग्राह्यः केनचित्क्वचित् ॥

"Listen, son, of this eternal and undying Purusa. He is said to be imperishable, unknowable (by senses), pervading everything. You cannot see him, neither me nor others, O best of the righteous. He is saguna yet also nirguna, he is complete, visible by knowledge is he said to be. He is present in the bodies of all though bodiless, and though residing so in bodies, he is not tainted by karma. He is my own antaratman, as also yours, as also of others. He is the witness in all, not graspable by anyone in any way."

The chapter continues thus, describing the greatness of this Purusa. Finally at the end, Brahma reveals his identity (Verse 17):

Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 12.339.17.

Show Sanskrit

स हि नारायणो ज्ञेयः सर्वात्मा पुरुषो हि सः । न लिप्यते फलैश्चापि पद्मपत्रमिवाम्भसा ॥ १७ ॥

"He that Purusa is to be Known as Narayana alone, who does not get tainted by fruits as a lotus petal is not tainted by the muddy water."

Thus, upon being enquired regarding the supreme being by Shiva, Brahma says it is none other than Narayana alone. Notably, the supreme Purusha described here cannot be only a nirguna being, as argue the Smartas, for he is explicitly stated to be both Saguna and nirguna. His presence in the material world is compared to the lotus, which, though blooming in muddy water, is not tainted by it.

Vishnu's supremacy stated in Shanti Parva

Another key passage occurs in the Shanti Parva, Mahabharata 12.350.11-18:

"Salutations to that greatly glorious one, the supreme self of all embodied beings: Narayana, the universe, nirguna yet the ground of the gunas. By His grace Brahma was born, and from His wrath Rudra; He is the source of every moving and unmoving being." The passage continues: "From Him came sacrifice and the sacrificer, the supreme Purusha called Aniruddha, creator and destroyer of the worlds. At the close of Brahma's night a lotus arises from His grace, and Brahma is born from it. From His anger a son named Rudra arises from the forehead for the work of destruction. Brahma and Rudra, born respectively from grace and wrath, carry out creation and dissolution under Narayana's command and are merely His instruments."

Show Sanskrit

नमोऽतियशसे तस्मै देहिनां परमात्मने । नारायणाय विश्वाय निर्गुणाय गुणात्मने ॥
यस्य प्रसादजो ब्रह्मा रुद्रश्च क्रोधसम्भवः । योऽसौ योनिर्हि सर्वस्य स्थावरस्य चरस्य च ॥
तदादेशितपन्थानौ सृष्टिसंहारकारकौ । निमित्तमात्रं तावत्र सर्वप्राणिवरप्रदौ ॥

Objection: Could the "Rudra" described here as subordinate be a different Rudra from the supreme Shiva?

The objection is that Brahma's son might be a different, inferior Rudra rather than the deity worshipped as supreme by Shaivas.

Answer: Not so, for this same section describes this said Rudra in the subsequent verses:

Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva 12.350.18

Show Sanskrit

कपर्दी जटिलो मुण्डः श्मशानगृहसेवकः । उग्रव्रतधरो रुद्रो योगी त्रिपुरदारणः । दक्षक्रतुहरश्चैव भगनेत्रहरस्तथा ॥

“Wearing matted hair, residing in crematoriums, having fierce vows, the Yogi Rudra is the slayer of the demon Tripura, the destroyer of the sacrifice of Dakṣa, and the one who tore an eye of Bhaga.”

Unless the Śaivas wish to argue that the slayer of Tripura and the destroyer of Dakṣa’s sacrifice is not the one they worship as supreme, it is beyond reasonable doubt that it is the same one.

Krishna's statement outside the Gita

Aside from the testimony of others to Vishnu's supremacy, Krishna Himself repeatedly asserts it throughout the Mahabharata. The most famous example is the Bhagavad Gita, and another statement appears outside it:

Mahabharata 14.118.37-38.

"All the other deities seek refuge in Rudra; Rudra seeks refuge in Brahma; Brahma seeks refuge in Me. I, O king, seek refuge in no one. No one is My refuge, and I am the refuge of all."

Show Sanskrit

रुद्रं समाश्रिता देवा रुद्रो ब्रह्माणमाश्रितः । ब्रह्मा मामाश्रितो राजन्नाहं कञ्चिदुपाश्रितः ॥
ममाश्रयो न कश्चित्तु सर्वेषामाश्रयो ह्यहम् ॥

The hierarchy that was implicit in the conversation between Shiva and Brahma, viz other deities < Shiva < Brahma < Vishnu, is here stated explicitly by Krishna. This is another example of not just relative superiority, but absolute superiority, which the Smartas cannot reinterpret by their framework of all deities being the same, because not only is Vishnu (as Krishna) stated to be supreme, but Shiva and Brahma are explicitly stated to be inferior and dependent on him.

The Bhagavad Gita

We come now to the crest-jewel of the Mahabharata, the Bhagavad Gita, where Krishna elaborately describes his own supremacy. Before analysing Krishna/Vishnu's supremacy in the Gita, we must address a claim circulating on the internet, that it is in fact Shiva who is speaking through Krishna in the Gita. Though it is sometimes claimed that Abhinavagupta or some other Shaiva Acarya has claimed this, they have not. This claim is entirely a modern imagination. The claim, lacking any support from scripture or even from the Shaiva Acaryas, may be discarded there and then. Even still I shall highlight some points on how it is impossible.

1. As seen above, the Gita is not the only place where Krishna states His supremacy; He does so in the Shanti and Ashvamedhika Parvans as well. The Bhagavad Gita is therefore not an exceptional case requiring the claim that another deity speaks through Him.

Bhagavad Gita 18.74.

"Thus have I heard this wondrous and thrilling dialogue between Vasudeva and the great-souled Partha. Through Vyasa's grace I heard this supreme and secret yoga directly from Krishna, the Lord of Yoga, as He Himself declared it."

Show Sanskrit

इत्यहं वासुदेवस्य पार्थस्य च महात्मनः । संवादमिममश्रौषमद्भुतं रोमहर्षणम् ॥
व्यासप्रसादाच्छ्रुतवानेतद्गुह्यमहं परम् । योगं योगेश्वरात्कृष्णात्साक्षात्कथयतः स्वयम् ॥

Sanjaya therefore identifies the speaker unmistakably as Krishna, not Shiva. Sanjaya's vision was granted by Vyasa and enabled him to see even the universal form; Krishna also identifies Himself as Vasudeva elsewhere in the Gita.

Krishna's statements in the Gita

The Gita's statements of Krishna's supremacy include these passages: Krishna identifies Himself as the origin of all, as unsurpassed, and presents worship of Him as superior to worship directed elsewhere.

Bhagavad Gita 10.8.

"I am the origin of all; everything proceeds from Me. Knowing this, the wise worship Me with devotion."

Show Sanskrit

अहं सर्वस्य प्रभवो मत्तः सर्वं प्रवर्तते । इति मत्वा भजन्ते मां बुधा भावसमन्विताः ॥

Bhagavad Gita 10.2.

"Neither the hosts of gods nor the great rshis know My origin, for I am in every way the source of the gods and the great rshis."

Show Sanskrit

4.1. Meta-analysis of the Puranas

The final category of scripture is the Puranas. Unlike the Vedas, Dharmashastras, Vedangas, Ramayana, and Mahabharata, they have clear sectarian divisions. There are Shaiva Puranas such as the Linga, Skanda, and Shiva Puranas; Vaishnava Puranas such as the Bhagavata, Garuda, and Vishnu Puranas; and texts devoted to other deities, each praising its own subject as supreme. Several Vaishnava arguments seek to reconcile this diversity.

I. Difference of scope

Each Purana begins with sages approaching a wise narrator-such as Shuka, Parashara, or Romaharshana-and asking questions. The Purana is narrated as an answer. Their opening questions differ:

Vaishnava Puranas

(i) Garuda Purana 1.1. The sages ask: "Who among the deities is the supreme Lord? Who alone is worshippable? Who is to be meditated upon? Who creates the world, who preserves it, and who destroys it?"

(ii) Padma Purana 1.1. They ask how the primordial lotus arose, how Brahma was born from it, and how he performed creation.

(iii) Vishnu Purana 1.1. The question concerns how the world arose and will arise again, in whom the moving and unmoving universe depends, from whom it came, and in whom and how it dissolves.

These questions are general: they concern the nature, creation, preservation, and dissolution of the world, and the identity of the supreme deity or being.

Shaiva Puranas

(i) Linga Purana 1.1. The sages specifically request the divine Purana "endowed with praise of the Shiva-linga."

(ii) Skanda Purana 1.1. The ṛṣis ask for the greatness of the trident-bearing Devadeva, including his meditation and worship.

(iii) Shiva Purana 2.1. They request the auspicious forms and divine deeds of Śiva and Śivā (Pārvatī).

The opening questions of the Shaiva Puranas are specific: Shiva or the linga is expressly named. This suggests that statements identifying Shiva with Brahman function as praise of the stated subject, while Vaishnava Puranas answer general questions about Brahman and the origin of the world by identifying the supreme with Vishnu.

Thus identification of Brahman with Vishnu is primary and taken as a matter of fact, whereas for Shiva and others it is only for praise as subject matter. This is in fact stated in the Agni Purana, which is neither Shaiva nor Vaishnava (Chapter 379.9-12):

Agni Purāṇa 379.9.

"Even the knowledge of Vedas is said to be ‘lower’ and the imperishable Brahman is ‘higher’ than it. This word ‘Brahman’ is principally used only for Bhagavan (the supreme lord), and for other (deities) as a courtesy or in worship."

ऐश्वर्यस्य समग्रस्य वीर्यस्य यशसः श्रियः । ज्ञानवैराग्ययोश्चैव षण्णां भग इतीङ्गना ॥ ३७९.११ ॥

"‘Bha’ means one who is the destroyer or the sustainer, it has two meanings. Similarly ‘Ga’ means one who is the leader or creator, O sage. (Or) ‘Bhaga’ are the six glories, Majesty, Valour, Success, Wealth, Knowledge, renunciation and fame."

The passage then asks who this "Bhagavan" is and identifies the name principally with Vishnu:

"In Vishnu all beings are situated, he is the support, being three-natured. Thus for Hari Vishnu alone is this word ‘Bhagavan’ (and thereby ‘Brahman’) principally to be used, and for others as a courtesy."

Thus, "Brahman" or "Bhagavan" are used primarily and actually for Vishnu, and for others as a courtesy. Accordingly, when a general question is asked about Brahman, the answer glorifies Vishnu. These terms may be used as courtesy or for worship for other deities, as when such questions about specific deities are asked in non-Vaishnava Puranas. Thus the statements of Vaishnava Puranas are treated as plain doctrinal facts, while those of others glorify the subject matter.

II. Guna-classification of Puranas

Another approach to understanding the contradictory nature of the Puranas with regard to the supremacy of a particular deity is the guna-classification of Puranas, argued by Ramanuja and others. The complete classification is given in Padma Purana 6.236.18-20:

"Matsya, Kurma, Linga, Shiva, Skanda, and Agni-these are known as tamasa Puranas. Vishnu, Narada, Bhagavata, Garuda, Padma, and Varaha are known to be sattvika and auspicious, O beautiful one. The Brahma Puranas are known as rajasa. The sattvika grant moksha, whereas the rajasa are generally auspicious."

Whichever method is used-analysis of the Puranas' scope or their guna-classification-both lines of reasoning reach the same conclusion: Vaishnava Puranas possess superior authority.

Objection II: Many scriptural sages and venerable saints worshipped deities other than Vishnu. Were they all wrong?

Answer: No, in fact, there is no issue at all with worship of these deities. Kṛṣṇa himself advises it in Gītā 3.11 and elsewhere, stating that devotees of such deities may attain material benefits and may even go to their realms. Their worship is also his worship, just done ignorantly (of his status as their supreme antaryāmin). Even Vaiṣṇavas may worship other deities, though recognizing Viṣṇu as their antaryāmin.

Krishna Himself recommends honouring other deities. Their devotees may receive material benefits or attain their respective realms, and their worship ultimately reaches Vishnu as their antaryamin, though without full knowledge of His position. Temporary fruits are distinguished from moksha.

Objection VII: From an Advaitic perspective, all deities are the same being, so hierarchy does not matter.

Answer: It is not within the purview of the work to dispute the Advaitins’ idea regarding the absolute (paramārthika) oneness of all deities (really, all beings in general), as it is neither within the scope of the document nor required for its thesis. What shall be disputed is the Advaitin idea of the shared supremacy and oneness of the five deities within the pragmatic (vyāvahārika) reality.

The Advaitins do not disregard the social hierarchies (varṇāśrama), neither the political hierarchies, nor the filial hierarchies, based on their idea of paramārthika oneness. All of these are taken as real under vyāvahārika reality. They do not say that the father and son are equal in age, nor that the king and subject are equal in authority, merely because of their paramārthika oneness. If they do not disregard these other hierarchies, they can similarly not deny the deific hierarchies, at least not prima facie on the basis of paramārtha. If the scriptures and their ācāryas affirm a deific hierarchy within vyavahāra, they must accept it.

The entire paper was written with the Smārtas in mind. There was a special effort to present not just pramāṇas demonstrating that Viṣṇu is supreme, but that Śiva and others are not, and he is superior to them. These passages do not fit in the Smārta idea.

Similarly, the paper extensively cited not Vaiṣṇava ācāryas, but Śaṅkara. He takes three separate instances of an Upaniṣadic statement mentioning a supreme being, not even explicitly alluding to Viṣṇu, as referring to Viṣṇu, and never as Śiva, Sūrya, or Gaṇeśa. He wrote a commentary not on any Śaiva Gītā but on the Bhagavad Gītā, and within it stated the worship of the other deities besides Kṛṣṇa, including Śiva, Gaṇeśa, goddess and Sūrya, to be inferior, explicitly stating “Vaiṣṇavas” to be superior. He wrote a commentary not on Śiva-sahasranāma or Lalitā-sahasranāma but only on Viṣṇu-sahasranāma, and within it stated and proved that Vedas praise Viṣṇu alone as supreme, and even stated Rudra to be a vibhūti (partial glory) of Viṣṇu on name 233. Thus, the core commentative work, which is most definitely from Śaṅkara, is unequivocally Vaiṣṇava in characteristic, at least at the vyāvahārika level.

One may object based on the various stotras and such works attributed to “Śaṅkarācārya.” However, there we must note that that vast corpus of poetry is attributed to a Śaṅkarācārya, not necessarily Ādi Śaṅkarācārya. The adjective “Ādi” (first) is used for him because his four successor lineages also use “Śaṅkarācārya” as the title of their leader. Thus, there being no certainty of this stotra literature being composed by Ādi Śaṅkarācārya or some other Śaṅkarācārya, it cannot be used to dispute what is found in his core commentative work.

Even his immediate successors, such as Sureśvarācārya, were Vaiṣṇavas in their devotion. Sureśvarācārya begins and ends his Vārttika on the Yājñavalkya–Maitreya conversation with salutations to Viṣṇu, not any other deity. Prominent Smārta ācāryas like Madhusūdana Sarasvatī and Śrīdhara Svāmī were Vaiṣṇavas in their devotion. It is not until Appayya Dīkṣita, seven hundred years after Śaṅkarācārya, that we see a prominent Smārta ācārya being Śaiva in their devotion. In fact, the Smārtas earlier used to primarily apply the tripuṇḍra tilaka of Vaiṣṇavas, and only later switched to primarily applying the Śaiva tripuṇḍra to distinguish themselves from the rival Vaiṣṇava sects.

From all this it is clear that both the scriptures accepted by the Smārtas, and their own founding ācārya, support the idea that Viṣṇu and his worship is superior to Śiva and other deities and their worship, within the vyavahāra hierarchy.

6. Summary & conclusion

Objections

Table not required. Summary:

Why tāmasic texts exist: for gradual upliftment (Kūrma 1.16).

What about worship of other deities: also valid, though inferior, unless done with right knowledge of Viṣṇu as antaryāmin; only worship of Viṣṇu grants mokṣa (Bhāgavata 10.63.28 and 5.5.6).

Why Viṣṇu worships Śiva: he does not worship Śiva but himself as his antaryāmin; he does so to inspire others (Mahābhārata 12.350).

What about Śiva supremacy in some Vedic passages: Rudra is itself a name (115th) in the Viṣṇu-sahasranāma. Śaṅkara says on name 848 that only Viṣṇu is stated to be supreme in the Vedas, as per Gītā 15.15, Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.2.15, and the Mahābhārata.

What about the Śiva-saṅkalpa Sūkta verse: it is reordered using Mīmāṃsā 5.4.1 to say Viṣṇu is supreme.

What about verses of equality between Śiva and Viṣṇu: explained by Gītā 17.18 as hyperbole by a god to his devotee, also seen for Arjuna (Mahābhārata 3.13.38, 40) and Nandi (Varāha Purāṇa 213.75).

What about oneness as per Advaita: it does not affect vyāvahārika reality; Śaṅkara affirms Viṣṇu’s exclusive supremacy in vyavahāra.

Conclusion

Thus, using close to fifty scriptural citations across Śruti, Smṛti, and Purāṇas, the supremacy of Viṣṇu, his primary status as Brahman, as creator of Śiva, Brahmā and everything else and his superiority over them, has been established. The supremacy of his worship, which is the only way to mokṣa, over that of other deities has also been established. The status of Śiva as having Viṣṇu as his antaryāmin, being his devotee, and being neither fully the same as nor fully different from him, as curd to milk, has been explained. In line with the stated methodology of samanvaya, no pramāṇa has been ignored or disregarded, and all objections have been addressed. Aside from scriptural pramāṇas, non-Vaiṣṇava Ācāryas such as Sāyaṇa, Śaṅkara, Madhusūdana Sarasvatī, and Paṇḍitācārya have been cited as supporting evidence where required.

Though I have intentionally avoided citing Vaiṣṇava Ācāryas in the document, they deserve mention here. The final Padma Purāṇa verse invokes not just the scriptures but also the consensus of the Ācāryas as evidence for Viṣṇu’s supremacy. Indeed, most Ācāryas throughout history have been Vaiṣṇava, belonging to sects such as Śrī, Mādhva, Gauḍīya, Puṣṭimārgī, Rāmānand, and Vārakarī.

I would also restate the disclaimer at the beginning: this work is not an attack on the Smārtas, Śaivas, or any other sect, but presents the Vaiṣṇava stance from a scriptural perspective.

Meta-analysis of the Purāṇas: summary

Tables are not required here; summary:

  • The beginning questions of Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas (Viṣṇu, Garuḍa, Varāha, etc.) are very general and ask who the supreme god is without naming any deity.
  • The beginning questions of Śaiva Purāṇas (Śiva, Liṅga, Skanda, etc.) are very specific and ask about Śiva or his worship by name.
  • Padma 6.236, Skanda 7.1.2.87, and Matsya 56.68: Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas are sāttvika, Śaiva Purāṇas tāmasa, and others rājasā.
  • Varāha Purāṇa 70.20–24: Śiva says sattva leads to mokṣa and is characteristic of Viṣṇu; tamas is veda-bāhya and characteristic of Śiva.
