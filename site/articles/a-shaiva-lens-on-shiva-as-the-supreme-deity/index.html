1. Preface

1.1 Introduction

The question of which deity constitutes the supreme Brahman, the ultimate ground of all existence, has animated Hindu philosophical debate for millennia. The preceding Hari-Tattva-Vimarsha presents a careful & learnt Vaishnava case. This response, composed in the spirit of ancient scholarly debate (vada), presents the Shaiva case: that it is Lord Shiva: specifically Sadshiva or Maheshvara, who is the ultimate transcendental ground of all existence, including that of Vishnu himself as a functional manifestation. This paper does not deny the greatness of the Param Shaiva Bhagavan Vishnu, nor is it an attack on the Vaishnava tradition. The purpose is to demonstrate through the same scriptures & methodology, the scriptural corpus, read without sectarian presupposition, that points to Shiva as the Para-Tattva.

1.2 Methodology

We accept the same hierarchy of scriptural authority, Shruti, including the Vedas & Upanishads, which take precedence, followed by Smriti such as Itihasa, Purana and Dharmashastra. We commit to the Mimamsa principle of samanvaya, reconciliation, and will not dismiss any pramana as interpolation for the sake of convenience.

However, we do dispute one foundational element of the opposing framework before all else: the Guna-based classification of the Puranas. We shall demonstrate this classification as self-contradictory, circular in its application and cannot serve as a neutral between the traditions of Vaishnavism and Shaivism.

2. On Guṇa-Based Purāṇa Classification

The Hari-Paratva-Vimarsha’s most consequential meta-argument is the classification of Puranas by guna, establishing the Vaishnava Puranas as Sattvika, and the Shaiva Puranas as Tamasa. This classification does enormous work in the document, and pre-emptively discredits any statements made in Shaiva Puranas before any verse is considered from a Shaiva source.

AND it is a good argument, however:

To dismiss the Shaiva Puranas just because they are “Tamasic” is to dismiss a body part of Bhagavan Hari himself for even Vaishnavas. The Padma Puran that states this is not a peripheral text to Vaishnavism, but the very heart of Bhagavan Vishnu.

2.2 Garuḍa Purāṇa’s Contradiction

If you read the passage of Padma Puran, Uttarakhanda carefully, Garud Puran, which is classified as a Sattvic Puran, praises a Tamasika Puran as greater than the other Puranas, including the Sattvic Puranas. In Brahmakhanda, Chapter 1, Verse 50 it says:

tṛtīyāṃśa-śravaṇād arthatash ca puṇyaṃ āhuḥ paṭhato vai daśāṃśam | tato varaṃ matsya-purāṇam āhuḥ tato varaṃ kūrma-purāṇam āhuḥ

They say that by hearing a third part [of the Purāṇa], or by understanding its meaning, one attains merit; and by reading it, one attains a tenth part of that merit. They say the Matsya Purāṇa is better than that, and they say the Kūrma Purāṇa is even better than that.

Here, a Sattvic Purana has established the supremacy of the Matsya Puran & Kurma Puran, which are considered “Tamasic” Puranas.

The Matsya Puran has an explanation for this:

In Chapter 53, Verses 67–68, it says: "The glory of Hari is greater in sāttvika Purāṇas; the glory of Brahmā is greater in rājasa Purāṇas; and that of Agni and Śiva is greater in tāmasa Purāṇas. In mixed Purāṇas, the glory of Sarasvatī and the Pitṛs is said to be greater."

2.3 Supremacy of Śiva in Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas

In the Padma Puran only, which classifies the Shaiva Puranas as “Tamasic”, in the 33rd Chapter of Svarga Khanda says, in verses 57-59, Narad Muni establishes Shiva’s supremacy.

Just as Nārāyaṇa, the Puruṣottama, is the greatest among the Devas, and just as Giriśa (Śiva) is the greatest among the lords (Īśvaras), so is this place (Avimukta) the highest among all sacred places.

Note that the identity of Narayan is tied to that of a Devata, while Shiva is identified as Ishvara.

Pūrva Mīmāṃsā Sūtras (specifically the Devatā-adhikaraṇa, 9.1.6–10), Maharṣi Jaimini and the commentator Śabara Svāmī define a deity strictly through the lens of ritual grammar.

त्यागोद्देश्यत्वं देवतात्वम्

The Meaning: "Deity-hood consists solely in being the intended recipient of a ritual relinquishment."

Mīmāṃsā strips deities of physical forms (vigraha) or personal, independent wealth in the empirical sense. Instead, a Devatā is a functional, structural placeholder within a sacrifice (Yajña). They are defined entirely by the fact that a sacrificial offering (Havis) is poured into the fire for them while their name is uttered in the dative case (e.g., Agnaye svāhā—"To Agni, svāhā!"). Thus, a Devatā is inherently bound to a cooperative, cosmic-ritual economy.

In contrast, the word Īśvara does not originate from a ritual-functional category, but from an intrinsic, verbal action in Sanskrit grammar.

The Meaning: "To possess absolute lordship, sovereignty, or to rule unconditionally."

An Īśvara is an independent master who commands, governs, and exercises unchecked autonomy. While a Devatā’s power is relational and structural (dependent on the Veda, the mantra, and the ritual space), an Īśvara's power is ontological and absolute.

In Srimad Bhagavat Puran, the core Vaishnava text of Vaishnavism, Shiva is described as Supreme again:

3. Viṣṇu as Tāmasic & Śiva as Sāttvic

3.1 Śiva Purāṇa on the True Nature of Viṣṇu

For most of this document up until now has only taken references from Sattvic Puranas from the Vaishnava perspective. Even though Shiva’s supremacy is established even while being called “tamasic” in Vaishnava Puranas, it is actually Vishnu who is Tamasic & Shiva as Sattvic from a Shaiva perspective:

Let us understand this from the perspective of the Shiva Puran.

In Chapter 16 of the Rudra Samhita, Brahma explains the true nature of Shiva & Vishnu:

Shiva Purana, Rudra Samhita 16.36

“Lord Shiva is known in three forms: He whose left side is Vaikuntha (Vishnu), whose right side is myself (Brahma), and Rudra is born from His heart.”

Shiva Purana, Rudra Samhita 16.37

4. Double Standards on Relative & Absolute Supremacy

Even setting aside the classification framework, the Hari-Paratva-Vimarsha applies a revealing double standard in its interpretation of passages. This methodological inconsistency runs through the entire document and must be named explicitly.

When Vedic Brahmana passages praise Vishnu — such as the Shatapatha Brahmana's 'Agni is lowest, Vishnu is highest' — the opposing paper reads these as statements of ontological supremacy. When identical language is used for Rudra — such as the Shvetashvatara's 'Eko hi Rudro na dvitiyaya tasthe' (Rudra is one, there is no second to him) — these are suddenly reinterpreted as relative superiority within a domain.

The Shvetashvatara is a PRINCIPAL Upanishad in the jnana-kanda — the section the opposing paper itself designates as 'the actual doctrinally decisive section of the Vedas.' The Shatapatha Brahmana is karma-kanda — the ritual section the same paper admits is 'doctrinally indecisive.' On the opposing paper's own methodology, the Shvetashvatara should outrank the Shatapatha Brahmana. Yet the hermeneutic works in the opposite direction.

The Hari-Paratva-Vimarsha uses a sophisticated move to resolve Vishnu's worship of Shiva: Vishnu, as the antaryamin of Rudra, worships himself when he appears to worship Shiva. This is structurally identical to what Shaiva acharyas say about Vishnu — that he is Sadashiva's Sattvika manifestation, and Sadashiva is his antaratman.

The opposing paper itself cites (from the Brahma Samhita) the milk-and-curd analogy: just as milk becomes curd but not vice versa, Vishnu enters Rudra but Rudra is not the source of Vishnu. The Shaiva inverts this: Sadashiva (milk) manifests as the Sattvika Vishnu (curd). Which came first — milk or curd? The Shvetashvatara Upanishad and the Shiva Purana are unambiguous: Sadashiva is the source. The Mahabharat 13.14, where Krishna himself says this, is addressed below.

Vishnu is "Tamasic internally" because he is "deluded" into sleep on Shesha, whereas Shiva is the "Sattvic internal" instructor of yogis who "never sleeps".

5.2 The Vaiṣṇava Claim: "Relative Superiority" and "Courtesy"

Finally, it claims that the core scholarly work of Adi Shankaracharya is "unequivocally Vaishnava" at the pragmatic (Vyavaharika) level.

While the Hari Paratva Vimarsha uses ritualistic Karma-kanda texts (like the Shatapatha Brahmana) to claim Vishnu's "absolute" supremacy, they dismiss the Shvetashvatara Upanishad—a Principal Upanishad in the doctrinally decisive Jnana-kanda—as "relative". 5.3 "Hara" as the Destroyer of the Root of Bondage

In Hari-Paratva-Vimarsha, Shiva is reduced to a functional "destroyer" of the physical universe, a role subordinate to the "creator", Vishnu. Adi Shankara’s commentary on Verse 1.10 destroys this reductionist view:

6. Supremacy of Śiva in Itihāsas

6.1 Lord Rāma on Supremacy of Śiva

The Hari-Paratva-Vimarsha argues that the deities and Rishis "accepted Viṣṇu as superior" following a functional dispute in the Bālakāṇḍa. It further asserts that the conclusions of Rishis are infallible because they "see even beyond the sensory". However, when the same "infallible" Rishis are consulted in the Mahābhārata, the hierarchy is explicitly reversed.

The Vaishnava document relies on the collective reaction of Rishis to a broken bow. In contrast, the Anushasana Parva of the Mahābhārata (13.14) features the specialized testimony of the Rishi Upamanyu, a sage whose vision "beyond the sensory" led him to become the acharya of Bhagavan Krishna himself.

The opposing document cites the Bālakāṇḍa (1.74) to claim Shiva was "made inert" by Vishnu's valor. This "defeat" is rendered doctrinally irrelevant by the words of Sri Rama in the Yuddha Khanda (6.123.19). After achieving his greatest victory over Ravana, Rama identifies the source of his success:

Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa 6.123.19

At this place [the bridge to Lanka], the Lord (Prabhu) Mahadeva formerly bestowed His grace (Prasadam) on me.

If Shiva were ontologically inferior or "stunned" by Vishnu, the "Sattvika" Rama would have no need for Shiva's Prasada. By crediting his victory to Shiva's grace, the Ramayana establishes that the Bālakāṇḍa incident was merely a functional world-play (lila), while the Yuddha Khanda reveals the permanent truth: Shiva is the sovereign Giver, and the Avatar is the grateful Receiver.

A common Vaishnava strategy to explain Rama’s worship of Shiva is the claim that he does so only to "set an example for the world" and is actually worshipping himself as the Antaryamin of Shiva. This defense is inapplicable to the Yuddha Khanda verse. Rama is speaking privately to Sita; he is not performing a public ritual for the benefit of subjects. He is stating a historical and spiritual fact: his victory was enabled by the grace of the Supreme Lord Shiva.

The Ramayana proves that Shiva is the Sattvic Bestower of Grace who empowers the avatar to fulfill his mission. The "Sattvika" Vishnu is a functional manifestation of the absolute Sadashiva. Rama’s recognition of Shiva as the Prabhu who gave him Prasada is not a "courtesy," but the ultimate recognition of the Para-Tattva from whom all avatars derive their power.
