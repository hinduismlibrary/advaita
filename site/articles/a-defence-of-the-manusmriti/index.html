Introduction

The Manusmṛti or, more properly, the Mānava-dharmaśāstra, is one of the most divisive names when it comes to Hindu scriptures. Even the mere mention of it is sure to cause some argument. It is a book which only inspires intense emotion, be it negative or positive; anyone who is familiar with it is seldom indifferent. On the one hand you have the traditional Hindus, who consider it a very holy scripture and the very basis of social organisation, and on the other hand you have the liberal Hindus or anti-Hindus, who consider it a condemnable text in every way, and some, like B. R. Ambedkar, even thought it necessary to openly burn the text to show their rejection of it. This document attempts to defend the Manusmṛti against this latter group, in light of its actual contents, other Hindu scriptures and historical evidence.

The critics of the Manusmṛti follow a very specific and predictable pattern in their criticism of it. They have, majorly, three criticisms of it. When one fails, they resort to the second, and then the third. The said criticisms are:

  1. That it is only a law book, not a religious scripture
  2. That even if it is a scripture, it has been corrupted
  3. That even if it is preserved, it is no longer applicable to the current age

These allegations are merely the symptoms, and not the actual cause of them opposing the Manusmṛti. The actual reason people resort to finding such pathways to attempt to discredit the Manusmṛti in the first place are the following allegations:

  1. That it is discriminatory to lower castes
  2. That it is discriminatory to women

All these allegations will be examined in turn.

1. Manusmṛti is not a religious scripture

In this regard, the only evidence can be other scriptures themselves. If the other Hindu scriptures attest the Manusmṛti as an authentic scripture, then it must be considered as an authentic scripture. Then, do other scriptures mention the Manusmṛti? And if so, how?

The first pramāṇa presented will be from the Vedas themselves, which are the supreme authority for a Hindu.

Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 2.2.10.2

He should apply the verses of Manu, for verily, whatever Manu has declared, is medicine.

Show Sanskrit

This should, in and of itself, be sufficient to prove the Manusmṛti’s status as a religious scripture. Even still, to demonstrate its authority further, we will see the references to it in the Rāmāyaṇa, in other smṛtis, in the Mahābhārata and by scholars of various sects.

I. Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa

Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa 4.18.31

Rāma said:

I have acted according to these verses, which were declared by Manu, are the progenitors of good character, and are accepted by those well versed in dharma.

Show Sanskrit

He then goes on to directly quote Manusmṛti 8.316 and 8.318.

II. Other smṛtis

The Manusmṛti is also widely praised by its contemporary texts, such as the Nārada, Yājñavalkya and Bṛhaspati smṛtis. Patrick Olivelle, the foremost contemporary authority on the Manusmṛti, writes in the introduction of his critical edition:

Patrick Olivelle, Manu’s Code of Law: A Critical Edition and Translation, introduction

Bṛhaspati, one of Manu’s successors and himself a composer of a Dharmaśāstra, pays Manu the ultimate compliment: Manu is the authority, and any text contradicting Manu has no validity. A couple of centuries earlier, Vātsyāyana, the author of the Kāmasūtra (1.1.5–10), refers to the origin of the MDh in words reminiscent of the first chapter of the MDh. And Yājñavalkya (1.4), another author of an early Dharmaśāstra, places Manu at the head of his list of the authors of Dharmaśāstras.

IV. Commentaries and sects

There are hundreds of references and quotations to the Manusmṛti as an authoritative text in Purāṇas as well, but for the sake of brevity they shall not be mentioned.

The Manusmṛti is also widely revered and considered an authority by all sects or sampradāyas of Hinduism. There exist nine major commentaries on the Manusmṛti, by scholars or ācāryas from all sects. Ādi Śaṅkarācārya, the highly revered founder of the Smārta sect, has quoted Manusmṛti and vouched for its authority many times. For example:

Śaṅkarācārya, Brahma Sūtra Bhāṣya 2.1.1; Manusmṛti 12.19

There exists another Vedic verse of praise (māhātmya) for Manu, which states whatsoever Manu has stated is medicine. And Manu has stated, in all beings is the self and in the self is all beings (exact quote of Manusmṛti 12.19).

Show Sanskrit

Similarly, Madhva and Rāmānuja, founding ācāryas of Vaiṣṇava sects, have mentioned Manusmṛti in their own commentaries of the Brahma Sūtra 1.3.36.

In conclusion, every single scripture, be it Vedas, Rāmāyaṇa, Mahābhārata and Purāṇas, other smṛtis, as well as great scholars from all sects, mention and quote the Manusmṛti repeatedly as a highly authoritative and divine scripture, not merely a law book.

Thus, Manusmṛti is an authoritative scripture of Hinduism.

2. Manusmṛti has been corrupted

Once it has thus been thoroughly established that the Manusmṛti is in fact an authentic scripture within Hindu epistemology, then comes the doubt whether the Manusmṛti we have today is the same one as the original scripture which has been referred to in the Vedas and other scriptures as seen. The critics of the Manusmṛti propose that the text we currently have as Manusmṛti is not written by a single Manu at all, but is the compilation of many sayings attributed to him, and interpolated and edited over a long period. In this regard I would raise a few points.

I. Cross textual attestation

The vast majority of verses found in the Manusmṛti are corroborated and have parallels in other scriptures, such as the Mahābhārata and the Rāmāyaṇa, as shown, as also in the contemporary works of Manusmṛti, i.e. other smṛtis like Yājñavalkya, Nārada and Vyāsa, as well as the gṛhya sūtras and dharma sūtras.

In the translation of the Manusmṛti by Gaṅgānāth Jha along with the commentary of Medhātithi, which is the most widely available translation, he has also included at the end of each verse comparative notes regarding parallel verses present in other smṛtis or dharmaśāstras.

Indeed, there is barely any verse in the whole of the Manusmṛti which does not have some parallel verse in the other smṛtis or dharma sūtras, most of them having several. Thus if a person were to interpolate anything in the Manusmṛti, he would also have had to interpolate these verses simultaneously in two or three other texts, which is absurd.

II. Manuscriptural stability

For a lot of other ancient texts, we see a significant amount of discrepancy in their manuscriptural record. There are multiple regional recensions, each differing by hundreds or even thousands of verses. In such cases the secular scholarship argues that those sections which are available in one particular region, must be later additions, and only the ones common to all must have made up the original, untampered text. This is not seen in the Manusmṛti. There are no competing short and long recensions for it. Manuscripts show the same text with minor variation. The scholar P. V. Kane writes, citing Dr. Julius Jolly, in his book History of Dharmaśāstras, page 140:

P. V. Kane, History of Dharmaśāstras, p. 140, citing Julius Jolly

The extant Manusmṛti is divided into twelve adhyāyas and contains 2694 ślokas. Dr. Jolly’s edition (published in 1895) prepared after collating numerous manuscripts and printed editions contains only one śloka more.

Similarly Patrick Olivelle writes on page 374 of his critical edition:

Patrick Olivelle, Manu’s Code of Law: A Critical Edition and Translation, p. 374

All the manuscripts, and commentaries of the Mānava-dharmaśāstra contain basically the same text. The additional verses found in some can easily be detected. There are no major recensions of the MDh giving longer or shorter versions of the text, as, for example, in the case of the two epics.

III. Wealth of commentaries

The Manusmṛti has the largest number of commentaries of any Dharmaśāstra. There are nine extant commentaries on the Manusmṛti from various regions of India including Medhātithi, Kullūka, Bhāruci, etc. And all these commentaries commentate on the same text with very minor variation, which clearly indicates the preservation of the text.

Dr. Julius Jolly writes in his aforementioned critical edition of the text, page 16:

Julius Jolly, Mānava Dharmaśāstra: The Code of Manu, p. 16

The great celebrity of the Code appears to have guarded it against sweeping alterations, so that the text has remained essentially the same nearly in all Commentaries, however widely distant from one another as to the time and locality of their composition.

This passage has also been quoted by Patrick Olivelle in his own recent critical edition of the text on page 370.

IV. The structure of the Manusmṛti

This is a relatively recent argument, put forth by Patrick Olivelle. Per Olivelle, the Manusmṛti shows a very deliberate structure, such that it must have been written by a single author, or at least under the oversight of one, because such a structure cannot be acquired by a text that was continuously edited and interpolated by redactors over centuries. He writes on page 19 of his critical edition:

Patrick Olivelle, Manu’s Code of Law: A Critical Edition and Translation, p. 19

further believe that this text was authored by a single individual or at least by a strong chairman with a committee of research assistants. The kind of deep structure, so subtle yet so clear, that I have discovered in the MDh makes it impossible to have been composed either through unconscious accumulation or through a series of editorial interventions spanning long intervals of time. It was conceived and put together by a single individual with extraordinary ability and a systematic mind.

In conclusion, the corroboration of the content of the Manusmṛti in other texts, the consistency of its manuscript tradition, the wealth of commentaries on it, and its deliberate structure, clearly made by a single author, completely eliminate any possibility of it having been significantly interpolated in any way, and the text we have now is almost exactly as it was written thousands of years ago and as it has been referenced and quoted in the Vedas, Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata.

Thus, the Manusmṛti is not corrupted.

3. The Manusmṛti is no longer applicable

This will be the shortest section, as, if the previous two arguments have been conceded, this one really has no grounds remaining.

The Manusmṛti itself states in multiple verses, 4.168, 7.18, 9.64 and 10.7, that its injunctions are sanātana, eternal. Thus, if one has conceded the previous two points, and the Manusmṛti is considered an authoritative and well preserved text, this point is automatically debunked.

Moreover, as discussed in section 1, the Vedas, which in Hinduism are apauruṣeya, i.e. eternal and time independent, state that the verses of Manu should be applied. Thus, this injunction is eternally valid within Hinduism, and the Manusmṛti is always applicable.

Before moving forward, an argument often cited in this regard that I would like to address is that the smṛti ordained for kaliyuga, the current age, is the Parāśara-smṛti. This is correct, however, if one is trying to discredit Manusmṛti because of certain passages in it he doesn’t like it, this line of argument will not offer him much benefit, because the vast majority of the Parāśara-smṛti is the same as Manu with very minor differences. Identical especially the most controversial passages which are the reason for people to attempt to discredit the Manusmṛti, as will be discussed shortly. Secondly, just because Parāśara-smṛti is especially ordained for the kaliyuga does not mean that Manusmṛti is invalid in kaliyuga. It just means that Parāśara-smṛti is a little better suited for the people living in kaliyuga.

Thus, Manusmṛti is still applicable in kaliyuga.

Finally, having successively addressed the pathways used by people to attempt to reject the Manusmṛti, we come to the actual heart of the matter, why they attempt to reject it in the first place.

4. Manusmṛti is discriminatory to śūdras

Of all, this is by far the most common allegation levied against the Manusmṛti. If you ask any random person on the street what they know about Manusmṛti, this is most likely what they will tell you.

(A)

The verses most commonly used to paint this narrative are verses like 8.273, which says that if a śūdra arrogantly attempts to lecture a brāhmaṇa on his duty, then oil should be poured in his mouth, or if he hears the Veda intentionally, trying to memorise it, then molten lac should be poured in his ears, 2.272, etc. Within these verses and those like it there are two things that they find objectionable. Firstly, that there is a punishment for such an action at all, and secondly, that the punishment itself is so severe. Let us see both objections.

(I)

Firstly, the restrictions themselves are, seen for themselves, fairly reasonable. Of course, a brāhmaṇa, who has his whole life studied scripture, should not be arrogantly lectured by a śūdra, though the śūdra may politely correct him, as noted by Medhātithi.

Of course, the Vedas, being the most sacred texts and capable of producing results by their utterance alone, should only be recited with absolutely correct pronunciation and intonation or else there will be adverse results. For example, see the story of the birth of the demon Vṛtra, wherein Tvaṣṭṛ, in the Vedic ritual attempting to produce a being who can slay Indra, was supposed to utter a formula:

Vṛtra formula, source locator not supplied in the paper

May the slayer of Indra be nourished.

May the one whose slayer is Indra be nourished.

Show Sanskrit

By the change of a single tone, his whole ritual was reversed. Hence a śūdra should not attempt to listen to it for memorisation without properly being initiated into its study, in which case he will become a brāhmaṇa.

1 See the paper on varṇa at Inquiry into Varṇa.

(II)

Secondly, regarding the severity of the punishments, note that such punishments are mere arthavāda or meant to inspire people to do good or avoid bad, and were not actually implemented. For example, it is said that when one circumambulates a temple, each step he takes is equal to an aśvamedha yajña. Does that mean if one takes 100 steps he is eligible to become the next Indra, Śatakratu? No. In fact, such exaggerated punishments are seen for Brahmins as well.

Manusmṛti 11.90

A twice born person, having, through folly, drunk wine, shall drink wine red hot. He becomes freed from his guilt, when his body has been completely burnt by it.

Show Sanskrit

(B)

This verse which presents a severe punishment prescribed for the brāhmaṇa similarly to the śūdra, brings us to our next point, that such verses that seem to show a particular bias against śūdras are in actuality cherry picked, and in general throughout the Manusmṛti, it is the upper three varṇas, especially the brāhmaṇas, who are most restricted, and the śūdras have the least restrictions. Patrick Olivelle writes on page 41 of his critical edition:

Patrick Olivelle, Manu’s Code of Law: A Critical Edition and Translation, p. 41

The ideology that drives Manu, I think, explains the plan of his book. As we will see (II. 2), he devotes 1034 verses (38.6%) to the discussion of the Brahmin and 971 verses (36%) to matters relating to the king; these two take up 74.8% of the entire text. Manu’s agenda is two fold: he wants to tell Brahmins how to behave as true Brahmins devoted to Vedic learning and virtue, and he wants to tell kings how to behave as true kings, devoted to Brahmins and ruling the people justly.

And on page 16:

Patrick Olivelle, Manu’s Code of Law: A Critical Edition and Translation, p. 16

The Rules for Vaiśyas and śūdras: Manu’s discussion of Vaiśyas and śūdras, the last two of the varṇas, is extraordinarily brief. Eight verses are devoted to the Vaiśya and just two to the śūdra.

Thus, the vast majority of the Manusmṛti is just rules for the brāhmaṇas and kṣatriyas, from which the śūdras are exempt or dealt with more leniently. Following are a few examples of the same.

On the topic of theft, the Manusmṛti states:

Manusmṛti 8.337–338

In the case of theft, the guilt of a śūdra is eightfold, that of the Vaiśya sixteen fold, and that of the Kṣatriya thirty two fold. That of the Brāhmaṇa sixty four fold, or fully hundred fold, or twice sixty four fold, when he is cognisant of the good or bad quality of the act.

Show Sanskrit

On the topic of foreign travel it states:

Manusmṛti 2.24

The twice born people should seek to resort to these countries. The śūdra may however, when distressed for a living, reside in any land.

Show Sanskrit

The Manusmṛti also restricts certain foods for twice borns only and not śūdras:

Manusmṛti 5.19

The mushroom, the village pig, garlic, the village cock, onions and leeks, the twice born man eating these intentionally would become an outcast.

Show Sanskrit

On the topic of eating meat it says:

Manusmṛti 5.33

In normal times the twice born man conversant with the law shall not eat meat unlawfully. Having eaten it unlawfully, he shall, after death, be devoured by them helplessly.

Show Sanskrit

These are just a few examples of many hundreds where the Manusmṛti selectively restricts the twice born men, brāhmaṇas, kṣatriyas and vaiśyas, and gives special leniency to śūdras. The narrative of the Manusmṛti oppressing śūdras, built by selectively cherry picked verses, fails under this light.

Moreover, it is vital to note that the scriptural categories of brāhmaṇa, śūdra, etc., are not the purely birth based castes as they are today, but based on both birth and a person’s qualities, where a person was able to change his birth varṇa by his qualities. I have discussed this in detail in my aforementioned paper on varṇa, and the Manusmṛti also hints towards it in many verses.

For example:

Manusmṛti 8.102

He shall treat like śūdras the Brāhmaṇas who tend cattle, who engage in trade, and who are craftsmen, actors, menial servants or money lenders.

Show Sanskrit

In conclusion, the verses which are used to show Manusmṛti as discriminatory to śūdras are cherry picked, the restrictions imposed by them largely justified, the punishments prescribed non literal, and the allegations ignorant of the real basis of varṇa.

Thus, the Manusmṛti is not discriminatory to śūdras.

5. The Manusmṛti is discriminatory to women

Before addressing verses that the critics claim are discriminatory to women, I would first of all like to quote some verses which are extremely positive about them.

Manusmṛti tells us to revere women, stating:

Manusmṛti 3.56

Where women are worshipped, there the gods rejoice. Where they are not worshipped, there all deeds and rites are fruitless.

Show Sanskrit

Manusmṛti also does not mention barbaric practices like sati, as is commonly said about it, but in fact opposes it.

Manusmṛti 5.155

[After her husband dies] If she wishes, she may emaciate her body by living on auspicious flowers, roots and fruits, but she should not even take the name of another man.

Show Sanskrit

Medhātithi, the most authentic commentator on the Manusmṛti, writes on this verse:

Medhātithi on Manusmṛti 5.155

From this it is clear that the act of killing herself after her husband is clearly forbidden for the woman. Further, in view of the distinct Vedic text, one shall not die before the span of his life is run out, being contradicted by the Smṛti text of Āṅgiras, this latter is open to be assumed to have some other meaning.

Another allegation commonly levied against the Manusmṛti is on the basis of the following verse:

Manusmṛti 9.3

The father guards her during virginity, the husband guards her in youth, the sons guard her in old age. The woman is never fit for independence.

Show Sanskrit

It is really the final line, the woman is never fit for independence, that is quoted and scrutinised. Indeed, this is unfair, because the word independence here should be considered given the context. Does the verse say that she will be ruled over by her father, husband, sons, etc.? No. Indeed the verse is purely about protection, that a woman should never be left alone without protection. This is not merely a modern attempt at re interpretation but acknowledged by Medhātithi in his commentary:

Medhātithi on Manusmṛti 9.3

The present text does not lay down that she shall not be independent in regard to anything at all. All that it means is that she is not capable of guarding herself, especially as she does not possess the requisite strength.

There are many other misinterpreted verses like this one that are a result of colonial era mistranslation further exacerbated by the Manusmṛti being targeted by reform movements like that of B. R. Ambedkar as an easy scapegoat, but all of them similarly fall apart under slightest scrutiny.

In conclusion, the Manusmṛti highly reveres women, does not endorse but in fact prohibits practices like sati, and the allegations against it with regards to a few cherry picked verses is due to deliberate mistranslation and misinterpretation.

Thus, the Manusmṛti is not discriminatory against women.
