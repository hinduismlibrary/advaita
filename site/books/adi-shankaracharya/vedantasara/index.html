काम्यानि स्वर्गादीष्टसाधनानि ज्योतिष्टोमादीनि। निषिद्धानि नरकाद्यनिष्ट-साधनानि ब्राह्मणहननादीनि । नित्यान्यकरणे प्रत्यवायसाधनानि सन्ध्यावन्दनादीनि। नैमित्तिकानि पुत्रजन्माद्यनुबन्धीनि जातेष्ट्यादीनि। प्रायश्चित्तानि पापक्षयसाधनानि चान्द्रायणादीनि। उपासनानि सगुण-ब्रह्मविषयमानसव्यापाररूपाणि शाण्डिल्यविद्यादीनि। एतेषां नित्यादीनां बुद्धिशुद्धिः परं प्रयोजनमुपासनानां तु चित्तैकाग्र्यं “तमेतमात्मानं वेदानुवचनेन ब्राह्मणा विविदिषन्ति यज्ञेन ” इत्यादिश्रुतेः “तपसा कल्मषं हन्ति” इत्यादि स्मृतेश्च। नित्य नैमित्तिक प्रायश्चित्तोपासनानां त्ववान्तरफलं पितृलोकसत्यलोकप्राप्तिः "कर्मणा पितृलोको विद्यया देवलोक" इत्यादिश्रुतेः।

Desire-driven rites are means to desired results such as heaven, for example the Jyotiṣṭoma. Forbidden acts lead to unwanted results such as hell, for example killing a Brahmin. Daily duties, such as twilight worship, cause a fault when omitted. Occasional duties, such as birth rites, are connected with events such as the birth of a son. Expiatory rites, such as the Cāndrāyaṇa, remove sin. Contemplative practices, such as the Śāṇḍilya meditation, are mental activities directed toward Brahman with attributes. The primary result of daily duties and the like is clarity of understanding, while contemplation produces steadiness of mind. Scripture says that Brahmins seek to know the Self through Vedic recitation and sacrifice; remembered teaching says that austerity destroys impurity. An intermediate result of daily, occasional, expiatory, and contemplative practices is attainment of the world of the ancestors or the world of Brahmā, as scripture teaches.

साधनानि नित्यानित्यवस्तुविवेकेहामुत्रार्थफलभोगविरागशमादिषट्क-सम्पत्तिमुमुक्षुत्वानि। नित्यानित्यवस्तुविवेकस्तावद् ब्रह्मैव नित्यं वस्तु ततोऽन्यदखिलमनित्यमिति विवेचनम्। ऐहिकानां स्रक्चन्दनवनितादि-विषयभोगानां कर्मजन्यतयानित्यत्ववदामुष्मिकाणामप्यमृतादिविषय-भोगानामनित्यतया तेभ्यो नितरां विरतिरिहामुत्रार्थफलभोगविरागः।

The four qualifications are discrimination between the eternal and the non-eternal, dispassion toward enjoyment of the fruits of action here and hereafter, the sixfold discipline beginning with tranquillity, and the desire for liberation. Discrimination means discerning that Brahman alone is eternal and everything else is non-eternal. Just as worldly enjoyments—garlands, sandalwood, women, and the like—are impermanent because they arise from action, so too the otherworldly enjoyments of objects such as immortality are impermanent. Complete withdrawal from them is dispassion toward the fruits enjoyed here and hereafter.

शमादयस्तु शमदमोपरतितितिक्षासमाधानश्रद्धाख्याः। शमः तावच्छ्रवणादिव्यतिरिक्तविषयेभ्यो मनसो निग्रहः। दमो बाह्येन्द्रियाणां तद्-व्यतिरिक्तविषयेभ्यो निवर्तनम्। निवर्तितानामेतेषां तद्-व्यतिरिक्तविषयेभ्य उपरमणमुपरतिरथवा विहितानां कर्मणां विधिना परित्यागः। तितिक्षा शीतोष्णद्वन्द्वसहिष्णुता। निगृहीतस्य मनसः श्रवणादौ तदनुगुणविषये च समाधिः समाधानम् । गुरूपदिष्ट-वेदान्तवाक्येषु विश्वासः श्रद्धा। मुमुक्षुत्वं मोक्षेच्छा। एवम्भूतः प्रमाताधिकारी "शान्तो दान्त" इत्यादिश्रुतेः।

The six disciplines are tranquillity, sense-restraint, withdrawal, endurance, collectedness, and trust. Tranquillity is control of the mind away from objects other than those involved in hearing the teaching and related practices. Sense-restraint is turning the external senses away from other objects. Withdrawal is the resting of these restrained senses from those objects, or the prescribed renunciation of ritual acts. Endurance is bearing pairs of opposites such as heat and cold. Collectedness is the mind, once restrained, being fixed on hearing and other appropriate subjects. Trust is confidence in the Vedānta statements taught by the teacher. The desire for liberation is longing for liberation. Such an inquirer is qualified, as scripture says, “peaceful, self-controlled.”

विषयो जीवब्रहौक्यं शुद्धचैतन्यं प्रमेयं तत्रैव वेदान्तानां तात्पर्यात्। सम्बन्धस्तु तदैक्यप्रमेयस्य तत्प्रतिपादकोपनिषत्प्रमाणस्य बोध्यबोधक-भावः। प्रयोजनं तु तदैक्यप्रमेयगताज्ञाननिवृत्तिः स्वस्वरूपानन्दावाप्तिश्च "तरति शोकमात्मवित्" इत्यादिश्रुतेः "ब्रह्मविद् ब्रह्मैव भवति" इत्यादि श्रुतिश्च॥४॥

The subject is the identity of the individual self and Brahman, pure consciousness, because that is the Upaniṣads’ central teaching. The relation is that of what is to be understood to the Upaniṣadic means of knowledge that teaches this identity. The purpose is the removal of ignorance concerning this identity and the attainment of the bliss that is one’s own nature, as scripture says, “the knower of the Self crosses sorrow” and “the knower of Brahman becomes Brahman itself.”

अयमधिकारी जननमरणादिसंसारानलसन्तप्तो प्रदीप्तशिरोजल-राशिमिवोपहारपाणिः श्रोत्रियं ब्रह्मनिष्ठं गुरुमुपसृत्य तमनुसरति "समित्पाणिः श्रोत्रियं ब्रह्मनिष्ठं" इत्यादिश्रुतेः। स गुरुः परम-कृपयाध्यारोपापवादन्यायेनैनमुपदिशति "तस्मै स विद्वानुपसन्नाय प्राह " इत्यादि श्रुतेः॥५॥

This qualified student, scorched by the fire of worldly existence—birth, death, and the rest—approaches a teacher who has studied the Veda and is established in Brahman, like one carrying water to extinguish a blaze upon one’s own head, and follows the teacher, as scripture says, “with fuel in hand, approach a teacher learned in the Veda and established in Brahman.” Out of supreme compassion, the teacher instructs the student by the method of superimposition and rescission, as scripture says, “to that student who has approached, the wise one spoke.”

The subtle bodies, also called liṅga bodies, have seventeen components: the five organs of knowledge, intellect and mind, the five organs of action, and the five vital airs. The organs of knowledge are hearing, touch, sight, taste, and smell. They arise separately and in order from the sattva portions of space and the other elements. Intellect is the inner-organ activity whose nature is determination. Mind is the inner-organ activity whose nature is resolving and doubting. Memory and ego are included within these two. These arise from the combined sattva portions of space and the other elements. Because they are luminous in nature, they are products of the sattva portions. Intellect together with the organs of knowledge forms the sheath of understanding. Identifying with agency, enjoyment, happiness, sorrow, and the rest, the empirical individual travels in this world and the next. Mind together with the organs of knowledge forms the sheath of mind.

Some authorities say that there are five other vital airs, called Nāga, Kūrma, Kṛkala, Devadatta, and Dhanañjaya. Nāga causes belching; Kūrma causes blinking; Kṛkala causes sneezing; Devadatta causes yawning; and Dhanañjaya nourishes the body. Some say there are only the five primary airs because these five functions are included among prāṇa and the others. This group of five airs arises from the combined rajas portions of space and the other elements. Joined with the organs of action, they form the sheath of vital air. Since they are active in nature, they are products of the rajas portions.

Here, too, the entire subtle body is the collective when it is the object of one cognition, like a forest or reservoir; it is the individual when it is the object of many cognitions, like trees or waters. Consciousness associated with this collective is called Sūtrātman, Hiraṇyagarbha, or Prāṇa because it runs through everything and is associated with the powers of knowledge, will, and action. Compared with the gross world, this collective aspect is the subtle body, the three sheaths beginning with the sheath of understanding, the dream state because it consists of impressions from waking, and the place where the gross world dissolves. Consciousness associated with the individual aspect is Taijasa because it is conditioned by the luminous inner organ. For the same reason, this individual aspect is also called the subtle body, the three sheaths beginning with the sheath of understanding, the dream state because it consists of waking impressions, and the place where the gross body dissolves. At that time, Sūtrātman and Taijasa experience subtle objects through mental modifications, as scripture says, “Taijasa enjoys the subtle.” Here, too, the collective and individual aspects, and Sūtrātman and Taijasa associated with them, are non-different, like a forest and its trees, the space delimited by them, a reservoir and its waters, or the reflected space in its water. Thus the subtle body arises.

From these fivefold-combined gross elements arise the worlds above—Bhūḥ, Bhuvaḥ, Svaḥ, Mahaḥ, Janaḥ, Tapaḥ, and Satya—as well as the worlds below—Atala, Vitala, Sutala, Rasātala, Talātala, Mahātala, and Pātāla. The cosmic egg arises, together with the four kinds of gross bodies within it and the foods and drinks suitable to them. The four kinds of bodies are womb-born, egg-born, sprout-born, and moisture-born. Humans, animals, and the like are womb-born; birds and serpents are egg-born; vines and trees that emerge through the earth are sprout-born; and lice, mosquitoes, and the like are moisture-born.

Here, too, the totality of the four kinds of gross bodies is the collective when it is the object of one or many cognitions, like a forest or a reservoir; it is the individual when considered as many, like trees or waters. Consciousness associated with the collective is called Vaiśvānara or Virāj because it identifies with all people and shines in diverse forms. The collective aspect is the gross body because it is a modification of food, the sheath of food, the support of gross experience, and the waking state. Consciousness associated with the individual is called Viśva because, without giving up identification with the subtle body, it has entered the gross body and the rest. This individual aspect is likewise the gross body, the sheath of food because it is a modification of food, and the waking state. At that time, Viśva and Vaiśvānara experience all gross objects in sequence: sounds, touches, forms, tastes, and smells through the five sense organs beginning with hearing, governed respectively by the deities of the directions, wind, sun, Varuṇa, and the Aśvins; speech, taking, movement, excretion, and pleasure through the five organs of action beginning with speech, governed respectively by Agni, Indra, Upendra, Yama, and Prajāpati; and resolve, determination, ego-making, and memory through the four inner organs called mind, intellect, ego, and memory, governed respectively by the moon, the four-faced creator, Śaṅkara, and Acyuta. This is supported by scripture’s statement, “the waking state is outwardly cognitive.” Here, too, the individual and collective gross bodies and Viśva and Vaiśvānara associated with them are non-different, as previously explained by the examples of a forest and its trees, delimited space, a reservoir and its water, and reflected space. Thus the gross universe arises from the fivefold-combined elements.

The collective of the gross, subtle, and causal worlds is one great universe, just as several forests together form one great forest, or several reservoirs form one great reservoir. The consciousness associated with it—from Vaiśvānara through Īśvara—is also one, like the space reflected in many forests or reservoirs. The consciousness that is not associated with these two—the great universe and the consciousness conditioned by it—is undivided from them like a red-hot iron ball. It is the direct meaning of the statement “All this is indeed Brahman,” and, when distinguished from them, its indicated meaning. Thus superimposition, the attribution of the unreal to the real, has been shown in general.

The false views about the Self are now set out in detail: “I superimpose this, this, that, and that upon the inner Self.” The very unsophisticated person says that the son is the Self, citing “The Self is born as a son,” and because love is seen for one’s son as for oneself; when the son thrives or perishes, one says, “I myself have thrived” or “I myself have perished.” A materialist says that the gross body is the Self, citing “This person is made of the essence of food,” and because one leaves one’s own son behind in a burning house to escape and says, “I am stout” or “I am thin.” Another materialist says that the senses are the Self, citing the scripture in which the vital functions approach their father, Prajāpati, and because without the senses the body does not move; one says, “I am blind” or “I am deaf.” Another says that the vital air is the Self, citing “There is another inner Self, made of vital air,” and because without vital air the senses cannot move; one says, “I am hungry” or “I am thirsty.” Another materialist says that the mind is the Self, citing “There is another inner Self, made of mind,” and because when the mind sleeps the vital functions and the rest are absent; one says, “I intend” or “I doubt.” A Buddhist says that the intellect is the Self, citing “There is another inner Self, made of understanding,” and because instruments have no power without an agent; one says, “I act” or “I enjoy.” The Prābhākara and the logician say that ignorance is the Self, citing “There is another inner Self, made of bliss,” and because intellect and the rest are seen to resolve into ignorance; one says, “I am ignorant.” The Bhāṭṭa says that consciousness conditioned by ignorance is the Self, citing “It is a mass of knowledge, made of bliss,” and because in deep sleep there is both illumination and non-illumination; one wakes and says, “I did not know myself.” Another Buddhist says that the void is the Self, citing “All this was non-being in the beginning,” and because in deep sleep everything is absent; one wakes and recalls the experience “I was not in deep sleep.”

The son and the rest are said to be not-Self. The apparent scriptural statements, arguments, and experiences put forward by the very unsophisticated theorists are successively contradicted as accounts of the Self by stronger scriptural statements, arguments, and experiences; therefore the non-Self nature of the son and the rest is clear. Moreover, powerful scripture teaches that the inner Self is not gross, has no eyes, vital air, or mind, is not an agent, and is pure consciousness and reality. The inert things from the son down to the void are illumined by consciousness and impermanent like a pot. The strong experience of the wise, “I am Brahman,” also defeats those apparent scriptural statements, arguments, and experiences. Therefore everything from the son to the void is indeed not-Self. The experience of Vedānta knowers is that the inward consciousness, which illumines all of them and whose nature is eternal, pure, awakened, liberated, and real, alone is the Self. Co-reference is illustrated by the sentence “That is this Devadatta.” The word “that” denotes Devadatta qualified by a past time, while “this” denotes Devadatta qualified by the present time; both words intend one and the same person. Likewise, in “That thou art,” the word “That” denotes consciousness qualified by remoteness and the like, while “thou” denotes consciousness qualified by immediacy and the like; their intended reference is the same consciousness. The relation of qualifier and qualified is also illustrated by this sentence: the meanings of “that” and “this,” Devadatta qualified by past and present time, respectively, exclude their mutual difference and qualify one another. Similarly, the meanings of “That” and “thou,” consciousness qualified by remoteness and immediacy, exclude mutual difference and qualify one another. The relation of indicator and indicated is also illustrated by the same sentence: when the incompatible qualifications of past and present time are given up, the words “that” and “this” indicate the non-conflicting Devadatta. Likewise, in the great sentence, when the incompatible qualifications of remoteness and immediacy are given up, “That” and “thou” indicate the non-conflicting consciousness. This is called the method of partial implication.

The meaning of the experience “I am Brahman” is now explained. After the teacher has clarified the meanings of “That” and “thou” by first teaching superimposition and rescission, and the student has been shown the indivisible meaning of the sentence, a mental modification shaped as indivisible reality arises: “I am Brahman, eternal, pure, awakened, liberated, real in nature, supreme bliss, infinite, and non-dual.” Containing a reflection of consciousness, that mental modification takes as its object the supreme Brahman, unknown and non-different from the inner Self, and removes the ignorance about it. The reflected consciousness within it is overwhelmed by Brahman, just as a lamp’s light, unable to illumine sunlight, is overwhelmed by it. The result is the benefit stated in the text as arising from knowledge of its subject matter, or from practicing that knowledge. Its limbs are restraint, observances, posture, regulation of breath, withdrawal of the senses, concentration, meditation, and samādhi. Restraints are nonviolence, truth, non-stealing, continence, and non-possessiveness. Observances are cleanliness, contentment, austerity, study, and dedication to the Lord. Remaining the illuminator of the inner organ, its reflections, and the rest, when those experiences come to an end and the vital force dissolves into the inward blissful supreme Brahman, ignorance and even its residual impressions perish. The knower remains as absolute liberation, an indivisible Brahman whose nature is a single, undivided bliss, free from every appearance of difference. Scripture says, “His vital forces do not depart,” “Here they are completely dissolved,” and “having been freed, he is released.”
