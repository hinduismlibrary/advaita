1. Preface

1.1. Introduction

The most important aspect of Hinduism is perhaps its deities, and thereby the most important debate is about the status of said deities, especially their relative superiority and subordination. In general the supreme being in Hinduism is called Brahman and different traditions identify different deities with Brahman, the Smārtas (at least in their modern form) say Brahman is devoid of forms, qualities or names at a Pāramārthika (absolute) level, but at a Vyāvahārika (experiential level) is manifest in the form of 5 deities, Viṣṇu, Śiva, Devī, Sūrya and Gaṇeśa. The Śaivas say that it is Lord Śiva alone who is supreme, both in Pāramārtha and in Vyāvahāra. Similarly the Vaiṣṇavas say Viṣṇu alone is supreme Brahman at all levels, and all other deities are subordinate to him. These are the three biggest groups, though there are others like the Śāktas who consider Devī to be supreme. This paper will primarily focus on establishing the Vaiṣṇava position of the absolute supremacy of Viṣṇu over all other deities using a reconciliatory scriptural approach, and will address the Śaiva and Smārta arguments against the same.

This paper is NOT meant to be an attack on the Śaivas, Smārtas or any other sect within Hinduism. As fellow Vaidika Hindus they and their theologies are always respectable to us. This paper is meant purely to convey the Vaiṣṇava view, with scripture as the basis, in the spirit of respectful debate as is the long-standing Hindu tradition.

1.2. Methodology

As stated, the paper will treat scriptures as the criterion by which all doctrines regarding Brahman are to be validated (or invalidated). This is because, of the 3 valid pramāṇas or means of knowledge in Hindu epistemology, viz., direct observation (Pratyakṣa), logical inference (Anumāna) and scripture (Śabda), the former two are inadmissible in the case of Brahman. It is not something which is seen, heard, touched, smelled or tasted. In the absence of any pratyakṣa evidence whatsoever, logical inference also becomes impossible. Thus we can know nothing about Brahman except by scripture. This is clearly established by the third sūtra of Vyāsa’s Brahma Sūtras:

“Due to scripture being the yoni (pramāṇa)”

Commentary by Śaṅkarācārya

“As stated, the scriptures, beginning with Ṛgveda, are the Yoni, i.e., cause (of knowledge) or pramāṇa of Brahman. By the scriptural pramāṇa alone Brahman, the cause of the creation, preservation and destruction of the world, is known. This is the meaning.”

This is accepted by all aforementioned schools, who all allege that their doctrine is backed by all the scriptures. Thus we will argue only based on what is acceptable by all of them as Pramāṇa.

Having established scripture as the basis, the paper next defines what counts as scripture. The highest scriptures are the Vedas, or Śruti. “Veda” here means not merely the four Saṃhitās, as is common in modern discussion, but also their associated Brāhmaṇas, Āraṇyakas, and Upaniṣads.

The Saṃhitās and Brāhmaṇas together form the karma-kāṇḍa, the ritual division. Their praises of particular deities occur within ritual contexts and are therefore treated by the source as doctrinally indecisive. The Āraṇyakas and Upaniṣads together form the jñāna-kāṇḍa, the knowledge division concerned with Brahman, the self, and the universe.

Smṛti

Aside from the Vedas, there are other scriptures within Hinduism, usually called Smṛtis. These derive their authority directly from the Vedas and are mentioned in them. They are:

1. Dharmaśāstras

Primarily featuring the Manusmṛti, these are texts dealing largely with social laws.

2. Itihāsas and Purāṇas

Made up of 2 Itihāsas, viz Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata, and 18 Purāṇas, they explain the Vedic teachings through the historic narratives and conversations of the realised people.

3. Vedāṅgas

Made of 6 auxiliary sciences, viz., pronunciation, prosody, etymology, grammar, astronomy/astrology and ritual performance, they are required for the practice of the Vedas.

These scriptures are acceptable to all sects, except perhaps the Purāṇas, which have clear sectarian divisions within them.

It should be understood that these texts are not merely secondary literature which can be ignored in favour of using the Vedas alone, but are essential for properly understanding the Vedas themselves. The Mahābhārata states in its first chapter:

“One should understand the Vedas using the Itihāsas and Purāṇas (for) the Vedas fear the one who has studied little, saying “this one shall misinterpret me”

A note on reconciliation

This topic is of course not anything new, and this debate has been going on for thousands of years. However, in most modern takes on this topic I have seen, I have noticed that, if they even invoke scriptures in the first place, they are largely dismissive or exclusionary rather than reconciliatory. They will list the pramāṇas that seemingly support their position, and either omit pramāṇas that do not fit their framework, declare them to be interpolations or forgeries or invoke the aforementioned maxim to claim it is contradicting some śruti with thin backing. This is not such a paper. One of the axioms of Mīmāṃsā is samanvaya or reconciliation. Thus this paper will neither omit nor dismiss any pramāṇas used by the opponent, and expects the opponent to do the same. It attempts to reconcile the entire scriptural corpus.

2. Vedas

2.1. Karma-kāṇḍa

As explained earlier, the Vedas have two divisions, the Karma-kāṇḍa being ritualistic and eulogistic and hence doctrinally indecisive, and the Jñāna Kāṇḍa being doctrinal. Hence our focus will be on Jñāna-kāṇḍa. There is however a common misconception that Indra or Agni or any other such deities are “supreme” in the Vedas or that Viṣṇu is a

“minor deity”, merely because he has less hymns in the karma-kāṇḍa, is rejected on its very premise, as they are praised in the Vedas for their importance in ritual, and not for their actual superiority. However, even aside from that, Viṣṇu is no “Minor deity” in the karma-kāṇḍa merely because he has less hymns. It is the quality of the Hymns that matter, what they say, and not their quantity.

Saṃhitā

The Ṛgveda (10.113.2) clarifies that Viṣṇu is worshipped due to his own intrinsic nature, whereas Indra is worshipped circumstantially:

“That Viṣṇu is great by his own vigour and greatness, whereas Indra, lord of wealth, became deserving of honour only after slaying Vṛtra with the deities.”

“Agni is the lowest and Viṣṇu the highest amongst the deities, in between them are all other deities.”

Objection: “highest” here does not indicate superiority, but literal height.

Agni is closest to the sacrifice being performed, whereas Viṣṇu is the highest in heaven or farthest away.

Answer: not so, for Sāyaṇa states in his Bhāṣya on the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa that superiority is intended.

Sāyaṇa’s Bhāṣya on Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 1.1.1 states:

“Here superiority (uttamatva) is stated.”

Regardless, there is an even more explicit statement in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 14.1.1.5:

“Therefore it is said: Viṣṇu is the supreme (śreṣṭha) among the deities.”

Hence, even in the karma-kāṇḍa, though the number of hymns for Viṣṇu is less, those hymns and their Brāhmaṇas still clearly state his status as supreme, most ancient, self-born, creator, and possessing the highest position. They also clarify that his worship is intrinsic, whereas that of Indra is circumstantial.

2.2. Jñāna-kāṇḍa

We now come to the jñāna-kāṇḍa, the doctrinally decisive section of the Vedas. Firstly, the Nārāyaṇa Sūkta, part of the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka, clearly identifies the supreme Brahman with Nārāyaṇa, i.e., Viṣṇu:

“Nārāyaṇa is the supreme effulgence, Nārāyaṇa is the supreme self. Nārāyaṇa is the supreme Brahman, Nārāyaṇa is the supreme tattva. Nārāyaṇa is the supreme meditator and Nārāyaṇa is the supreme meditation.”

Show Sanskrit

नारायणः परो ज्योतिरात्मा नारायणः परः । नारायणः परं ब्रह्म तत्त्वं नारायणः परः ।
नारायणः परो ध्याता ध्यानं नारायणः परः ॥

Objection: Nārāyaṇa here does not mean Viṣṇu.

Answer: the etymology of “Nārāyaṇa” throughout scripture, and the closing verse of the Sūkta itself, identify Nārāyaṇa with Viṣṇu.

The etymology of the word “Nārāyaṇa” throughout scripture will be considered shortly. Here, however, such a discussion is unnecessary, because the text itself identifies Nārāyaṇa with Viṣṇu in its final verse:

“OM. Nārāyaṇa we acknowledge, Vāsudeva we meditate upon, may that Viṣṇu propel us forward.”

Show Sanskrit

ॐ नारायणाय विद्महे वासुदेवाय धीमहि । तन्नो विष्णुः प्रचोदयात् ॥

Thus, Vāsudeva, Viṣṇu and Nārāyaṇa are synonyms for the same deity as per the Nārāyaṇa sūktam itself.

Though this passage is decisive against the Śaiva pakṣa, it is not so against the Smārtas, for they affirm Viṣṇu as supreme and Brahman alongside Śiva and others. The following Vedic passages establish not merely Viṣṇu’s superiority, but His exclusive superiority over Śiva, Brahmā, and the other deities. The Nārāyaṇa Upaniṣad, Khaṇḍa 1, states:

“Brahmā is born from Nārāyaṇa. Rudra is born from Nārāyaṇa. Indra is born from Nārāyaṇa. From Nārāyaṇa the Prajapatis are born. From Nārāyaṇa, the twelve Adityas, Rudras and all Vedas are born. They proceed also in Nārāyaṇa. They eventually dissolve in Nārāyaṇa.”

नारायणाद् ब्रह्मा जायते । नारायणाद् रुद्रो जायते । नारायणादिन्द्रो जायते । नारायणात् प्रजापतयः प्रजायन्ते । नारायणाद् द्वादशादित्या रुद्रा वसवः सर्वाणि च छन्दांसि । नारायणादेव समुत्पद्यन्ते । नारायणे प्रवर्तन्ते । नारायणे प्रलीयन्ते ॥

Thus Nārāyaṇa is characterised as the origin of Brahmā, Rudra, Indra, all deities, and the scriptures; He is also their basis and the one in whom they finally dissolve. The Smārta claim that Nārāyaṇa is supreme only in addition to Rudra and others therefore cannot be maintained, since He is explicitly their source. Similarly, the first verse of the Mahopaniṣad states:

“Indeed, Nārāyaṇa alone was (in the beginning), Not Brahmā, not Śiva, not water, not fire not soma, neither sky not earth, not the constellations, Sun nor moon.”

An almost identical verse is found in the Paiṅgi-rahasya Brāhmaṇa; the only difference is that it uses “Śaṅkara” instead of “Īśāna” for Śiva:

“Nārāyaṇa alone was (in the beginning), neither Brahmā nor Śiva.”

एको ह वै नारायण आसीन्न ब्रह्मा न च शङ्करः ॥

By all these verses, Viṣṇu is much prior to Śiva and other deities and is their creator, and therefore cannot be the same as them (Smārta view), neither inferior (Śaiva view). Also, the definition of Brahman as stated in Brahma Sūtra 1.1.2 is as the cause of the world, thus this also reinforces Viṣṇu’s exclusive status as Brahman.

Objection: the two Upaniṣads are not part of the eleven major Upaniṣads, and the Brāhmaṇa is no longer extant. May these verses be rejected as later forgeries or interpolations?

Answer: that cannot be so, for these verses are quoted by prominent ācāryas of both the Smārta and Śaiva traditions.

Among the Smārtas, Madhusūdana Sarasvatī, one of the most influential and revered Smārta ācāryas, cites the Nārāyaṇa Upaniṣad verse on page 8 of the cited edition, the Mahopaniṣad verse on pages 8 and 767, and the Paiṅgi-rahasya Brāhmaṇa verse on page 763.

Among the Śaivas, Śrīpati Paṇḍitācārya, a prominent Vīraśaiva ācārya and defender of Śaiva theology, quotes the Mahopaniṣad and Nārāyaṇa Upaniṣad verses on page 321 of his commentary on the Brahma Sūtras, the Śrīkara Bhāṣya. Although he presents them in the pūrvapakṣa and offers a Śaiva explanation, he does not deny their authenticity.

The traditional ācāryas therefore preferred samanvaya to dismissing difficult passages as interpolations. For that reason, subsequent objections based only on alleging forgery or interpolation are not repeated.

Once Viṣṇu’s identity as Brahman has been established by these pramāṇas, references to Brahman in the Upaniṣads may be read as referring to Viṣṇu. The author argues that this is not merely a Vaiṣṇava view: Śaṅkarācārya, commenting on Chāndogya Upaniṣad 8.12.3 and its “uttama puruṣa,” writes:

“Uttama-puruṣa—He is the one spoken of in the Gītā.”

Objection: there are many Gītās. It is not necessary that this refers to Kṛṣṇa’s Gītā.

Answer: not so. Kṛṣṇa’s Gītā is the Gītā commented upon by Śaṅkara, and Ānandagiri identifies the exact verse being referenced.

“Because I transcend the perishable and am higher even than the imperishable, I am celebrated in the world and in the Veda as Puruṣottama.” — Bhagavad Gītā 15.18

Show Sanskrit

यस्मात्क्षरमतीतोऽहमक्षरादपि चोत्तमः । अतोऽस्मि लोके वेदे च प्रथितः पुरुषोत्तमः ॥

Ānandagiri quotes this exact Gītā passage in his sub-commentary. Śaṅkara also glosses the inner ruler in Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 3.7.3 as “this Īśvara, known as Nārāyaṇa.” The author therefore concludes that Śaṅkara links the Upaniṣadic supreme being to Nārāyaṇa and to the speaker of the Bhagavad Gītā.

Puruṣa Sūktam

The most famous Vedic Sūkta is the Puruṣa Sūkta, for it describes not elemental deities but the supreme being from whom everything came to be. Its best-known recension has sixteen verses in the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā; the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka preserves a longer twenty-four-verse recension in the jñāna-kāṇḍa.

“The Puruṣa has a thousand faces, a thousand eyes and a thousand feet. He pervades the entire world, extending in the ten directions.”

Further:

“This much is his greatness, and even greater is he, that puruṣa. In one pada of his come all the world and its beings, and of three pādas the immortal in heaven.”

एतावानस्य महिमा अतो ज्यायाँश्च पूरुषः । पादोऽस्य विश्वा भूतानि त्रिपादस्यामृतं दिवि ॥

The sūkta then goes on to describe how everything came to be from the body of this primordial being, the Puruṣa. Even from these verses, available in the Ṛgveda version also, an argument for the Puruṣa being Viṣṇu may be made, due to the emphasis on him pervading the world (Viṣṇu literally means one who pervades), the reference to one pada of his containing the earth and three containing the heavens, alluding to Viṣṇu’s Trivikrama epithet referenced also in the Ṛgveda, etc. but if still there is any doubt it is cleared by the final verse of the Yajurveda version:

“Hrī and Lakṣmī are your wives, the day and night your two sides”

Show Sanskrit

ह्रीश्च ते लक्ष्मीश्च पत्न्यौ । अहोरात्रे पार्श्वे ॥

Thus this verse clears any debate entirely. The primordial Puruṣa, the source of everything, is none other than the husband of Lakṣmī, lord Viṣṇu. Hence, in the Jñāna-kāṇḍa, which is the doctrinal foundation of Hinduism, Viṣṇu is stated to be the supreme Brahman, prior to and the creator of other deities including Śiva and Brahmā, identified with the supreme Puruṣa even as per the Smārta commentators.

3. Smṛtis

We come now to the Smṛtis, which not only further the conclusion derived from Śruti but also validate it, since Śruti is interpreted in light of Smṛti. Here “Smṛti” means the Dharmaśāstras, Vedāṅgas, and Itihāsas. The Purāṇas, though technically also Smṛti, are considered separately.

3.1. Dharmaśāstras and Vedāṅgas

In the first chapter of the Manusmṛti (1.10–11), the creation of the world is described, and the identity of its cause is stated to be Nārāyaṇa:

“Water is called nāra, water being the offspring of Nara. Since water was the original residence (ayana) of that being, he is therefore called Nārāyaṇa. That unmanifest and eternal cause, partaking of the nature of both the existent and non-existent, produced the being called Brahmā among people.”

Show Sanskrit

आपो नारा इति प्रोक्ता आपो वै नरसूनवः । ता यदस्यायनं पूर्वं तेन नारायणः स्मृतः ॥ १.१० ॥
यत्तत्कारणमव्यक्तं नित्यं सदसदात्मकम् । तद्विसृष्टः स पुरुषो लोके ब्रह्मेति कीर्त्यते ॥ १.११ ॥

The author identifies this Nārāyaṇa with Lord Viṣṇu and reads the passage as describing Him as the eternal cause and the creator of Brahmā.

Even in the Vedāṅgas, Viṣṇu is described as the original and supreme deity. For example, the Bṛhat Parāśara Horā Śāstra, attributed to Parāśara, father of Vyāsa, opens by answering how the universe is created and dissolved (1.9–11):

“Viṣṇu is the unmanifest one, the lord and deity, master of the world, of the nature of pure sattva, ultimately devoid of gunas yet endowed with them (as per his will), the creator of the universe, the husband of Lakṣmī, the cause. By a single fraction of his he creates and preserves the world as a līlā, his three pādas are the nectar of those who truly see.”

Show Sanskrit

कथं सृष्टिरियं जाता जगतश्च लयः कथम् ।
एकोऽव्यक्तात्मको विष्णुरनादिः प्रभुरीश्वरः । शुद्धसत्त्वो जगत्स्वामी निर्गुणस्त्रिगुणान्वितः ॥
संसारकारकः श्रीमान्निमित्तात्मा प्रतापवान् । एकांशेन जगत्सर्वं सृजत्यवति लीलया ॥
त्रिपादं तस्य देवस्य ह्यमृतं तत्त्वदर्शिनः ॥

Śiva and Brahmā are described separately in the subsequent verses (1.20), without such absolute language.

“Endowed with Bhūśakti, Brahmā creates, endowed with Nīlaśakti, Śiva destroys.”

Show Sanskrit

भूशक्त्या सृजते ब्रह्मा नीलशक्त्या शिवोऽत्ति हि ॥

Thus, even in the Dharmaśāstras and Vedāṅgas, which are focused on very different topics from doctrinal theology, Viṣṇu is still consistently upheld as the supreme, while Brahmā and Śiva are mentioned as subservient.

3.2. Rāmāyaṇa

Near the very beginning of the Rāmāyaṇa, the supremacy of Viṣṇu is shown when all the deities, including Śiva, approach Him in prayer (1.15.32):

“Then all the Devas Ṛṣis and gandharvas, including Śiva, along with all the apsaras and respective followers, began to worship Viṣṇu with divine eulogies.”

Show Sanskrit

तदा देवर्षिगन्धर्वाः सरुद्राः साप्सरोगणाः । स्तुतिभिर्दिव्यरूपाभिस्तुष्टुवुर्मधुसूदनम् ॥

Another instance occurs near the end of the first kāṇḍa, where Paraśurāma—himself a great devotee of Śiva—narrates how the deities once disputed whether Śiva or Viṣṇu was supreme (1.74.14–16):

“then all the deities began to ask Brahmā to test the relative superiority of the blue throated Śiva and Viṣṇu. Having known this purpose of the deities, he created a dispute between them. From the dispute a great battle commenced between the blue throated Śiva and Viṣṇu, each desirous of victory over the other.”

Show Sanskrit

तदा तु देवताः सर्वाः पृच्छन्ति स्म पितामहम् । शितिकण्ठस्य विष्णोश्च बलाबलनिरीक्षया ॥
अभिप्रायं तु विज्ञाय देवतानां पितामहः । विरोधं जनयामास तयोः सत्यवतां वरः ॥
विरोधे तु महद्युद्धमभवद्रोमहर्षणम् । शितिकण्ठस्य विष्णोश्च परस्परजयैषिणोः ॥

“then, Śiva’s bow, of terrifying exploits, was broken by the mere roaring of Viṣṇu, and the three-eyed Mahādeva himself was stunned. Then all the deities along with the Ṛṣis and cāraṇas came and beseeched both of them for peace, whereby they both, best of the deities, left. Having seen the bow of Śiva broken by the valour of Viṣṇu, the deities along with all the Ṛṣis accepted Viṣṇu as superior.”

Show Sanskrit

तदा तु जृम्भितं शैवं धनुरभीमपराक्रमम् । हुंकारेण महादेवः स्तम्भितोऽथ त्रिलोचनः ॥
देवैस्तदा समागम्य सर्षिसङ्घः सचारणैः । याचितौ प्रशमं तत्र जग्मतुस्तौ सुरोत्तमौ ॥
जृम्भितं तद्धनुर्दृष्ट्वा शैवं विष्णुपराक्रमैः । अधिकं मेनिरे विष्णुं देवाः सर्षिगणास्तदा ॥

Thus, the deities and Ṛṣis themselves concluded regarding their doubt on the superiority of the two deities, that it is Viṣṇu who is supreme. The commentaries on this passage should also be seen:

Commentary by Maheśvara Tīrtha (Tattvadīpikā)

“Seeing the bow and Śiva himself made inert by the valour of Viṣṇu, they accepted the all-pervading Viṣṇu as superior to Śiva in both power and knowledge—that is, as sarvottama, supreme among all.”

A more detailed commentary, addressing an objection of the Śaivas and Smārtas, is by Govindarāja:

Commentary by Govindarāja (Bhūṣaṇa)

“If it be said that the acceptance of Viṣṇu as superior arose from bias or ignorance, that is not so, because the text says ‘along with the ṛṣis.’ The ṛṣis perceive what lies beyond the senses; their conclusion can therefore be neither biased nor erroneous.”

It is not actually the narrative itself which is the most important point of this passage, but the fact that the Ṛṣis, who can never err nor be biased, concluded based on this that Viṣṇu is superior.

“By this it should be understood that śruti passages such as ‘Rudra is one; there is no second to him’ are to be read like ‘O Indra, there is none superior to you’: they state superiority over others within the deity’s own domain. By contrast, the śruti ‘Nārāyaṇa alone existed’ establishes the supreme reality as Viṣṇu, another name of Nārāyaṇa.”

Show Sanskrit

एतेन “एक एव रुद्रो न द्वितीयाय तस्थे” इत्यादिश्रुतिः “नकिरिन्द्र त्वदुत्तरः” इत्यादिवदितरापेक्षया श्रेष्ठत्वाभिप्राया । “एको ह वै नारायण आसीत्” इत्यादिश्रुत्युक्तो नारायणापरपर्यायो विष्णुरेव परं तत्त्वमित्यभिहितम् ॥

The Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad passage is examined more fully in the objections section. Here Govindarāja explains that praises of Śiva and Indra express relative superiority within their respective domains, whereas the Mahopaniṣad establishes Viṣṇu’s absolute superiority by excluding Śiva and the other deities. The Rāmāyaṇa then confirms Viṣṇu’s superiority over Śiva.

The Rāmāyaṇa further states Rāma’s (i.e., Viṣṇu’s) superiority in terms of power, declaring in 5.51.45:

“Neither the four-faced self-born Brahmā, nor the three-eyed Rudra, the killer of the demon tripura, nor Indra, the great king of the deities, can save the one who Rāma wishes to slay in battle”

Show Sanskrit

ब्रह्मा स्वयंभूश्चतुराननो वा रुद्रस्त्रिणेत्रस्त्रिपुरान्तको वा । इन्द्रो महेन्द्रः सुरनायको वा त्रातुं न शक्तो युधि रामवध्यम् ॥

Near the end of the Rāmāyaṇa, when Rāma slays Rāvaṇa, all the deities approach him in prayer again and eulogise him as Viṣṇu, where he is specifically stated to be Brahman (6.105.12-14):

“You are the glorious deity Nārāyaṇa, the husband of Lakṣmī, having the Cakra as your weapon. You are the one-tusked Varāha, victor over your past, present and future foes. You are the imperishable Brahman, the truly real. The greatest dharma in all the realms, you are the four-armed Viśvaksena, wielder of the bow, master of your senses, you are Puruṣa and Puruṣottama.”

Show Sanskrit

भवान्नारायणो देवः श्रीमांश्चक्रायुधो विभुः । एकशृङ्गो वराहस्त्वं भूतभव्यसपत्नजित् ॥
अक्षरं ब्रह्म सत्यं च मध्ये चान्ते च राघव । लोकानां त्वं परो धर्मो विष्वक्सेनश्चतुर्भुजः ॥
शार्ङ्गधन्वा हृषीकेशः पुरुषः पुरुषोत्तमः ॥

Thus, the Rāmāyaṇa consistently describes Viṣṇu as being the supreme deity worshipped by the other deities, being recognised as superior to Śiva by the Ṛṣis and deities, more powerful than Śiva, Brahmā and Indra, and directly identified as the supreme Brahman.

3.3. Mahābhārata

One does not need to look very far in the Mahābhārata to find the praise of Viṣṇu, in fact the very first word of the entire hundred thousand verse epic is “Nārāyaṇa”, the first one saluted in the opening benedictions. In that same opening chapter (1.1.22-24) he is praised further, and identified as Brahman:

“Having saluted Sri Hari Viṣṇu, the primal puruṣa, the deity, the one worshipped and offered to by men, the one true imperishable eternal Brahman, who is both manifest and unmanifest, asat and sat, yet also beyond sat and asat, who is the whole world, creator of all, the ancient, the supreme unending one, the auspicious and the cause of auspiciousness, the worshippable, the flawless, the pure, the master of his senses, the master of the mobile and immobile beings, I shall narrate the entire view of the great Ṛṣi, the venerable high-souled Vyāsa, of unmatched brilliance.”

Show Sanskrit

आद्यं पुरुषमीशानं पुरुहूतं पुरुष्टुतम् । ऋतमेकाक्षरं ब्रह्म व्यक्ताव्यक्तं सनातनम् ॥
असच्च सच्चैव च यद्विश्वं सदसतः परम् । परावराणां स्रष्टारं पुराणं परमव्ययम् ॥
मङ्गल्यं मङ्गलं विष्णुं वरेण्यमनघं शुचिम् । नमस्कृत्य हृषीकेशं चराचरगुरुं हरिम् ॥

Conversation between Brahmā and Rudra

It is generally understood that of the 3 deities of the Trimurti, it is either Śiva or Viṣṇu or both who are supreme, and Brahmā is lower. However, in the Mahābhārata, Brahmā is actually superior to Śiva and is stated to be his father, Mahābhārata 12.338.11-12

“Earlier, while the intelligent four-headed Brahmā was seated on the mountain, his forehead-born son—the three-eyed Śiva, lord of yogins—came from heaven before him and lovingly saluted his feet.”

Show Sanskrit

अथ तत्रासतस्तस्य चतुर्वक्त्रस्य धीमतः । ललाटप्रभवः पुत्रः शिव आगाद्यदृच्छया ॥

What follows is a conversation between the two, where Śiva enquires Brahmā regarding who the supreme being is (12.338.22-25)

“There are many beings, O Brahmā, which are created by you, who are self born. But there is also the supreme Puruṣa. Who is that one, that even you meditate upon, that purusottama? This is my doubt, answer me this, for it is my great curiosity.”

Show Sanskrit

रुद्र उवाच । बहवः पुरुषा ब्रह्मंस्त्वया सृष्टाः स्वयम्भुवा । सृज्यन्ते चापरे ब्रह्मन् स चैकः पुरुषो विराट् ॥
को ह्यसौ चिन्त्यते ब्रह्मंस्त्वया वै पुरुषोत्तमः । एतन्मे संशयं ब्रूहि महत्कौतूहलं हि मे ॥

Brahmā answered

“There are many beings, son, as you have said. But there is something beyond this and it is not limited to this, thus should be understood. I shall narrate unto you the basis of that singular Puruṣa, who is said to be the origin of the many beings.”

Thus it leads into the next chapter:

“Listen, son, of this eternal and undecaying Puruṣa. He is imperishable, beyond the measure of the senses, and all-pervading. Neither you, nor I, nor others can see Him, O best of the righteous. He is both saguṇa and nirguṇa, the universe itself, and is perceived through knowledge. Bodiless, He dwells in every body, yet is never tainted by karma. He is My inner self, yours, and that of all beings; He is the witness in all and cannot be grasped by anyone.” The chapter continues to describe this Puruṣa and finally identifies Him: “That Puruṣa is to be known as Nārāyaṇa, the self of all, who remains untouched by fruits as a lotus leaf is untouched by water.”

Show Sanskrit

शृणु पुत्र यथा ह्येष पुरुषः शाश्वतोऽव्ययः । अक्षयश्चाप्रमेयश्च सर्वगश्च निरुच्यते ॥
न स शक्यस्त्वया द्रष्टुं मयान्यैर्वापि सत्तम । सगुणो निर्गुणो विश्वो ज्ञानदृश्यो ह्यसौ स्मृतः ॥
अशरीरः शरीरेषु सर्वेषु निवसत्यसौ । वसन्नपि शरीरेषु न स लिप्यति कर्मभिः ॥
स हि नारायणो ज्ञेयः सर्वात्मा पुरुषो हि सः । न लिप्यते फलैश्चापि पद्मपत्रमिवाम्भसा ॥

Thus, upon being enquired regarding the supreme being by Śiva, Brahmā says it is none other than Nārāyaṇa alone. Notably, the supreme Puruṣa described here cannot be only a nirguṇa being, as argue the Smārtas, for he is explicitly stated to be both Saguna and nirguṇa. His presence in the material world is compared to the lotus, which, though blooming in muddy water, is not tainted by it.

Viṣṇu’s supremacy stated in Śānti Parva

Another key passage occurs in the Śānti Parva, Mahābhārata 12.350.11–18:

“Salutations to that greatly glorious one, the supreme self of all embodied beings: Nārāyaṇa, the universe, nirguṇa yet the ground of the guṇas. By His grace Brahmā was born, and from His wrath Rudra; He is the source of every moving and unmoving being.” The passage continues: “From Him came sacrifice and the sacrificer, the supreme Puruṣa called Aniruddha, creator and destroyer of the worlds. At the close of Brahmā’s night a lotus arises from His grace, and Brahmā is born from it. From His anger a son named Rudra arises from the forehead for the work of destruction. Brahmā and Rudra, born respectively from grace and wrath, carry out creation and dissolution under Nārāyaṇa’s command and are merely His instruments.”

Show Sanskrit

नमोऽतियशसे तस्मै देहिनां परमात्मने । नारायणाय विश्वाय निर्गुणाय गुणात्मने ॥
यस्य प्रसादजो ब्रह्मा रुद्रश्च क्रोधसम्भवः । योऽसौ योनिर्हि सर्वस्य स्थावरस्य चरस्य च ॥
तदादेशितपन्थानौ सृष्टिसंहारकारकौ । निमित्तमात्रं तावत्र सर्वप्राणिवरप्रदौ ॥

Objection: Could the “Rudra” described here as subordinate be a different Rudra from the supreme Śiva?

The objection is that Brahmā’s son might be a different, inferior Rudra rather than the deity worshipped as supreme by Śaivas.

Show Sanskrit

कपर्दी जटिलो मुण्डः श्मशानगृहसेवकः । उग्रव्रतधरो रुद्रो योगी त्रिपुरदारुणः । दक्षक्रतुहरश्चैव भगनेत्रहरस्तथा ॥

Answer: not so. The same section identifies this Rudra by familiar deeds and epithets of Śiva.

The passage describes the ascetic, matted-haired Rudra who dwells in cremation grounds, slew Tripura, destroyed Dakṣa’s sacrifice, and tore out Bhaga’s eye. Unless these familiar deeds belong to a different figure, the subordinate Rudra here is the same Śiva worshipped by the Śaivas.

Kṛṣṇa’s statement outside the Gītā

Aside from the testimony of others to Viṣṇu’s supremacy, Kṛṣṇa Himself repeatedly asserts it throughout the Mahābhārata. The most famous example is the Bhagavad Gītā, but the source first cites a statement outside it:

“All the other deities seek refuge in Rudra; Rudra seeks refuge in Brahmā; Brahmā seeks refuge in Me. I, O king, seek refuge in no one. No one is My refuge, and I am the refuge of all.”

Show Sanskrit

रुद्रं समाश्रिता देवा रुद्रो ब्रह्माणमाश्रितः । ब्रह्मा मामाश्रितो राजन्नाहं कञ्चिदुपाश्रितः ॥
ममाश्रयो न कश्चित्तु सर्वेषामाश्रयो ह्यहम् ॥

The hierarchy that was implicit in the conversation between Śiva and Brahmā, viz other deities < Śiva < Brahmā < Viṣṇu, is here stated explicitly by Kṛṣṇa. This is another example of not just relative superiority, but absolute superiority, which the Smārtas cannot reinterpret by their framework of all deities being the same, because not only is Viṣṇu (as Kṛṣṇa) stated to be supreme, but Śiva and Brahmā are explicitly stated to be inferior and dependent on him.

The Bhagavad Gītā

We come now to the crest-jewel of the Mahābhārata, the Bhagavad Gītā, where Kṛṣṇa elaborately describes his own supremacy. Before analysing Kṛṣṇa/Viṣṇu’s supremacy in the Gītā, we must address a claim circulating on the internet, that it is in fact Śiva who is speaking through Kṛṣṇa in the Gītā. Though it is sometimes claimed that Abhinavagupta or some other Śaiva Ācārya has claimed this, they have not. This claim is entirely a modern imagination. The claim, lacking any support from scripture or even from the Śaiva Ācāryas, may be discarded there and then. Even still I shall highlight some points on how it is impossible.

1. As seen above, the Gītā is not the only place where Kṛṣṇa states His supremacy; He does so in the Śānti and Aśvamedhika Parvans as well. The Bhagavad Gītā is therefore not an exceptional case requiring the claim that another deity speaks through Him.

“Thus have I heard this wondrous and thrilling dialogue between Vāsudeva and the great-souled Pārtha. Through Vyāsa’s grace I heard this supreme and secret yoga directly from Kṛṣṇa, the Lord of Yoga, as He Himself declared it.”

Show Sanskrit

इत्यहं वासुदेवस्य पार्थस्य च महात्मनः । संवादमिममश्रौषमद्भुतं रोमहर्षणम् ॥
व्यासप्रसादाच्छ्रुतवानेतद्गुह्यमहं परम् । योगं योगेश्वरात्कृष्णात्साक्षात्कथयतः स्वयम् ॥

Sañjaya therefore identifies the speaker unmistakably as Kṛṣṇa, not Śiva. The source adds that Sañjaya’s vision was granted by Vyāsa and enabled him to see even the universal form; Kṛṣṇa also identifies Himself as Vāsudeva elsewhere in the Gītā.

Kṛṣṇa’s statements in the Gītā

The Gītā’s statements of Kṛṣṇa’s supremacy are familiar, but the source reviews them systematically: Kṛṣṇa identifies Himself as the origin of all, as unsurpassed, and presents worship of Him as superior to worship directed elsewhere.

“I am the origin of all; everything proceeds from Me. Knowing this, the wise worship Me with devotion.” — Bhagavad Gītā 10.8

4.1. Meta-analysis of the Purāṇas

The final category of scripture is the Purāṇas. Unlike the Vedas, Dharmaśāstras, Vedāṅgas, Rāmāyaṇa, and Mahābhārata, they have clear sectarian divisions. There are Śaiva Purāṇas such as the Liṅga, Skanda, and Śiva Purāṇas; Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas such as the Bhāgavata, Garuḍa, and Viṣṇu Purāṇas; and texts devoted to other deities, each praising its own subject as supreme. The source presents several Vaiṣṇava ways of reconciling this diversity.

I. Difference of scope

Each Purāṇa begins with sages approaching a wise narrator—such as Śuka, Parāśara, or Romaharṣaṇa—and asking questions. The Purāṇa is narrated as an answer. The source compares their opening questions:

Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas

(i) Garuḍa Purāṇa 1.1. The sages ask: “Who among the deities is the supreme Lord? Who alone is worshippable? Who is to be meditated upon? Who creates the world, who preserves it, and who destroys it?”

(ii) Padma Purāṇa 1.1. They ask how the primordial lotus arose, how Brahmā was born from it, and how he performed creation.

(iii) Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.1. The question concerns how the world arose and will arise again, in whom the moving and unmoving universe depends, from whom it came, and in whom and how it dissolves.

These questions are general: they concern the nature, creation, preservation, and dissolution of the world, and the identity of the supreme deity or being.

Śaiva Purāṇas

(i) Liṅga Purāṇa 1.1. The sages specifically request the divine Purāṇa “endowed with praise of the Śiva-liṅga.”

(ii) Skanda Purāṇa 1.1. The ṛṣis ask for the greatness of the trident-bearing Devadeva, including his meditation and worship.

(iii) Śiva Purāṇa 2.1. They request the auspicious forms and divine deeds of Śiva and Śivā (Pārvatī).

The opening questions of the Śaiva Purāṇas are specific: Śiva or the liṅga is expressly named. The source therefore argues that statements identifying Śiva with Brahman function as praise of the stated subject, while Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas answer general questions about Brahman and the origin of the world by identifying the supreme with Viṣṇu.

Thus identification of Brahman with Viṣṇu is primary and taken as a matter of fact, whereas for Śiva and others it is only for praise as subject matter. This is in fact stated in the Agni Purāṇa, which is neither Śaiva nor Vaiṣṇava (Chapter 379.9-12):

“Even the knowledge of Vedas is said to be ‘lower’ and the imperishable Brahman is ‘higher’ than it. This word ‘Brahman’ is principally used only for Bhagavan (the supreme lord), and for other deities as a courtesy or in worship.”

Thus, “Brahman” or “Bhagavān” are used primarily and actually for Viṣṇu, and for others as a courtesy. Accordingly, when a general question is asked about Brahman, the answer glorifies Viṣṇu. These terms may be used as courtesy or for worship for other deities, as when such questions about specific deities are asked in non-Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas. Thus the statements of Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas are treated as plain doctrinal facts, while those of others glorify the subject matter.

“Matsya, Kūrma, Liṅga, Śiva, Skanda, and Agni—these are known as tāmasa Purāṇas. Viṣṇu, Nārada, Bhāgavata, Garuḍa, Padma, and Varāha are known to be sāttvika and auspicious, O beautiful one. The Brahma Purāṇas are known as rājasa. The sāttvika grant mokṣa, whereas the rājasa are generally auspicious.”

“The one who is the supreme Puruṣa, the supreme soul, is Janārdana. From him came Brahmā, from him Rudra, and in that manner the entire world.”

Objection II: Many scriptural sages and venerable saints worshipped deities other than Viṣṇu. Were they all wrong?

Answer: no. The source does not reject the worship of other deities.

Kṛṣṇa Himself recommends honouring other deities. Their devotees may receive material benefits or attain their respective realms, and their worship ultimately reaches Viṣṇu as their antaryāmin, though without full knowledge of His position. The source nevertheless distinguishes temporary fruits from mokṣa.

“One is not liberated from bodily bondage until one has devotion to Me, Vāsudeva; embodied beings suffer while bound by desire until they serve Your lotus feet.”

Objection III: Why does Viṣṇu, in His avatāras, worship Śiva?

“If I did not worship boon-giving Lord Śiva, who is My own self as I indwell him, no one else would do so. Whatever example I establish, the world follows; therefore I worship him.”

Objection IV: What about Vedic texts such as the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad and Śrī Rudram, which praise Rudra?

“Rudra is one; there is no second like him.” — Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad 3.2
“Salutations to Sadāśiva, lord of the world, lord of all, the great deity.” — Śrī Rudram

Answer: the source presents two Vaiṣṇava approaches.

One approach treats such verses as statements of relative superiority within a deity’s domain. Another takes “Rudra” as a name of Viṣṇu, since Rudra appears in the Viṣṇu-sahasranāma and Viṣṇu is the antaryāmin of Rudra.

Śaṅkara explains the name Kathita: “He alone is proclaimed supreme by the Vedas; or, He is proclaimed by all the Vedas, and therefore is called Kathita.”

“There is a rejection of the textual order because of the specific purpose of Śruti and because the literal sequence would be absurd.” — Mīmāṃsā Sūtra 5.4.1

Objection VII: From an Advaitic perspective, all deities are the same being, so hierarchy does not matter.

Answer: the work does not dispute Pāramārthika oneness, but addresses hierarchy within Vyāvahārika reality.

Advaitins do not discard social, political, or familial hierarchies merely because of Pāramārthika oneness. These distinctions remain valid within Vyāvahārika reality. The source argues by analogy that a scripturally affirmed hierarchy among deities cannot be dismissed solely by appealing to Pāramārtha.

The paper is written substantially with the Smārta position in view. It extensively cites Śaṅkara rather than relying only on Vaiṣṇava ācāryas: Upaniṣadic passages he applies to Viṣṇu, his Gītā commentary treating worship of other deities as inferior, and his Viṣṇu-sahasranāma Bhāṣya describing Viṣṇu as the one praised by all the Vedas.

The source also notes Vaiṣṇava devotion among early Advaita teachers, including Sureśvara, Madhusūdana Sarasvatī, and Śrīdhara Svāmī, and concludes that both the scriptures accepted by Smārtas and Śaṅkara’s central commentaries support Viṣṇu’s superiority within the Vyāvahārika hierarchy.
