MMK 2.25

One who is and is not [a goer] also does not go in the three aspects of going. Therefore, going and a goer and also that which is gone over do not exist.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

tasmād gatiśca gantā ca gantavyaṃ ca na vidyate || 25 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Since the goer, the action of going, and the destination have all failed analysis, the text concludes that none is established in its own nature. Candrakīrti now brings in scriptural illustrations of non-going and dependent continuity.

Candrakīrti cites the *Akṣayamatinirdeśa*: “Venerable Śāriputra, ‘going down’ is a term for contraction, and ‘going up’ is a term for expansion. Where neither contraction nor expansion applies, that is the path of the noble ones. When no path is applied, non-coming and non-going are the noble ones’ way.” The quotation supports the preceding conclusion that going cannot be found as an independently real path.

He next explains continuity through the example of a seed and a sprout. If the seed itself passes into the sprout, the sprout would simply be the seed, which would entail permanence. If the sprout came from something wholly other, it would be causeless, like a rabbit’s horn. The Buddha therefore teaches that while the seed exists, the sprout is neither the seed nor wholly other than it: this is continuity without either annihilation or permanence. Likewise, an impression appears from a seal without the seal itself transferring into it; the impression is neither identical with the seal nor wholly unrelated to it. Formations are similarly neither eternal nor cut off.

A further illustration concerns a face seen in a mirror or a vessel of oil. A child sees the reflected face, becomes attached to it, and runs after the desired image. Yet the face does not transfer into the reflection, and the face is never found inside the image. In the same way, understand all phenomena through such examples: their appearances do not require a self-existent thing to travel from one place or state to another.

Candrakīrti closes with a long quotation from the *Samādhirājasūtra*. It says that all existence is dreamlike: no person is found who is born or dies. Beings and persons are not obtained by analysis; phenomena are like foam, a plantain trunk, an illusion, space, lightning, the moon reflected in water, or a mirage. No one dies in this world and travels as a self to another world. Yet actions do not simply disappear: what has been done gives its fruit, wholesome or unwholesome, amid saṃsāra. The teaching rejects both permanence and annihilation, and denies that a fixed self who acted must later experience the result as the very same or as a wholly different person. There is no transmigration or return, and phenomena are neither simply existent nor simply nonexistent. The three realms are dreamlike, unstable, impermanent, and illusory; nothing has truly arrived or come here, and continuities are empty and signless. The unborn, peaceful, signless state is the range of the Sugatas and the qualities of the Buddhas. Their powers, dhāraṇīs, knowledge, and strength, together with the highest means of attaining the five higher knowledges and displaying miraculous powers, are praised in the concluding verses.
