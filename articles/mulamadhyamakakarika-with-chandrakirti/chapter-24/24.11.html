MMK 24.11

vināśayati durdṛṣṭā śūnyatā mandamedhasam
sarpo yathā durgṛhīto vidyā vā duṣprasādhitā || 11 ||

Emptiness, when wrongly understood, destroys the person of dull understanding, like a snake wrongly seized or a spell wrongly practiced.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

vināśayati durdṛṣṭā śūnyatā mandamedhasam | sarpo yathā durgṛhīto vidyā vā duṣprasādhitā || 11 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Thus, one who explains emptiness without understanding the arrangement of the two truths destroys a person of this kind:

The convention of the world is produced by ignorance alone and is without own-nature. The yogin who understands its emptiness, whose characteristic is ultimate reality, does not fall into either extreme. How? Since previously no own-nature of an existent thing was apprehended, one does not afterwards fall into nonexistence by thinking, “What existed then does not exist now.” And, because the world’s convention, which has the form of a reflection, is not contradicted, one does not deny action, the result of action, Dharma, non-Dharma, and the rest. Nor does one superimpose upon the ultimate the nature of an existent thing. For one sees action and result and the rest only in things without own-nature, and does not see them in things that have own-nature.

But whoever, not seeing the division of the two truths in this way, sees the formations as empty, may, while seeing emptiness, imagine that the formations do not exist; or may imagine some emptiness to exist as a real thing, and may also imagine an own-nature of existent things to serve as its support. In either way, wrongly understood emptiness will certainly destroy that person. How so? If that person imagines, “All this is empty; everything does not exist,” then a wrong view follows. As it is said:

“This Dharma, wrongly understood, destroys the person who is not discerning, because he sinks into it, which is equal to the view of nonexistence.”

But if that person does not wish to deny everything, then the rejection of emptiness necessarily follows: “How can these things, even though perceived by the whole world of gods, demons, and humans, be empty?” Having rejected it in this way, thinking that the meaning of emptiness is not absence of own-nature, that person is certainly bound for the lower realms by an evil action that brings about the ruin of the true Dharma. As it is said in the noble Ratnāvalī:

“Another fool, through misunderstanding this, thinking himself wise, with his own nature destroyed by rejection, goes headfirst to Avīci.”

Thus emptiness, when grasped as nonexistence, destroys the one who grasps it. But if this person conceives emptiness as an existent thing, and the formations that are its support as existing, even then, because the person has gone astray from the path leading to nirvāṇa, he becomes bewildered by the teaching of emptiness. Thus emptiness, when grasped as an existent thing, also destroys the one who grasps it.

Indeed, something that is beneficial, when done otherwise, does not produce a result, like a seed sown out of season; but when sown at the proper time, it produces a great result. In the same way, a snake, when caught by means of jewels, spells, medicines, and the like, brings a great mass of wealth through taking its crest-jewel, and by that means provides a livelihood for snake-catchers; but when caught by disregarding the instructions, it destroys the one who grasps it. And just as a science, practiced according to its instruction, helps the practitioner, but, when practiced after departing from the instruction, destroys that very practitioner, so too emptiness, a great science, when practiced and grasped according to the instruction, by rejecting grasping at existence, nonexistence, and the rest, through the middle way, joins the one who grasps it to the rain-stream of the cloud of nirvāṇa without remainder, which has the single function of quenching the fire of suffering—birth, aging, death, and the rest. But when it is grasped after departing from the special instruction, it certainly destroys the one who grasps it by the reasoning stated above.
