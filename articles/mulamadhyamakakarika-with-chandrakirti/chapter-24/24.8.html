MMK 24.8

dve satye samupāśritya buddhānāṃ dharmadeśanā
lokasaṃvṛtisatyaṃ ca satyaṃ ca paramārthataḥ || 8 ||

The Buddhas’ teaching of the Dharma depends on two truths: the truth of worldly convention and the truth in the ultimate sense.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

dve satye samupāśritya buddhānāṃ dharmadeśanā | lokasaṃvṛtisatyaṃ ca satyaṃ ca paramārthataḥ || 8 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Who makes the reproach stated above against us? One who does not know the division of the two truths, as taught in the Blessed One’s teaching, without error, and who is devoted only to studying the text. Therefore the Teacher, out of compassion, in order to remove the other’s understanding of the meaning of the false teaching, first addresses the arrangement of the two truths taught in the Blessed One’s teaching:

The Blessed Buddhas’ teaching of the Dharma proceeds depending on two truths. What are the two truths? The truth of worldly convention and the truth in the ultimate sense.

There, because it is said, “The world is declared to have the aggregates as its self; the world is supported by them,” the person designated in dependence upon the five aggregates is called the world. “Convention” means covering all around. Ignorance is called convention because it covers all around the reality of every thing. Or convention means mutual arising, that is, depending upon one another. Or convention means agreement, sign, or worldly usage; this is characterized by designation and designated, cognition and knowable, and the like. Convention in the world is worldly convention.

Why is it distinguished as “worldly convention,” as though a non-worldly convention also existed? This is a statement of things as they stand; that question does not arise here. Or, those whose faculties are afflicted by eye-disease, jaundice, and the like, and whose vision is reversed, are “non-worlds”; their convention is non-worldly convention. For this reason it is distinguished as worldly convention. This has been explained at length in the Madhyamakāvatāra and should be understood from there.

What is true through worldly convention is the truth of worldly convention. This entire usage without remainder—designation and designated, cognition and knowable, and the like—is called the truth of worldly convention. For these usages are not possible in the ultimate sense. There, it is said:

“When what is to be spoken has ceased and the sphere of thought has ceased, reality is unborn and unceased, like nirvāṇa.”

Therefore, in the ultimate, how could speech proceed? How could cognition proceed? For the ultimate is not dependent on another, peaceful, to be experienced individually by the noble ones, and beyond all elaboration. It is not taught, nor is it cognized. It was said earlier: “Not dependent on another, peaceful, not elaborated by elaborations, free from conceptual construction, and not many in meaning: this is the characteristic of reality.”

It is the highest meaning; therefore it is the ultimate meaning. That alone is truth in the ultimate sense. The distinction between these two truths is to be understood at length from the Madhyamakāvatāra. Depending on these two truths, the Blessed Buddhas’ teaching of the Dharma proceeds.
