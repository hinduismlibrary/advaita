MMK 27.26

kathaṃ tāvad upādātur ekadeśo vinaṅkṣyate
na naṅkṣyate caikadeśa evaṃ caitan na yujyate || 26 ||

How indeed would one part of the appropriator perish, while one part does not perish? This is not reasonable.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

kathaṃ tāvad upādātur ekadeśo vinaṅkṣyate | na naṅkṣyate caikadeśa evaṃ caitan na yujyate || 26 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Now he states why it is not reasonable that one part perishes and one part remains:

“How indeed would one part of the appropriator perish, while one part does not perish? This is not reasonable.”

Here, when the destruction of one part and the continuance of one part are conceived, is the destruction or continuance conceived for one part of the appropriator, or for appropriation? If, first, destruction of one part and continuance of another part of the appropriator are conceived, that is not reasonable. Why? Because:

“How indeed would one part of the appropriator perish, while one part does not perish?”

There is no reasoning by which we could conceive the destruction of one part and non-destruction of another. For this very reason, the Teacher, not seeing such reasoning, says: “This is not reasonable.”

Alternatively, the appropriator is called the self. And that self, when sought among the aggregates in five ways, is not found. For one that is not found, how could one part perish and another part not perish? For this very reason he says: “This is not reasonable.”

Alternatively, if one part of the appropriator perished and another part did not perish, then the very same appropriator would be a god in one part and a human in another. This is not accepted. Therefore he says: “This is not reasonable.” Thus, in this way, it is not reasonable for the appropriator to be finite and infinite.
