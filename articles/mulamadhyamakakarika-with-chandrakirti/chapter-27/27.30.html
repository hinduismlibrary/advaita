MMK 27.30

sarvadṛṣṭiprahāṇāya yaḥ saddharmam adeśayat
anukampām upādāya taṃ namasyāmi gautamam || 30 ||

He who taught the true Dharma to abandon all views, out of compassion—I bow to him, Gautama.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

sarvadṛṣṭiprahāṇāya yaḥ saddharmam adeśayat | anukampām upādāya taṃ namasyāmi gautamam || 30 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

“Dharma” is so called because it supports those who have fallen into the grasping, attainment, and apprehension involved in abandoning saṃsāra and nirvāṇa. “True Dharma” is the Dharma of the noble ones who have done what was to be done. Or “true Dharma” is a beautiful Dharma, worthy of praise because it causes the exhaustion of all the sufferings of saṃsāra.

He who taught the true Dharma—

“Without cessation, without arising, without annihilation, not eternal; not one in meaning, not different in meaning, not coming, not going; the calming of proliferation, peace”—

under the name “dependent arising,” for the abandonment of all views, out of compassion for the world, relying solely on great compassion, with love for the people of the entire three worlds as for a single beloved son, not desiring gain, honor, or repayment, I bow to that unsurpassed, unequalled Teacher.

What is his name? Gautama: that is, one born from the lineage of the supreme seers.

As it is said in the Noble Rice-Stalk Sūtra by the noble Maitreya, the Great Bodhisattva:

“Whoever sees this dependent arising correctly, as it is, with right wisdom, always and completely, as not a living being, without a living being, correctly, without distortion, unborn, not become, unmade, unconditioned, unobstructed, unimpeded, peaceful, fearless, not to be taken away, imperishable, of the nature of non-appeasement—and sees it as unreal, trifling, empty, without essence, a disease, a boil, a thorn, a misfortune, impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self—does not turn back toward the former limit: ‘What indeed was I in the past? Or was I not in the past? Who was I in the past? How was I in the past?’

“Nor does he turn back toward the later limit: ‘What will I be in the future? Or will I not be in the future? Who will I be in the future? How will I be in the future?’

“Nor does he turn back toward the present: ‘What is this? How is this? Who are we? What will we become? From where has this sentient being come? Having passed away from here, where will it go?’

“Whatever views there may be in the world among certain ascetics and brahmins, connected with doctrines of self, doctrines of a living being, or connected with rites and auspicious observances—all of those, at that time, are abandoned by him, fully known, cut off at the root, like a palm tree cut off at the crown, become nonexistent, and by nature will not arise or cease in the future.”

Then the venerable Śāriputra, having rejoiced in and become glad at the speech of the Bodhisattva, the Great Being Maitreya, rose from his seat and departed. Those monks too departed.

Thus ends the twenty-seventh chapter, called “Examination of Views,” in the Madhyamaka Commentary Prasannapadā, composed by Ācārya Candrakīrti.

And here ends the Treatise on the Middle Way, skilled in explaining the definitive and interpretable meanings of all worldly and transcendent teaching, and bestowing the seat of awakening on hearers, solitary buddhas, and unsurpassed perfectly awakened buddhas.
