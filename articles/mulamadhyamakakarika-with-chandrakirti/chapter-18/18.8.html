MMK 18.8

sarvaṃ tathyaṃ na vā tathyaṃ tathyaṃ cātathyameva ca
naivātathyaṃ naiva tathyaṃ etadbuddhānuśāsanam || 8 ||

All is true, or not true; both true and not true; neither not-true nor true. This is the Buddhas’ instruction.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

sarvaṃ tathyaṃ na vā tathyaṃ tathyaṃ cātathyameva ca | naivātathyaṃ naiva tathyaṃ etadbuddhānuśāsanam || 8 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

An opponent says: “Although it is so, the reality-nature is unproduced and unceased, like nirvāṇa; and in it there is no activity of speech or mind. Nevertheless, if it is not taught, it cannot be known by people. Therefore, for the sake of entry into it, there must at some time be a sequence of teachings, in dependence on conventional truth. Let that be stated.” It is replied: “Here, the sequence of teaching for entry into the nectar of reality of the Blessed Buddhas is to be understood, namely this:”

“Whatever is dear to each person at first, let one do that for that person; for a vessel of the True Dharma is never in any way obstructed.”

And thus it was said by the Blessed One: “The world disputes with me; I do not dispute with the world. Whatever is accepted as existing in the world, that too is accepted as existing by me. Whatever is accepted as not existing in the world, that too is not accepted as existing by me.” And from the scripture:

“Just as a foreigner cannot be made to understand by another language, so the world cannot be made to understand without the worldly.”

From the very beginning, then, for the person to be trained whose desire had arisen through hearing the distinction of the nature and character of the objects established in his own tradition, that which has been imagined and apprehended as true by those blinded by the darkness of ignorance—aggregates, elements, sense-bases and the like—was described by the Blessed One as true, in dependence on their view, in order to produce reverence for himself in the world. “This Blessed One is omniscient and all-seeing; he knows the entire course of the world. He has taught without error the abiding, arising, and dissolution, and so forth, of the receptacle-world extending up to the summit of existence, from the circle of wind and so forth up to the element of space, and of the world of beings, with its very diverse divisions, causes, results, pleasures, and dangers.”

Thus, afterward, for the person to be trained in whom the thought “the Blessed One is omniscient” had arisen, that very same all was taught as not true. That which does not become otherwise is called true. But conditioned formations do become otherwise, because they perish in each moment. Therefore, because there is the existence of becoming otherwise, they are not true. The particle “or” is to be understood as having the meaning of “and,” combining the teachings: “Everything is true and not true.”

For some, all this was taught as both true and not true. With regard to ordinary people, all this is true; with regard to noble knowledge, all this is false, because by them it is not apprehended in that way.

For some who had long cultivated the seeing of reality, whose tree-roots of the coverings had not yet been entirely uprooted, it was taught: “Neither not-true nor true.” This too was taught for the abandonment of even that small remainder of covering, like the denial of both whiteness and darkness in the son of a barren woman.

And this is the instruction of the Buddhas, the Blessed Ones. Instruction is leading away from a wrong path and establishing on the right path. Teaching in this sequence is “instruction”; or teaching in accordance with the capacity of the person to be trained is “instruction.”

All these teachings of the Buddhas, the Blessed Ones, endowed with great compassion and knowledge of means, are established as means of entry into the nectar of reality. For the Tathāgatas do not utter a statement that is not a means for entry into the nectar of reality. Like the administration of medicine in accordance with a disease, they teach the Dharma as appropriate, out of their wish to care for those to be trained. As it is said in the Śataka:

“It is said: ‘existent, nonexistent, both existent and nonexistent, and neither.’ Indeed, depending on the disease, a medicine called wholesome comes about.” || 8 ||
