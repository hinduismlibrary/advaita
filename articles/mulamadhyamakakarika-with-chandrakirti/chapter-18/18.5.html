MMK 18.5

karmakleśakṣayānmokṣaḥ karmakleśā vikalpataḥ
te prapañcātprapañcastu śūnyatāyāṃ nirudhyate || 5 ||

Liberation comes from the cessation of action and afflictions. Action and afflictions are from conceptualization; they are from proliferation, but proliferation ceases in emptiness.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

karmakleśakṣayānmokṣaḥ karmakleśā vikalpataḥ | te prapañcātprapañcastu śūnyatāyāṃ nirudhyate || 5 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Since this sequence for the cessation of birth has been established in this way, therefore:

“Liberation comes from the cessation of action and afflictions.”

It is established that liberation comes from the cessation of action and afflictions. For when appropriation has ceased, becoming dependent on it does not occur. When becoming has ceased, how could there be the arising of birth, aging, death, and the rest? In this way it is established that liberation comes from the cessation of action and afflictions.

Then it must be stated: through the cessation of which do action and afflictions cease completely? The response:

“Action and afflictions are from conceptualization; they are from proliferation, but proliferation ceases in emptiness.”

For the affliction of passion and the rest arises in the foolish ordinary person who wrongly conceptualizes form and the rest. For he will say:

“Passion, hatred, and confusion are said to arise from conception; for they arise in dependence on wholesome and unwholesome inversions.”

And it is said in a sūtra:

“Desire, I know your root: surely you are born from conception. I shall not conceive you; therefore you will not exist for me.”

Thus action and afflictions proceed from conceptualization. And those conceptualizations arise from the diverse proliferation cultivated in beginningless saṃsāra, characterized by knowledge and knowable, expression and expressed, agent, action, instrument and activity, pot, cloth, crown, chariot, form, feeling, woman, man, gain, loss, happiness, suffering, fame, disgrace, blame, praise, and the rest. And this worldly proliferation ceases when there is the seeing of emptiness in which all own-natures are entirely empty.

How so? When there is the apprehension of an entity, the net of proliferation stated above would arise. For those attached to a daughter of a barren woman do not bring about proliferation concerning her, apprehending her as possessed of form, beauty, and youth. And without bringing about proliferation, they do not bring about wrong conception concerning that object. And without bringing about the net of conception, through holding to “I” and “mine,” they do not produce the multitude of afflictions rooted in the view of the existent group. And without producing the multitude of afflictions having the view of the existent group as their nature, they do not perform wholesome, unwholesome, and imperturbable actions. And without performing actions, they do not experience the wilderness of saṃsāra, all woven together as birth, aging, death, grief, lamentation, suffering, unhappiness, distress, and the rest.

In the same way, in the state of seeing emptiness, yogis do not apprehend all the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields in their own nature. And not apprehending the own-nature of entities, they do not bring about proliferation concerning them. And without bringing about that proliferation, they do not bring about conception concerning it. And without bringing about conception, through holding to “I” and “mine,” they do not produce the multitude of afflictions rooted in the view of the existent group. And without producing the multitude of afflictions beginning with the view of the existent group, they do not perform actions. And without performing actions, they do not experience saṃsāra called birth, aging, and death.

Thus, having reached emptiness, whose characteristic is the auspicious cessation of all proliferation, because the entire net of conceptual proliferation ceases, through the cessation of proliferation conceptualization ceases; through the cessation of conceptualization all action and affliction cease; through the cessation of action and affliction birth ceases. Therefore emptiness itself is called nirvāṇa, because it is characterized by the cessation of all proliferation. As stated in the Śataka:

“In brief, the Tathāgatas describe Dharma as non-violence. Emptiness alone is nirvāṇa; only these two are here.”

But the Teacher Bhāvaviveka, not accepting that hearers and solitary buddhas attain the emptiness stated above, describes it thus: “Even for a noble hearer, observing only a heap of formations that have arisen and perish at every moment, which are not self and not belonging to self, because entities of self and what belongs to self do not exist, the view arises: ‘Only dharmas arise and perish.’ For self is the object of making ‘I’; through its absence that too is absent. And just because it is absent, there is no internal or external thing anywhere; since making ‘mine’ does not arise, the determination of its own-nature ‘I am without mine, without I’ arises, apart from conventional designation. But this is before the great bodhisattvas who see all formations as unborn and dwell in the conduct of non-conceptual wisdom.” Therefore he says:

“That one who is without mine and without I does not exist either.”

This Teacher does not follow the opinion of the Master in a matter of this kind, as explained in the Madhyamakāvatāra at “But in the Far-Going, [the one] superior in wisdom.” Therefore no effort is undertaken again to refute that. For this very reason it is said by the Blessed One in the noble Aṣṭasāhasrikā:

“Subhūti, whoever wishes to awaken to the awakening of a hearer should train in this very perfection of wisdom. Subhūti, whoever wishes to awaken to the awakening of a solitary buddha should train in this very perfection of wisdom. Subhūti, the bodhisattva, the great being, who wishes to awaken to unsurpassed, complete awakening, should train in this very perfection of wisdom,” and so on.

And it is said:

“Whoever wishes to become a hearer of the Sugata, or to become a solitary buddha, or likewise a Dharma King, without coming to this patience cannot reach it, like one who sees the shore but has not crossed over.”
