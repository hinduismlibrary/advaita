MMK 11.1

pūrvā prajñāyate koṭirnetyuvāca mahāmuniḥ
saṃsāro 'navarāgro hi nāsyādirnāpi paścimam || 1 ||

“The first limit is not discerned,” said the great sage. For saṃsāra is without first or last; it has neither beginning nor end.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

pūrvā prajñāyate koṭirnetyuvāca mahāmuniḥ | saṃsāro 'navarāgro hi nāsyādirnāpi paścimam || 1 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Here it is said: The self indeed exists, because saṃsāra exists. For if the self were not, who would transmigrate through the five destinations by the successive continuity of birth and death, going on and on? For the Blessed One said: “Monks, the saṃsāra of birth, aging, and death has no first or last limit”; “For beings obscured by ignorance, bound by craving, tied by craving’s knot, transmigrating and running on, the first limit is not discerned.” And when saṃsāra exists because of the Blessed One’s teaching, then the transmigrator too exists. And that is called the self. The response: The self would exist if saṃsāra itself existed. How? Because of this:

“The first limit is not discerned,” the great sage said. “For saṃsāra is without first or last; it has neither beginning nor end.” “Limit” has “part” and “place” as synonyms. “The first limit” means the former place. For if something called saṃsāra existed, surely it would have a former and a latter, just like pots and the like. And the Blessed One said: “For, monks, the saṃsāra of birth, aging, and death has no first or last limit.” Since, by the statement that saṃsāra has no first or last limit, there are no first and last limits of saṃsāra, did the Blessed One not clearly declare: “Saṃsāra itself does not exist”? Therefore saṃsāra does not exist, because its first and last limits are not perceived, like a whirling firebrand. Thus it is established.

Here this is considered: If the former and latter of saṃsāra were denied by the Blessed One, why then did he say, “Therefore, monks, you should train thus: ‘We shall undertake the practice for the ending of saṃsāra’”? The response: Because of the taking up of such qualifications as “beings obscured by ignorance,” it is understood that this saṃsāra without first and last is theirs alone, but not that of those whose trees of ignorance and obscurations have been utterly uprooted by the force of the wind of knowledge of reality. But for those whose entire trees, with the roots of afflictions and latent impressions, have been burned by the fire of knowledge of the supramundane path, an end indeed exists. It is to be understood thus.

The question is: How is there instruction about an end for those without a beginning? This is seen among external things, such as rice and the like: although there is no beginning, an end exists through contact with burning and the like. As Āryadeva’s venerable one said:

“Just as an end of a seed is seen, but its beginning is not found, so also, because of the deficiency of causes, the arising of birth is not possible.”

And that instruction about an end is taught while abiding in worldly convention alone, in dependence on worldly knowledge, for the sake of encouraging beings bound in the prison of saṃsāra. But when reality is examined, saṃsāra itself does not exist; from where, then, its destruction? Like the destruction of the snake-delusion in a rope when a lamp is present.

Here it is said: If so, in dependence on worldly knowledge, why is a beginning not also spoken of as having an end? The response: Because the fault of being causeless would follow, even in dependence on worldly knowledge the beginning of saṃsāra is absent. Thus, in both ways, the absence of a beginning alone is to be understood.
