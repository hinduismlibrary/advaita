MMK 15.11

"Since that which exists by its essence is not non-existent," is [the view of] permanence. "That which arose before is now non-existent,"leads to [the view of] annihilation.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

asti yad dhi svabhāvena na tan nāstīti śāśvatam | nāstīdānīm abhūt pūrvam ity ucchedaḥ prasajyate || 11 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

If something exists by its own nature, it cannot later become non-existent, because an intrinsic nature would not disappear; that is the eternalist view. If something accepted as truly existent before is then said to be non-existent now, that is annihilation. Since no such intrinsic nature is established, neither extreme follows.

The opponent worries that denying own-nature must itself be annihilation. Candrakīrti distinguishes denying a thing once accepted as real from failing to find an intrinsically real thing at all. The former is nihilism; the latter is like a clear-sighted person who does not see the hairs imagined by someone with an eye disorder. The teaching “things do not exist” is directed at those who falsely grasp them as inherently existent, not at denying conventional cause and result.

Candrakīrti adds that the middle way alone avoids both extremes. To accept a dependent mental process while denying only imagined nature still leaves a real substrate and does not fully escape existence and non-existence. The Buddha’s teaching of emptiness is the definitive instruction; accounts that posit an ultimately real mind or other basis remain provisional. The aim is to stop fixation on all four claims—own-nature, other-nature, existence, and non-existence—so that beings can realize the peace beyond views.
