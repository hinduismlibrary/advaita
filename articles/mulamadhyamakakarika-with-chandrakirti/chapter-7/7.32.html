MMK 7.32

na svātmanā nirodho 'sti nirodho na parātmanā
utpādasya yathotpādo nātmanā na parātmanā || 32 ||

Cessation does not exist by itself, nor does cessation exist by another. As with arising, arising is not by itself nor by another.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

na svātmanā nirodho 'sti nirodho na parātmanā | utpādasya yathotpādo nātmanā na parātmanā || 32 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Further, if cessation is conceived as a destroyer of entities, is another cessation of it accepted, or not? If accepted, that is not reasonable:

“Cessation does not exist by itself, nor does cessation exist by another. As with arising, arising is not by itself nor by another.”

How it does not exist, he explains:

“As with arising, arising is not by itself nor by another.”

Just as it was said, beginning with “How would this arising, unborn, produce itself?,” that arising does not produce itself, so too cessation does not destroy itself. How? Reply:

“How would this unceased cessation destroy itself? If it is destroyed, what is destroyed again when it is destroyed?”

This is to be stated in the same way as with arising. Thus cessation does not exist by itself.

Now cessation does not exist by another either. How? Just as it was said regarding arising, “If another arising produces it,” and so on, it should also be said regarding cessation:

“If another destroys it, destruction would be endless. If it is destruction without destruction, then for you everything would be destroyed in that way.”

Thus cessation too is not possible by another; there is no cessation of cessation.

Someone might think: “There is no cessation of cessation.” This is unreasonable. If there were no cessation of cessation, then because it lacks cessation, it would be deprived of the characteristic of the conditioned. Thus, if destruction of destruction is conceived, destruction is not reasonable; if it is not conceived, still it is not reasonable. How, then, will destruction fit the opponent’s position?

Someone might say: “Even so, when this same analysis is applied, destruction is not reasonable for you either. Since the fault is common to both, one should not raise an objection against just one of them.” Reply: This objection does not apply to me. Why? Entities that are without own-nature by themselves, while remaining without own-nature, enter the course of convention for fools attached to ‘this is real,’ by the very reasoning established without analysis. In these, we do not apply the analysis stated above. Like an illusion, a dream, and a gandharva city, worldly entities, though without proof, have become established for all people whose eye of understanding is obscured by the darkness of ignorance. Fools accept them as established only through mutual dependence. As has been said in the Śataka:

“Existence is like a circle of fire, a conjuration, a dream, an illusion, the moon reflected in water, smoke, an echo, a mirage, and a cloud.”

Therefore, when arising is accepted, what is to be produced is accepted; when what is to be produced is accepted, arising is accepted. When cessation is accepted, what is to be ceased is accepted; when what is to be ceased is accepted, cessation is accepted. Since worldly convention is accepted in this way, how could the same consequence apply to our position?

But for one who accepts that destruction is without a cause and declares conditioned things to be momentary, because destruction would be without cause and would not exist, like a sky-flower, how will the momentariness of entities be established? How will conditionedness be established for things without destruction? Everything becomes incoherent for him. The Blessed One, explaining that aging and death are conditioned by birth and that the characteristics of the conditioned are included among formations, has clearly shown that destruction has a cause. And because this depends only on birth, momentary destruction is easy to establish; everything is coherent.

Someone might say: “Destruction is nonexistence. What is to be done with a cause for what is non-existent? Therefore destruction is without a cause.” But then the consequence of having no cause also follows for an entity. An entity is what exists; what exists, what use has it for a cause? What is already born is not born again. Therefore, because the consequence of lacking a cause follows everywhere, this is unreasonable.

Further, just as arising is accepted as having a cause, because there is nonexistence before and existence afterwards, so too let destruction be accepted as having a cause. For destruction does not always occur: before arising it is absent, and afterwards it exists. What is said—“What is nonexistence to be done with a cause?”—is unreasonable. We do not desire that something be done by the cause of destruction; rather, we explain that destruction itself is done. Someone might say: “In that case, since it is being done, destruction too would become an entity.” We accept this. For destruction is an entity with respect to its own nature, but it is not an entity because it has the nature of the cessation of qualities such as form. Further, death also brings about two effects: the destruction of formations and the non-destruction of not knowing, according to scripture. How, then, is destruction not caused?

Further, if an opponent accepts emptiness, whose characteristic is a conceived non-entity, as having the form of an entity, how has it not been accepted that a non-entity is an entity? And because it is an entity, how could emptiness be unconditioned? Therefore, everything accepted by you is abandoned. For this very reason he will say:

“How could nirvāṇa be both non-entity and entity? Nirvāṇa is not conditioned, while entity and non-entity are conditioned.”

Enough of this digression. We shall explain what is presently in hand.
