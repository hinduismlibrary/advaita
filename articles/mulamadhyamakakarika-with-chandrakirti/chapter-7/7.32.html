MMK 7.32

Cessation does not exist by its own self, nor does cessation [exist] by something else. This is like how what has been born is neither given birth to by itself nor another [cf. 25]

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

na svātmanā nirodho 'sti nirodho na parātmanā | utpādasya yathotpādo nātmanā na parātmanā || 32 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

If cessation is said to destroy things, does that cessation itself undergo another cessation? It cannot cease by itself: before it has ceased, how could it destroy itself; once it has ceased, what remains to be destroyed? Nor can another cessation destroy it without creating the same regress. The same reasoning used against arising applies here.

The opponent objects that if cessation itself does not cease, it lacks one of the marks of a conditioned thing. Candrakīrti answers that the objection confuses ultimate analysis with conventional usage. Things empty of own-nature nevertheless enter ordinary dealings through mutual dependence and common agreement, without withstanding ultimate analysis. They are like a whirling firebrand, an illusion, a dream, a magical display, the moon reflected in water, smoke, an echo, a mirage or a cloud. We conventionally speak of something produced when there is a thing produced, and of cessation when there is something that ceases; no infinite regress follows from these mutually dependent descriptions.

The opponent claims that destruction is causeless because it is a nonexistence and asks what cause nonexistence could need. But the same argument would remove causes from existing things: if something already exists, what need has it of a cause? Candrakīrti says that cessation, like arising, is not present at every time; it is spoken of in dependence on conditions and on the prior presence of the thing that ceases. The cause does not have to make an independently existing “destruction”; rather, cessation is conventionally produced as the ending of the thing’s form and functions. The Buddha’s teaching that aging and death depend on birth also presents cessation as conditioned. To treat emptiness as a real entity while denying that absence can be spoken of as an entity is inconsistent. Conventional arising and cessation are accepted as dependent designations; the ultimate analysis finds no self-existing thing in them.
