1. Intro

A recent paper, “Ancient DNA indicates 3,000 years of genetic continuity in the Northern Iranian Plateau, from the Copper Age to the Sassanid Empire,” now allows us to shed light on the genetic history of Iran. Kurganists like to claim that the Iranic languages entered Iran due to an inflow of peoples from the Steppe, but this recent paper seems to undermine all of the evidence. Along with this paper, we will look at the genetics of priestly Parsis, who have essentially been stuck in time, genetically speaking, since they were kicked out of Iran during the Islamic invasions and have not mixed with the native Indian population in any meaningful sense.

2. On the 2025 paper

The historical samples date from around 355 BCE to 460 CE, covering the Achaemenid–Seleucid, Parthian, and Sassanid periods. There are 13 male or probably male individuals. Seven gave usable Y-DNA results—six independent paternal lines, since two were identical twins—and not one was R1a. The other six are unknown, so the exact result is 0 R1a out of the seven typed males, not 0 out of all 13.

The autosomal evidence is just as damaging to the Steppe argument. Seven historical individuals had enough genome-wide DNA for analysis. The paper does not model Sintashta directly, so it cannot give a literal Sintashta percentage. However, when the authors tested Bronze Age Steppe affinity using Samara Yamnaya, they concluded that the apparent affinity was due to shared CHG ancestry, not a separate Steppe admixture signal.

In their own words, the Bronze Age Steppe affinity was “only apparent due to shared CHG-related ancestries.” Even the paper’s cautious conclusion describes Bronze Age Steppe ancestry as only “relatively minor.”

3. Parsis

The second part of our observations will be in response to a possible objection to the first: the low sampling frequency, for which we will now observe the Parsi population in India.

“The Parsis in India are endogamous, and children of women married outside the community are traditionally not accepted within the fold.”

—The Potential Impact of Intermarriage on the Population Decline of the Parsis of Mumbai, India, Zubin Shroff and Márcia C. Castro

“The present-day Iranian population exhibited a striking difference from the Parsis, mainly in carrying an additional European component (light blue) and substantially lower South Asian ancestry (dark green) (Fig. 3 and Additional file 1: Figure S2a and Table S4).”

—Chaubey et al., Genome Biology (2017) 18:110

The Parsi community—Persian Zoroastrian migrants who came to India after 700 CE, around the time Arabs started invading Iran—has very little “European component” compared with modern Iranians, who have had Steppe inputs from Turkic invader populations like the Ghaznavids.

Whatever Steppe ancestry the Parsis may have perhaps comes from Hasanlu/Armenia MLBA—as seen in historical Iranian samples—and mixing with Indians. Perhaps Andronovo ancestry began to arrive in Iran through Turkic invasions, beginning with the Ghaznavids from around the 10th century CE onwards.

Chaubey et al. Genome Biology (2017) 18:110. DOI 10.1186/s13059-017-1244-9.

López et al. (2017) tested 71 Parsi priests and found 0 R1a1a. The study also found that their Iranian Zoroastrian ancestry was mainly preserved through the male line. So here again, an Iranian-derived paternal population has no R1a1a at all (López et al., 2017, Table S9).
