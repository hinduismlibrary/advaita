1. Preface

1.1 Introduction

One of the most widely discussed topics within Hinduism is the status of meat, or mamsa, because of both its great relevance to the daily lives of more than a billion people and its highly controversial nature. The heated disagreements are mainly between three groups.

First are the strict vegetarians, who allege that Hinduism completely bans meat-eating. They base this view on many verses that speak of ahimsa (minimal violence), condemn meat, and glorify abstinence. The second group takes the opposite position. Pointing to such things in scripture as animal sacrifice, hunting, and meat-eating by revered personalities, it says that Hinduism in fact permits all meat. Neither group is willing to engage with the other’s references, instead characterising them as later developments, corruptions, or misinterpretations.

The third group sees all this and concludes that Hindu scriptures must simply be contradictory: different texts, written at different times by different people, which can be used to justify any position at all.

This work belongs to none of these three groups. Without ignoring any references, it will show that the scriptures have a clear and consistent view, using only the primary sources themselves. In the second half, it will also address several adjacent questions.

1.2 On pramanas

Before attempting to prove anything, we must discuss how something is to be proven at all: our epistemology. The scope of this work is not to discuss the economic, nutritional, or any other such aspect of meat. It is only to clarify what Hinduism says about it. In that regard, there is only one valid epistemic source. Lord Krishna states in the Gita:

तस्माच्छास्त्रं प्रमाणं ते कार्याकार्यव्यवस्थितौ। ज्ञात्वा शास्त्रविधानोक्तं कर्म कर्तुमिहार्हसि॥

The Supreme Lord said: “He who discards scriptural procedure and proceeds according to his own wishes attains neither success, happiness, nor the highest goal. Therefore, scripture is your basis for determining what is and is not to be done. Having understood the scriptural injunction, it befits you to perform actions in this world.”

—Bhagavad Gita 16.23–24

Similarly, the Mimamsa Sutras, which contain maxims for scriptural interpretation, state in their second aphorism:

“That which is enjoined by a statement of scripture is dharma.”

Because meat is a matter of dharma and karyakaryavyavasthiti, this work will treat scripture as its sole basis. Here, scripture means the Vedas and the texts endorsed by the Vedas: the Manusmrti, the itihasas (the Mahabharata and Ramayana), and the Puranas.

1.3 Summary

The synthesis of all scriptural injunctions regarding meat-eating, as this article will demonstrate, is as follows.

Purvardha: the main thesis

  1. The general rule (utsarga) is that meat is forbidden because it violates ahimsa.
  2. In certain ritual contexts—including Vedic yajna, shraddha (ancestral rites), tantric rituals, brahmana-bhoga, and hunting by kshatriyas—meat-eating is permitted. This is an exception (apavada) that does not undermine the general rule.
  3. Although meat is permitted in these exceptional cases, it is not encouraged; the scriptures highly praise complete abstention from it.

Uttarardha: related questions

  1. According to some acaryas, another exception is the local practice (deshacara) of certain regions, such as eating fish in Bengal; this is disputed. Although the nutritional value of meat is recognised, it is not a valid exception.
  2. The scriptures strictly forbid beef, even within the exceptional contexts. They also discourage animals such as domesticated pigs, chickens, and camels while preferring animals such as goats, wild boars, and deer.
  3. The Ramayana clearly states that Lord Rama consumed meat under the hunting exception.

2. Purvardha

2.1 Condemnation of meat-eating

The two most important places in scripture for this topic are Manusmrti Chapter 5 and Mahabharata 13.116–117. Indeed, a thorough reading of these two chapters alone would allow anyone to understand the Hindu view of meat. in this article, they will be presented side by side, in a clear progression, alongside parallel verses and other pramanas.

“Meat is never obtained without the killing of animals. The killing of animals does not lead to heaven; therefore, one should avoid meat.”

—Manusmrti 5.48

“Ahimsa is the highest dharma; ahimsa is the greatest austerity. Ahimsa is the highest truth, from which dharma proceeds. Meat is not produced from grass, wood, or stone; it is obtained only by killing a living being. Therefore, there is fault in eating it.”

—Mahabharata 13.116.25–26

These verses clearly establish the principle: meat is forbidden because it can be obtained only by killing animals, which violates ahimsa.

These chapters next explain the etymology of the word mamsa (“meat”) and the karmic consequences of eating it.

“Those who eat the meat of living beings are themselves eaten by those beings in the afterlife; of this I have no doubt. ‘Me he (mam sa) has eaten, therefore I too shall eat him’: understand this, O descendant of Bharata, as the essence of the word mamsa. Both texts give the same wordplay. The sentence “Me he has eaten; I shall eat him too” begins with “me he” (mam sa), which is stated to be the essence of the word mamsa. The Mahabharata goes one step further and explains the general karmic principle—not merely in relation to meat, but to all actions—that whatever one does, one must face in return.

The point is clear. Dharma rests on many principles, including satya (truthfulness) and dama (self-control), but the greatest of these—the one encompassing them all—is ahimsa (minimal violence). Meat, because it violates ahimsa, is therefore forbidden.

2.2 Exceptions in ritual contexts and hunting

Some strict vegetarians, relying on these pramanas, assume a blanket prohibition on all meat. They are then unable to account for the clear references to animal sacrifice in the Vedas, such as the following.

Show Sanskrit

होतारौ यक्षदश्विनौ छागस्य वपाया मेदसो जुषेताम्।

hotaarau yakshadashvinau chhaagasya vapaayaa medaso jushetaam |

“The priest worships the Ashvins with the omentum fat of the goat.”

—Yajurveda 22.42

yanmantham yamodanam yanmaamsam niprinaami te | te te santu svadhaavanto madhumanto ghritashchutah ||

“That mixture, that cooked meat which I offer to you—may it be sweet, filled with ghee, and accompanied by svadha.”

—Atharvaveda 8.4.42

Another Vedic passage is quoted by Ramanuja in his Gita-bhashya on 2.3:

“There is no injury to the animal in rites such as the Agnishomiya. By relinquishing a lower body, such as that of a goat, the animal attains a superior body and heaven. Where the righteous, and not the evildoers, go—there may the god Savitr establish you.’”

Among many other pramanas, strict vegetarians may attempt to reinterpret, ignore, or question the authenticity of such passages, while nonvegetarians may take them as a licence to eat meat in all circumstances. Both approaches are wrong.

The correct interpretive framework is explained by Shankara in his Brahmasutra-bhashya 3.1.25:

Show Sanskrit

ननु “न हिंस्यात्सर्वभूतानि” इति शास्त्रमेव भूतविषयां हिंसामधर्म इत्यवगमयति। बाढम्—उत्सर्गस्तु सः; अपवादोऽयम् “अग्नीषोमीयं पशुमालभेत” इति। उत्सर्गापवादयोश्च व्यवस्थितविषयत्वम्। तस्माद्विशुद्धं कर्म वैदिकम्।

nanu "na himsyaatsarvabhuutaani" iti shaastrameva bhuutavishayaam himsaamadharma ityavagamayati | baadham-utsargastu sah; apavaado'yam "agniishomiiyam pashumaalabheta" iti | utsargaapavaadayoshcha vyavasthitavishayatvam | tasmaadvishuddham karma vaidikam |

“The scripture enjoins, ‘One should not kill any being,’ and thus teaches that violence against beings is adharma. Certainly. But that is the general rule (utsarga), while this is the exception (apavada): ‘Let him sacrifice the animal for Agnishoma.’ The distinct scopes of a general rule and its exception are well known. Therefore, animal sacrifice is a pure Vedic act.”

In Mimamsa, a broad injunction such as ahimsa is the utsarga; a narrower injunction, such as the command to sacrifice an animal in a particular ritual, is the apavada. The latter is treated as a specific exception to the general rule. The same interpretive pattern is found in grammar and other subjects.

In the case of meat, however, this framework need not be imposed from outside: the scriptures themselves state it clearly. Meat is forbidden by the principle of ahimsa, but the apparent violence of a Vedic animal sacrifice is not considered himsa at all.

“The violence prescribed by the Vedas for this world of moving and unmoving beings should be understood as ahimsa, for dharma has issued from the Veda.”

—Manusmrti 5.44

As discussed earlier, dharma is whatever the scriptures—most notably the Vedas—enjoin. Ahimsa is dharma, and what the Vedas declare is dharma; therefore, acts declared by the Vedas to be dharmic are, even when they outwardly resemble violence, regarded as ahimsa.

The word ahimsa itself is worth discussing. The prefix a-, properly called nan, has several meanings, as stated in the following verse:

Show Sanskrit

तत्सादृश्यमभावश्च तदन्यत्वं तदल्पता। अप्राशस्त्यं विरोधश्च नञर्थाः षट् प्रकीर्तिताः॥

tatsaadrishyamabhaavashcha tadanyatvam tadalpataa | apraashastyam virodhashcha nanarthaah shat prakiirtitaah ||

“The six meanings of nan are similarity, absence (abhava), separation, minimisation (alpata), non-commendability, and opposition.”

Two meanings are especially relevant here: abhava (complete absence) and alpa (minimal or least). Although the former is usually assumed, the latter is more accurate in this context. Ahimsa does not mean an absolute ban on all violence, but minimal violence: refraining from unnecessary harm to any being. Actions enjoined by scripture—such as defending one’s country, self-defence, or a king’s punishment of criminals—are considered necessary and therefore do not violate ahimsa. Scriptural injunctions involving animal sacrifice or the consumption of meat fall under the same principle.

A similar principle appears in the Devi Bhagavatam.

Janaka replied: “That which appears to be himsa in Vedic rituals is in fact ahimsa. It is called himsa only in a qualified or nominal sense; it is not otherwise himsa.”

The Mahabharata states the same principle.

“What is consecrated in rites for the ancestors and deities, according to the proper Vedic procedure, is called havis, and one is not polluted by eating it. The shruti also states that animals were created for Vedic rituals. Conduct apart from this is described as demonic practice.”

—Mahabharata 13.116.5

These verses first establish the permissibility of animal sacrifice and consumption within yajna, citing the same shruti that says animals were created for yajna. The Manusmrti says yajna is “for the benefit of all,” alluding to the teaching that a sacrificed animal attains a higher realm. The Mahabharata also clarifies that what is sacrificed is called havis, not mamsa, the latter being associated with karmic consequences.

Second, the texts make clear that this is only an exception (apavada), not the general rule (utsarga), because they condemn meat-eating outside this exception as “demonic practice.”

Show Sanskrit

हविर्यत्संस्कृतं मन्त्रैः प्रोक्षिताभ्युक्षितं शुचि। वेदोक्तेन प्रमाणेन पितृणां प्रक्रियासु च। अतोऽन्यथा वृथामांसमभक्ष्यं मनुरब्रवीत्॥

haviryatsamskritam mantraih prokshitaabhyukshitam shuchi | vedoktena pramaanena pitrinaam prakriyaasu cha | ato'nyathaa vrithaamaamsamabhakshyam manurabraviit ||

“That which is properly consecrated by mantras, purified according to the Vedic procedure, or used in rites for the ancestors, is pure havis. Anything else is vain meat, which Manu declared unfit to be eaten.”

—Mahabharata 13.116.50

Ritual exceptions to the prohibition are also supported in the Puranas.

Show Sanskrit

यद् घ्राणभक्षो विहितः सुरायास्तथा पशोरालभनं न हिंसा। एवं व्यवायः प्रजया न रत्या इमं विशुद्धं न विदुः स्वधर्मम्॥
ये त्वनेवंविदोऽसन्तः स्तब्धाः सदभिमानिनः। पशून् द्रुह्यन्ति विश्रब्धाः प्रेत्य खादन्ति ते च तान्॥

yad ghraanabhaksho vihitah suraayaastathaa pashoraalabhanam na himsaa | evam vyavaayah prajayaa na ratyaa imam vishuddham na viduh svadharmam ||

ye tvanevamvido'santah stabdhaah sadabhimaaninah | pashuun druhyanti vishrabdhaah pretya khaadanti te cha taan ||

“The smelling of wine is prescribed, and the ritual offering of an animal is not himsa. Similarly, intercourse is for the conception of a child, not for pleasure. Those impious and arrogant men who do not understand this pure dharma confidently harm animals; after death, those animals eat them.”

—Bhagavata Purana 11.5.13–14

The Bhagavata states that acts such as smelling wine, eating meat, and sexual intercourse have prescribed contexts in which they are lawful—ritual contexts for the first two and conception for the third. Those who perform these acts outside their prescribed contexts face the karmic consequences; one who kills an animal outside a ritual context is eaten by that animal in the afterlife.

Show Sanskrit

मांसाशनं ये कुर्वन्ति तैः कार्यं पशुहिंसनम्। महिषाजवराहाणां बलिदानं विशिष्यते॥
देव्यग्रे निहता यान्ति पशवः स्वर्गमव्ययम्। न हिंसा पशुजा तत्र निघ्नतां तत्कृतेऽनघ॥
अहिंसा याज्ञिकी प्रोक्ता सर्वशास्त्रविनिर्णये॥

maamsaashanam ye kurvanti taih kaaryam pashuhimsanam | mahishaajavaraahaanaam balidaanam vishishyate ||

devyagre nihataa yaanti pashavah svargamavyayam | na himsaa pashujaa tatra nighnataam tatkrite'nagha ||

ahimsaa yaajnikii proktaa sarvashaastravinirnaye ||

“Those who eat meat may perform animal sacrifice, especially the sacrifice of a buffalo, goat, or wild boar. Animals sacrificed before the Goddess attain everlasting heaven. No himsa arises from the animal’s death in that act, O sinless one. The consensus of all scriptures declares the ahimsa of yajna.”

—Devi Bhagavatam 3.26.32–34

It is notable that sacrifice “before the Goddess” refers not to a Vedic sacrifice but to a tantric one. In addition to sacrifices in Vedic, ancestral, and tantric contexts, the Mahabharata adds the exception of brahmana-bhoga, feeding brahmanas.

Show Sanskrit

अप्रोक्षितं वृथामांसं विधिहीनं न भक्षयेत्। भक्षयन्निरयं याति नरो नास्त्यत्र संशयः॥
प्रोक्षिताभ्युक्षितं मांसं तथा ब्राह्मणकाम्यया। अल्पदोषमिह ज्ञेयं विपरीते तु लिप्यते॥

aprokshitam vrithaamaamsam vidhihiinam na bhakshayet | bhakshayannirayam yaati naro naastyatra samshayah ||

prokshitaabhyukshitam maamsam tathaa braahmanakaamyayaa | alpadoshamiha jneyam vipariite tu lipyate ||

“One should not eat meat that has not been consecrated and is devoid of proper procedure. A person who eats it goes to hell; of this there is no doubt. Meat properly consecrated, or prepared for feeding brahmanas, entails only minimal fault; one is tainted by acting otherwise.”

—Mahabharata 13.116.47–48

Finally, the Manusmrti summarises all the ritual exceptions, together with the exception of an emergency, in a single verse.

Show Sanskrit

प्रोक्षितं भक्षयेन्मांसं ब्राह्मणानां च काम्यया। यथाविधि नियुक्तस्तु प्राणानामेव चात्यये॥

prokshitam bhakshayenmaamsam braahmanaanaam cha kaamyayaa | yathaavidhi niyuktastu praanaanaameva chaatyaye ||

“One may eat meat that has been properly consecrated, at the wish of brahmanas, when duly appointed according to rule, or when one’s life is in danger.”

—Manusmrti 5.27

Hunting

We now come to the final exception: hunting. Unlike the exceptions already discussed, this is not technically a ritual context, but its justification is tied to ritual exceptions.

Show Sanskrit

आरण्याः सर्वदैवत्याः प्रोक्षिताः सर्वशो मृगाः। अगस्त्येन पुरा राजन् मृगया येन पूज्यते॥
राजर्षयः सर्व एव मृगयां यान्ति भारत। लिप्यन्ते न हि दोषेण न चैतत्पातकं विदुः॥

aaranyaah sarvadaivatyaah prokshitaah sarvasho mrigaah | agastyena puraa raajan mrigayaa yena puujyate ||

raajarshayah sarva eva mrigayaam yaanti bhaarata | lipyante na hi doshena na chaitatpaatakam viduh ||

“In ancient times, O king, all forest animals were consecrated by the great sage Agastya. Therefore all royal sages hunt deer without being tainted, for it is not known to be a sin.”

—Mahabharata 13.117.17–19

The Manusmrti also alludes to this.

Show Sanskrit

यज्ञार्थं ब्राह्मणैर्वध्याः प्रशस्ता मृगपक्षिणः। भृत्यानां चैव वृत्त्यर्थमगस्त्यो ह्याचरत्पुरा॥

yajnaartham braahmanairvadhyaah prashastaa mrigapakshinah | bhrityaanaam chaiva vrittyarthamagastyo hyaacharatpuraa ||

“Brahmanas may kill the permitted animals and birds for yajna and for the sustenance of dependants, as Agastya did in ancient times.”

—Manusmrti 5.22

The Bhagavata likewise permits hunting for kshatriyas, while specifying that it should not be excessive.

Show Sanskrit

तीर्थेषु प्रतिदृष्टेषु राजा मेध्यान् पशून् वने। यावदर्थमलं लुब्धो हन्यादिति नियम्यते॥

tiirtheshu pratidrishteshu raajaa medhyaan pashuun vane | yaavadarthamalam lubdho hanyaaditi niyamyate ||

“The rule is that, in sacred places and according to scriptural injunction, a king may hunt forest animals fit for sacrifice, but only as many as are required and without greed.”

—Bhagavata Purana 4.26.10

2.3 Abstention even from lawful meat

The fifth chapter of the Manusmrti has now been cited extensively, and its position on meat-eating is clear: eating meat is generally forbidden, but ritual exceptions exist. Nevertheless, one verse from the chapter is often quoted in isolation to claim that meat is permitted in every context.

Show Sanskrit

न मांसभक्षणे दोषो न मद्ये न च मैथुने। प्रवृत्तिरेषा भूतानां निवृत्तिस्तु महाफला॥

na maamsabhakshane dosho na madye na cha maithune | pravrittireshaa bhuutaanaam nivrittistu mahaaphalaa ||

“There is no fault in eating meat, drinking liquor, or engaging in intercourse; these are the natural tendencies of living beings, but abstention from them yields great rewards.”

—Manusmrti 5.56

The intended meaning should now be clear from the rest of the chapter. The verse does not say that meat, liquor, and intercourse are permissible in every circumstance. It says that no universal fault attaches to them: they may be lawful in some contexts and prohibited in others. Meat and liquor are lawful in prescribed ritual contexts, while intercourse is lawful for procreation, as the Bhagavata explains.

Medhatithi clarifies the point:

“It has been asserted that ‘there is no sin in the eating of meat.’ This means that there is no sin if one eats meat remaining from the worship of the gods, meat eaten at the wish of brahmanas, or meat eaten under the other circumstances specified above—and only if he wishes to eat it. These three things, in their strictly restricted forms, constitute the natural way of living beings sanctioned by scripture for the maintenance of the body.”

The final phrase, however—“abstention yields great rewards”—brings us to the next point. Although scripture permits meat in certain exceptional cases, it does not encourage meat-eating; on the contrary, it highly praises abstention.

Show Sanskrit

वर्षे वर्षेऽश्वमेधेन यो यजेत शतं समाः। मांसानि च न खादेद्यस्तयोः पुण्यफलं समम्॥
फलमूलाशनैर्मेध्यैर्मुन्यन्नानां च भोजनैः। न तत्फलमवाप्नोति यन्मांसपरिवर्जनात्॥

varshe varshe'shvamedhena yo yajeta shatam samaah | maamsaani cha na khaadedyastayoh punyaphalam samam ||

phalamuulaashanairmedhyairmunyannaanaam cha bhojanaih | na tatphalamavaapnoti yanmaamsaparivarjanaat ||

“One who performs an Ashvamedha every year for a hundred years and one who abstains from meat receive equal fruits of merit. Even by eating pure fruits, roots, and the foods of sages, one does not attain as much merit as one gains by abstaining from meat.”

—Manusmrti 5.53–54

The Mahabharata also praises abstention from meat at length.

Show Sanskrit

सप्तर्षयो वालखिल्यास्तथैव च मरीचिपाः। मांसस्याभक्षणं राजन् प्रशंसन्ति मनीषिणः॥

अद्वेष्यः सर्वभूतानां विश्वस्यः सर्वजन्तुषु। साधूनां सम्मतो नित्यं भवेन्मांसं विवर्जयन्॥

saptarshayo vaalakhilyaastathaiva cha mariichipaah | maamsasyaabhakshanam raajan prashamsanti maniishinah ||

na bhakshayati yo maamsam na cha hanyaanna ghaatayet | tanmitram sarvabhuutaanaam manuh svaayambhuvo'braviit ||

adveshyah sarvabhuutaanaam vishvasyah sarvajantushu | saadhuunaam sammato nityam bhavenmaamsam vivarjayan ||

“The seven rshis, the Valakhilyas, the Maricipas, and other great sages praise abstention from meat, O king. He who neither eats meat, harms, nor causes harm is the friend of all beings, as Svayambhuva Manu declared. By abstaining from meat, one becomes unhated by all beings, trusted by every creature, and always approved by the good.”

—Mahabharata 13.116

Show Sanskrit

ददाति यजते चापि तपस्वी च भवत्यपि। मधुमांसनिवृत्त्येति प्राहैवं स बृहस्पतिः॥
मासि मास्यश्वमेधेन यो यजेत शतं समाः। न खादति च यो मांसं सममेतन्मतं मम॥
सदा यजति सत्रेण सदा दानं प्रयच्छति। सदा तपस्वी भवति मद्यमांसविवर्जनात्॥
सर्वे वेदा न तत्कुर्युः सर्वे यज्ञाश्च भारत। यो भक्षयित्वा मांसानि पश्चादपि निवर्तते॥

dadaati yajate chaapi tapasvii cha bhavatyapi | madhumaamsanivrittyeti praahaivam sa brihaspatih ||

maasi maasyashvamedhena yo yajeta shatam samaah | na khaadati cha yo maamsam samametanmatam mama ||

sadaa yajati satrena sadaa daanam prayachchhati | sadaa tapasvii bhavati madyamaamsavivarjanaat ||

sarve vedaa na tatkuryuh sarve yajnaashcha bhaarata | yo bhakshayitvaa maamsaani pashchaadapi nivartate ||

“By abstaining from meat and honey, one at once gives charity, performs Vedic ritual, and becomes an ascetic; so Brhaspati declared. One who performs an Ashvamedha every month for a hundred years and one who abstains from meat are, in my view, equal. By abstaining from wine and meat, one perpetually performs great Vedic sacrifices, practises charity, and undertakes austerity. All the Vedas and all rituals together, O descendant of Bharata, cannot produce the merit gained by one who gives up meat even after having eaten it in the past.”

Scripture praises not only complete abstinence, but also gradual abstinence through fortnightly, monthly, or yearly vows.

Show Sanskrit

कौमुद्यास्तु विशेषेण शुक्लपक्षे नराधिप। वर्जयेत्सर्वमांसानि धर्मो ह्यत्र विधीयते॥
अथवा मासमेकं वै सर्वमांसान्यभक्षयन्। अतीत्य सर्वदुःखानि सुखं जीवेद् निरामयः॥
वर्जयन्ति हि मांसानि मासशः पक्षशोऽपि वा। तेषां हिंसानिवृत्तानां ब्रह्मलोको विधीयते॥
अमांसभक्षणं चैव कौमुद्यां पार्थ राजभिः। सर्वभूतात्मभूतैस्तैर्विदितार्थपरावरैः॥

kaumudyaastu visheshena shuklapakshe naraadhipa | varjayetsarvamaamsaani dharmo hyatra vidhiiyate ||

athavaa maasamekam vai sarvamaamsaanyabhakshayan | atiitya sarvaduhkhaani sukham jiived niraamayah ||

varjayanti hi maamsaani maasashah pakshasho'pi vaa | teshaam himsaanivrittaanaam brahmaloko vidhiiyate ||

amaamsabhakshanam chaiva kaumudyaam paartha raajabhih | sarvabhuutaatmabhuutaistairviditaarthaparaavaraih ||

“One should especially abstain from all meat during the bright fortnight, O king; this is the prescribed dharma. Alternatively, one may abstain from all meat for an entire month; thereby, having passed beyond all sorrow, one lives happily and free from illness. Those who abstain from meat for a month or even a fortnight, having withdrawn from violence, are destined for Brahmaloka. Kings of old, O son of Prtha—men who regarded themselves as one with all beings and knew the higher and lower truths—abstained from meat during the moonlit season.”

नात्मनोऽस्ति प्रियतरः पृथिव्यामनुसृत्य ह। तस्मात्प्राणिषु सर्वेषु दयावानात्मवान् भवेत्॥
सर्वमांसानि यो राजन् यावज्जीवं न भक्षयेत्। स्वर्गे स विपुलं स्थानं प्राप्नुयान्नात्र संशयः॥
धन्यं यशस्यमायुष्यं स्वर्ग्यं स्वस्त्ययनं महत्। मांसस्याभक्षणं प्राहुर्नियताः परमर्षयः॥

sarvamaamsaani yo raajan yaavajjiivam na bhakshayet | svarge sa vipulam sthaanam praapnuyaannaatra samshayah ||

dhanyam yashasyamaayushyam svargyam svastyayanam mahat | maamsasyaabhakshanam praahurniyataah paramarshayah ||

“Nothing on earth is dearer to a being than its own self. Therefore, one should be compassionate towards every living being, regarding them as oneself. He who abstains from all meat throughout his life, O king, attains an exalted place in heaven; of this there is no doubt. The great rshis declare abstention from meat to bring prosperity, fame, long life, heaven, and great welfare.”

Guna-classification of meat

Having addressed a pramana used to justify all meat, let us now address one used to condemn every form of meat-eating.

Objection. The Mahabharata and Skanda Purana recount a story in which rshis condemn an animal sacrifice being performed by Indra.

Show Sanskrit

ततो दीनान् पशून् दृष्ट्वा ऋषयस्ते तपोधनाः। ऊचुः शक्रं समागम्य नायं यज्ञविधिः शुभः॥
अपविज्ञानमेतत्ते महान्तं धर्ममिच्छतः। न हि यज्ञे पशुगणा विधिदृष्टाः पुरंदर॥
धर्मोपघातकस्त्वेष समारम्भस्तव प्रभो। नायं धर्मकृतो धर्मो न हिंसा धर्म उच्यते॥

tato diinaan pashuun drishtvaa rishayaste tapodhanaah | uuchuh shakram samaagamya naayam yajnavidhih shubhah ||

apavijnaanametatte mahaantam dharmamichchhatah | na hi yajne pashuganaa vidhidrishtaah puramdara ||

dharmopaghaatakastvesha samaarambhastava prabho | naayam dharmakrito dharmo na himsaa dharma uchyate ||

“Seeing the miserable animals bound for sacrifice, the ascetic rshis approached Indra and said: ‘This is not an auspicious procedure of yajna. Although you seek dharma, this is a grave error in judgement. O Indra, animal sacrifices are not prescribed in this yajna. What you have begun violates dharma; an act does not become dharma merely because it is performed in its name, and violence is not called dharma.’”

—Mahabharata 14.94.12–14

The same story appears in the Skanda Purana 2.9.6:

Show Sanskrit

महर्षय उपाजग्मुस्तत्र भास्करवर्चसः। सम्मानिताः सुरगणैः पाद्यार्घ्यस्वागतादिभिः॥
ते बृहन्मुनयोऽपश्यन्मेध्यांस्तान् क्रोशतः पशून्। धर्मव्यतिक्रमं दृष्ट्वा कृपया ते द्विजोत्तमाः॥
महेन्द्रप्रमुखानूचुर्देवान् धर्मधियस्ततः। अहिंसैव परो धर्मस्तत्र वेदेऽस्ति कीर्तितः। साक्षात्पशुवधो यज्ञे न हि वेदस्य सम्मतः॥

maharshaya upaajagmustatra bhaaskaravarchasah | sammaanitaah suraganaih paadyaarghyasvaagataadibhih ||

te brihanmunayo'pashyanmedhyaamstaan kroshatah pashuun | dharmavyatikramam drishtvaa kripayaa te dvijottamaah ||

mahendrapramukhaanuuchurdevaan dharmadhiyastatah | ahimsaiva paro dharmastatra vede'sti kiirtitah | saakshaatpashuvadho yajne na hi vedasya sammatah ||

“The great radiant rshis arrived at the yajna and were honoured by the deities with the washing of their feet, offerings, and words of welcome. They then saw the sacrificial animals crying out. Moved by compassion at this violation of dharma, those best of brahmanas spoke to the deities, with Indra at their head: ‘Ahimsa is the highest dharma, as the Veda declares. The actual killing of animals in this yajna is not approved by the Veda.’”

Answer. Read in full, the story does not condemn animal sacrifice absolutely. It introduces a classification by guna: devotees of Vishnu, including the devas, should not perform it, although it remains permitted for Shaivas and others. The rshis do not say that animals are never sacrificed; they say that this particular sacrifice does not accord with the applicable vidhi. Beginning with verse 18 of the Skanda Purana version:

Show Sanskrit

यादृशो हि गुणो यस्य स्वभावस्तस्य तादृशः। स्वस्वभावानुसारेण प्रवृत्तिः स्याच्च मानवैः॥ १८॥
सात्त्विकानां हि वो देवः साक्षाद्विष्णू रमापतिः। अहिंसायज्ञेऽस्ति ततोऽधिकारस्तस्य तुष्टये॥ १९॥
प्रत्यक्षपशुमालभ्य यज्ञस्याचरणं तु यत्। धर्मः स विपरीतो वै युष्माकं सुरसत्तमाः॥

yaadrisho hi guno yasya svabhaavastasya taadrishah | svasvabhaavaanusaarena pravrittih syaachcha maanavaih || 18 ||

saattvikaanaam hi vo devah saakshaadvishnuu ramaapatih | ahimsaayajne'sti tato'dhikaarastasya tushtaye || 19 ||

pratyakshapashumaalabhya yajnasyaacharanam tu yat | dharmah sa vipariito vai yushmaakam surasattamaah ||

“As one’s guna is, so is one’s nature; and according to one’s nature, one inclines towards particular actions. For sattvika beings such as you devas, Vishnu, the husband of Sita, is the deity; you are therefore qualified to please him through an ahimsa-yajna. The practice of sacrificing an actual animal is contrary to dharma for you devas.”

The rshis then clarify that animal sacrifice is permitted for non-Vaishnavas:

3. Uttarardha

3.1 Cow and other forbidden meats

Hunting and prescribed ritual contexts are valid exceptions for eating meat, but this does not mean that every animal may be sacrificed and eaten. As seen in the Devi Bhagavatam, goats, buffaloes, and wild boars are preferred for sacrifice. The Manusmrti 5.12–18 elaborates on lawful and forbidden meats.

Show Sanskrit

क्रव्यादान् शकुनान् सर्वांस्तथा ग्रामनिवासिनः। अनिर्दिष्टांश्चैकशफान् टिट्टिभं च विवर्जयेत्॥
कलविङ्कं प्लवं हंसं चक्राह्वं ग्रामकुक्कुटम्। सारसं रज्जुवालं च दात्यूहं शुकसारिके॥
मत्स्यादान् विड्वराहांश्च मत्स्यानेव च सर्वशः॥
पाठीनरोहितावाद्यौ नियुक्तौ हव्यकव्ययोः। राजीवान् सिंहतुण्डांश्च सशल्कांश्चैव सर्वशः॥

“He shall avoid all carnivorous birds and all birds that live in villages, unspecified one-hoofed animals, and the tittibha; the sparrow, plava, hamsa, cakravaka, domesticated chicken, crane, rajjuvala, datyuha, parrot, and starling; fish-eating animals, the domesticated pig, and all fish—except the pathina and rohita when properly dedicated in rites, as well as the rajiva, simhatunda, and all fish with scales.”

Among birds, carnivorous species such as herons and vultures, along with village-dwelling species such as pigeons and crows, are forbidden. One-hoofed animals such as horses, donkeys, and mules are also forbidden, as are several specifically named birds, including the chicken—perhaps the most widely eaten meat today. Fish and fish-eating animals are forbidden, with exceptions for the listed species when properly consecrated.

Show Sanskrit

na bhakshayedekacharaanajnaataamshcha mrigadvijaan | bhakshyeshvapi samuddishtaan sarvaan panchanakhaamstathaa ||

shvaavidham shalyakam godhaam khadgakuurmashashaamstathaa | bhakshyaan panchanakheshvaahuranushtraamshchaikatodatah ||

“He shall not eat solitary or unknown animals and birds, even when they resemble species declared fit for consumption; nor shall he eat five-nailed animals. Among five-nailed animals, the porcupine, hedgehog, alligator, rhinoceros, tortoise, and hare are declared fit to eat, as are animals having one row of teeth—with the exception of the camel.”

Also forbidden are animals that do not live in herds, such as owls and snakes, and newly discovered or unknown species even when they resemble permitted animals. Five-nailed animals such as monkeys are forbidden, subject to the stated exceptions. Animals with one row of teeth are generally allowed, except for the camel. The broad principle is that domesticated animals (gramya) and carnivorous animals are forbidden.

These verses also demonstrate how Manu and other shastras operate through the utsarga–apavada system. Manu first says that all fish are forbidden—the general rule—and immediately allows five species as exceptions. Likewise, he first prohibits all five-nailed animals and then names exceptions. The text does not need to say explicitly, “These are exceptions”; the relationship is understood.

Beef

The most famous prohibition in Hinduism, however, is the prohibition on beef. Many misconceptions—whether genuine or maliciously spread—claim that beef was not originally forbidden in Hinduism and that the prohibition is a later development. The scriptures tell a different story.

3.2 Deshacara and health: valid exceptions?

Two other cases are sometimes claimed as exceptions to the prohibition on meat. Deshacara is recognised by a few ancient acaryas, while health reasons are cited mostly in modern times. Let us examine both.

Deshacara: disputed

Some acaryas from regions with ancient customs of meat-eating have argued that the inhabitants of those regions may continue such practices even outside sacrificial contexts. They cite the following passage from the Brhaspati Smriti 1.26–30 and Shukraniti 4.46–49:

Show Sanskrit

देशजातिकुलादीनां ये धर्मास्तत्प्रवर्तिताः। तथैव ते पालनीयाः प्रजा प्रक्षुभ्यतेऽन्यथा॥ जनापरक्तिर्भवति बलं कोशश्च नश्यति। उदूह्यते दाक्षिणात्यैर्मातुलस्य सुता द्विजैः…

deshajaatikulaadiinaam ye dharmaastatpravartitaah | tathaiva te paalaniiyaah prajaa prakshubhyate'nyathaa || janaaparaktirbhavati balam koshashcha nashyati | uduuhyate daakshinaatyairmaatulasya sutaa dvijaih...

“Whatever customs pertain to a region, caste, or clan are to be tolerated; otherwise, the populace rebels, its loyalty is lost, and the army and treasury are destroyed. Twice-born men of the south marry the daughters of their maternal uncles…”

The argument is that practices followed in particular regions (desha), castes (jati), or clans (kula) should be permitted—not because they are inherently moral, but as a pragmatic measure to keep the populace in order. The passage then lists local practices.

The relevant statement for our purposes is:

Show Sanskrit

मत्स्यादाश्च नराः पूर्वे।

matsyaadaashcha naraah puurve |

“People in the east eat fish.”

Some acaryas from eastern regions such as Bengal, including Raghunandana, have used this statement to argue that eastern people may eat fish even when it has not been ritually consecrated.

Because this position was accepted by respected acaryas and has scriptural support, it is not invalid in itself. Nevertheless, the great majority of other acaryas rejected it. The Gautama Dharmasutra 9.20, for example, states:

Show Sanskrit

देशजातिकुलधर्माश्चाम्नायैरविरुद्धाः प्रमाणम्॥ २०॥

deshajaatikuladharmaashchaamnaayairaviruddhaah pramaanam || 20 ||

“The customs of a region, caste, and clan may be taken as a basis of dharma if they do not contradict the Vedic scriptures.”

The majority view is therefore that local customs may be tolerated only when they do not contradict the Vedic scriptures. Because scripture strongly condemns meat-eating outside ritual contexts or hunting, such customs are to be rejected. The statement of the Brhaspati Smriti and Shukraniti may instead be understood as a pragmatic rule of niti: the king tolerates these practices lest the populace rebel, rather than declaring the practices themselves to be dharmic.

Even the acaryas who recognise deshacara as an exception for meat-eating restrict it to ancient local customs, not new practices. One therefore cannot argue, for example, that a Malayali may eat beef merely because the practice has recently become prevalent in Kerala.

Health reasons: entirely condemned

Another justification commonly offered, especially in modern times, is health.

First, this work does not deny the nutritional benefits of meat. Some vegetarians approach the issue by attempting to deny meat’s nutritional superiority, but its nutritive value is acknowledged in scripture, Ayurveda, and modern science. Because this is a scriptural analysis, only the scriptural position will be stated here.

Show Sanskrit

न मांसात्परमं किंचिद्रसतो विद्यते भुवि॥
क्षतक्षीणाभितप्तानां ग्राम्यधर्मरतात्मनाम्। अध्वना कर्शितानां च न मांसाद्विद्यते परम्॥
सद्यो वर्धयति प्राणान् पुष्टिमग्र्यां दधाति च॥

kshatakshiinaabhitaptaanaam graamyadharmarataatmanaam | adhvanaa karshitaanaam cha na maamsaadvidyate param ||

“There is nothing on earth superior to meat in taste. Nothing is superior to meat for the injured, emaciated, exhausted, those engaged in labour, or those wearied by travel. It quickly increases vitality and provides excellent nourishment.”

—Mahabharata 13.117.7–8

Although meat’s power to produce nourishment (vrddhi) is recognised, seeking that nourishment is condemned in the same chapter.

न हि प्राणात्प्रियतरं लोके किंचन विद्यते। तस्माद्दयां नरः कुर्याद्यथात्मनि तथा परे॥

“He who wishes to nourish his own flesh with the flesh of another—there is no man lower or more cruel than he. Nothing in the world is dearer than life; therefore, one should show the same compassion to others as to oneself.”

—Mahabharata 13.117.13

The formula

—Manusmrti 5.52 and Vishnu Smriti 51.74

Show Sanskrit

स्वमांसं परमांसेन यो वर्धयति निर्दयः। स नूनं नरकं याति प्रोक्तमेतन्महर्षिभिः॥

svamaamsam paramaamsena yo vardhayati nirdayah | sa nuunam narakam yaati proktametanmaharshibhih ||

“The pitiless person who nourishes his own flesh with the flesh of another certainly goes to hell; so the great rshis have declared.”

—Skanda Purana 6.22.2

Show Sanskrit

स्वमांसं परमांसेन यो वर्धयितुमिच्छति। न पापकृत्तमो लोके तस्मादन्यो हि विद्यते॥
असंस्कृतान् पशून् मन्त्रैर्नाद्याद्विप्रः कथंचन। संस्कृतानद्याच्छाश्वतं विधिमास्थितः॥

svamaamsam paramaamsena yo vardhayitumichchhati | na paapakrittamo loke tasmaadanyo hi vidyate ||

asamskritaan pashuun mantrairnaadyaadviprah kathamchana | samskritaanadyaachchhaashvatam vidhimaasthitah ||

“There is no greater sinner in the world than one who wishes to nourish his own flesh with the flesh of another. A brahmana should never eat an animal that has not been consecrated with mantras; he may eat one that has been consecrated, while remaining established in the eternal rule.”

—Vishnudharmottara Purana 3.25.24

Thus, although the nutritional value of meat is recognised, one should not eat it for that reason, because doing so amounts to nourishing one’s own flesh with the flesh of another.

3.3 Did Lord Rama eat meat?

We now come to the final topic, which is perhaps the most controversial apart from beef.

This entire paper should make clear that it would not be morally problematic if Rama ate meat. Many Hindus—especially vegetarian devotees of Lord Rama—regard the claim as an accusation and deny it for that reason. Yet, as shown by the passages from the Manusmrti, Mahabharata, and Bhagavata, eating hunted meat is entirely lawful for a kshatriya such as Rama. Rama himself says:

Show Sanskrit

मांसहेतोरपि मृगान् विनोदार्थं च धन्विनः। घ्नन्ति लक्ष्मण राजानो मृगयायां महावने॥

maamsahetorapi mrigaan vinodaartham cha dhanvinah | ghnanti lakshmana raajaano mrigayaayaam mahaavane ||

“O Lakshmana, kings and bowmen hunt wild animals in the great forest both for sport and for meat.”

—Ramayana 3.43.30

We may now examine passages from Valmiki’s Ramayana—all within the context of sacrifice or hunting—in which Rama ate meat.

Rama first commands Lakshmana to hunt and bring him meat:

Show Sanskrit

मृगं हत्वानय क्षिप्रं लक्ष्मणेह शुभेक्षण। कर्तव्यः शास्त्रदृष्टो हि विधिर्धर्ममनुस्मर॥
भ्रातुर्वचनमाज्ञाय लक्ष्मणः परवीरहा। चकार स यथोक्तं च तं रामः पुनरब्रवीत्॥
ऐणेयं श्रपयस्वैतच्छालां यक्ष्यामहे वयम्। त्वर सौम्य मुहूर्तोऽयं ध्रुवश्च दिवसोऽप्ययम्॥
स लक्ष्मणः कृष्णमृगं मेध्यं हत्वा प्रतापवान्। अथ चिक्षेप सौमित्रिः समिद्धे जातवेदसि॥
तं तु पक्वं परिज्ञाय निष्टप्तं छिन्नशोणितम्। लक्ष्मणः पुरुषव्याघ्रमथ राघवमब्रवीत्॥
अयं सर्वः समस्ताङ्गः श्रुतः कृष्णमृगो मया। देवतां देवसंकाश यजस्व कुशलो ह्यसि॥
रामः स्नात्वा तु नियतो गुणवान् जप्यकोविदः। संग्रहेणाकरोत्सर्वान् मन्त्रान् सत्रावसानिकान्॥

mrigam hatvaanaya kshipram lakshmaneha shubhekshana | kartavyah shaastradrishto hi vidhirdharmamanusmara ||

bhraaturvachanamaajnaaya lakshmanah paraviirahaa | chakaara sa yathoktam cha tam raamah punarabraviit ||

aineyam shrapayasvaitachchhaalaam yakshyaamahe vayam | tvara saumya muhuurto'yam dhruvashcha divaso'pyayam ||

sa lakshmanah krishnamrigam medhyam hatvaa prataapavaan | atha chikshepa saumitrih samiddhe jaatavedasi ||

tam tu pakvam parijnaaya nishtaptam chhinnashonitam | lakshmanah purushavyaaghramatha raaghavamabraviit ||

ayam sarvah samastaanggah shrutah krishnamrigo mayaa | devataam devasamkaasha yajasva kushalo hyasi ||

raamah snaatvaa tu niyato gunavaan japyakovidah | samgrahenaakarotsarvaan mantraan satraavasaanikaan ||

Rama said: “O Lakshmana, quickly kill a deer and bring it here. Remember dharma, for the prescribed rite is to be performed according to scripture.” Having received his brother’s command, Lakshmana, the slayer of enemy heroes, did as instructed. Rama then said: “Cook this deer. We shall perform the sacrifice with it. Hurry, gentle one; the auspicious moment has begun, and the day is passing.” Lakshmana, son of Sumitra, killed a consecrated black deer and placed it in the blazing fire. When he knew it to be fully cooked and drained of blood, he said to Rama: “This black deer has been thoroughly cooked by me, complete in all its limbs. O you who resemble the gods, offer it to the deity, for you are skilled in the rite.” The disciplined and virtuous Rama then bathed and, being versed in recitation, performed all the concluding mantras of the ritual.

—Ramayana 2.56.23–28

Another passage in the Ayodhyakanda describes Rama offering meat to Sita:

Show Sanskrit

तां तथा दर्शयित्वा तु मैथिलीं गिरिनिम्नगाम्। निषसाद गिरिप्रस्थे सीतां मांसेन छन्दयन्॥
इदं मेध्यमिदं स्वादु निष्टप्तमिदमग्निना। एवमास्ते स धर्मात्मा सीतया सह राघवः॥

taam tathaa darshayitvaa tu maithiliim girinimnagaam | nishasaada giriprasthe siitaam maamsena chhandayan ||

idam medhyamidam svaadu nishtaptamidamagninaa | evamaaste sa dharmaatmaa siitayaa saha raaghavah ||

“Having shown the mountain river to the princess of Mithila, Rama sat upon the mountainside and gratified Sita with meat, saying, ‘This is sacrificial; this is tasty; this has been well roasted over the fire.’ Thus the righteous descendant of Raghu sat there with Sita.”

These are instances of eating sacrificial meat. The following passages concern hunting.

Show Sanskrit

तौ तत्र हत्वा चतुरो महामृगान् वराहमृश्यं पृषतं महारुरुम्। आदाय मेध्यं त्वरितं बुभुक्षितौ वासाय काले ययतुर्वनस्पतिम्॥

tau tatra hatvaa chaturo mahaamrigaan varaahamrishyam prishatam mahaarurum | aadaaya medhyam tvaritam bubhukshitau vaasaaya kaale yayaturvanaspatim ||

“There the two of them, Rama and Lakshmana, being hungry, killed four large wild animals—a wild boar, a white-footed antelope, a spotted deer, and a great black deer. Quickly taking the consecrated meat, they went at evening to a tree under which to spend the night.”

—Ramayana 2.52.102

The word bubhukshitau, “hungry,” makes clear that the hunting was undertaken for food. The passage also uses medhyam. This word comes from medha, a Vedic ritual, and means “sacrificial” or “worthy of sacrifice.” Valmiki thereby clarifies that, because of Agastya’s ancient sacrifice, wild animals are medhya and fit to be eaten.

The word is used again in another hunting passage:

Show Sanskrit

क्रोशमात्रं ततो गत्वा भ्रातरौ रामलक्ष्मणौ। बहून् मेध्यान् मृगान् हत्वा चेरतुर्यमुनावने॥

kroshamaatram tato gatvaa bhraatarau raamalakshmanau | bahuun medhyaan mrigaan hatvaa cheraturyamunaavane ||

“After travelling only about a krosha, the brothers Rama and Lakshmana killed many medhya animals and moved through the Yamuna forest.”

—Ramayana 2.55.3

Show Sanskrit

निहत्य पृषतं चान्यं मांसमादाय राघवः। त्वरमाणो जनस्थानं ससाराभिमुखस्तदा॥

nihatya prishatam chaanyam maamsamaadaaya raaghavah | tvaramaano janasthaanam sasaaraabhimukhastadaa ||

“Having killed another spotted deer and taken its meat, Rama quickly proceeded towards Janasthana.”

—Ramayana 3.44.27

The sheer number of these passages should be sufficient to establish that Rama ate meat, but only within the scripturally prescribed contexts of hunting and sacrifice. Several objections are nevertheless raised.

Objections

I. Objection: Mamsa means the pulp of a fruit. This meaning appears in many dictionaries and is advanced by the commentator Shivasahaya.

Answer. Even in English, the word “meat” can mean the edible part of a nut or fruit. But when a passage states that someone hunted particular animals and ate the meat, the context makes clear that the meat belongs to the animals named in the same sentence, not to an unmentioned fruit.

In addition to the context of hunting or sacrifice, words such as bubhukshitau (“hungry”) show that hunting was undertaken for food, while medhyam identifies the result as sacrificially fit.

Moreover, meat is prohibited in the first place because of ahimsa. To admit that Rama hunted or sacrificed animals, but deny that he ate the meat, defeats the moral purpose of the argument: hunting purely for sport and wasting the animal would be worse than hunting and eating it. Sacrificial meat—including, in this framework, hunted meat—is havis or prasada, and wasting it is sinful.

Finally, Shivasahaya is the only commentator who denies that Rama ate meat. Govindaraja, Amrtakataka, Nageshabhatta, and the other commentators agree that he did. Shivasahaya is therefore an extreme outlier. Consider their explanations of Ramayana 2.52.102:

Show Sanskrit

तौ तत्र हत्वा चतुरो महामृगान् वराहमृश्यं पृषतं महारुरुम्। आदाय मेध्यं त्वरितं बुभुक्षितौ वासाय काले ययतुर्वनस्पतिम्॥

tau tatra hatvaa chaturo mahaamrigaan varaahamrishyam prishatam mahaarurum | aadaaya medhyam tvaritam bubhukshitau vaasaaya kaale yayaturvanaspatim ||

Govindaraja:

Show Sanskrit

मेध्यमिति ऋश्यादिसर्वविशेषणम्।

medhyamiti rishyaadisarvavisheshanam |

“Medhyam qualifies the deer and the other animals.”

Show Sanskrit

बुभुक्षितौ तेषां मेध्यं मांसं त्वरितमादायेति। बुभुक्षावशात् केवलमग्नितापमात्रं कृत्वा स्वीकृत्येत्यर्थः। उक्ताहारस्य वन्याहारान्तर्गतत्वात् ग्रहः। “देवान् पितॄन् समभ्यर्च्य खादन् मांसं न दोषभाक्” इति शास्त्रात्।

bubhukshitau teshaam medhyam maamsam tvaritamaadaayeti | bubhukshaavashaat kevalamagnitaapamaatram kritvaa sviikrityetyarthah | uktaahaarasya vanyaahaaraantargatatvaat grahah | "devaan pitriin samabhyarchya khaadan maamsam na doshabhaak" iti shaastraat |

“Being hungry, the two quickly took the medhya meat. Because of their hunger, they accepted it after only lightly roasting it over the fire. Deer meat is included among foods fit to be eaten in the forest, as scripture states: ‘One incurs no fault by eating meat after honouring the deities and ancestors.’”

—Amrtakataka:

Show Sanskrit

मांसस्य च वन्याहारान्तर्गतत्वान्न दोषः मृगयाधर्मकत्वाच्च न मृगहनने दोषः। बुभुक्षितौ हत्वा तानादाय गतावित्यनेन भोजनमर्थसिद्धमेवेति।

maamsasya cha vanyaahaaraantargatatvaanna doshah mrigayaadharmakatvaachcha na mrigahanane doshah | bubhukshitau hatvaa taanaadaaya gataavityanena bhojanamarthasiddhameveti |

“Because deer meat is included within forest food, there is no fault in eating it. Nor is there fault in killing deer, since hunting is the dharma of kshatriyas. The statement that, being hungry, they killed the animals and took them away necessarily implies that they ate them.”

—Nageshabhatta:

Show Sanskrit

वराहादीन् चतुरो महामृगान् हत्वा खेलनार्थं सन्ताड्य बुभुक्षितौ तौ रामलक्ष्मणौ मेध्यं व्रतिभिरभोक्तव्यं फलादिकमित्यर्थः।

varaahaadiin chaturo mahaamrigaan hatvaa khelanaartham santaadya bubhukshitau tau raamalakshmanau medhyam vratibhirabhoktavyam phalaadikamityarthah |

“Having ‘killed’—that is, playfully struck—the four great animals beginning with the boar, the hungry Rama and Lakshmana took medhyam, meaning fruits and similar foods fit to be eaten by those observing a vow.”

—Shivasahaya:

This comparison shows that Shivasahaya’s interpretations of hatva as “playfully struck” and medhyam as “fruit” are unorthodox and contrary to the consensus of the commentators.

II. Objection: Rama promised not to eat meat.

Show Sanskrit

चतुर्दश हि वर्षाणि वत्स्यामि विजने वने। मधुमूलफलैर्जीवन् हित्वा मुनिवदामिषम्॥

chaturdasha hi varshaani vatsyaami vijane vane | madhumuulaphalairjiivan hitvaa munivadaamisham ||

Rama said: “I shall dwell in the secluded forest for fourteen years, living on honey, roots, and fruit, and giving up amisha like a sage.”

—Ramayana 2.20.29

Answer. Here amisha does not mean meat, but the pleasures of royal life. Every commentator, including Shivasahaya—the strongest proponent of the view that Rama did not eat meat—explains it this way.

Show Sanskrit

आमिषं राजभोग्यं वस्तु। “आमिषं पुनपुंसकम्। भोग्यवस्तुनि सम्भोगे” इति मेदिनी।

aamisham raajabhogyam vastu | "aamisham punapumsakam | bhogyavastuni sambhoge" iti medinii |

“Amisha means an object of royal enjoyment, as stated in the Medini dictionary.”

—Amrtakataka:

Show Sanskrit

आमिषं राजभोग्यं वस्तु हित्वा त्यक्त्वा। आकर्षणेऽपि रूढिः स्यादामिषम्।

aamisham raajabhogyam vastu hitvaa tyaktvaa | aakarshane'pi ruudhih syaadaamisham |

“I shall give up amisha, meaning objects of royal pleasure. Amisha may also mean an attraction.”

—Shivasahaya:

Shivasahaya may have avoided taking amisha to mean meat because such a reading would harm his own position. If Rama promised to give up meat during exile alongside the other comforts he was renouncing, that would imply that he had previously eaten it.

Even if one interprets amisha as meat, other commentators explain that it refers specifically to meat prepared by royal cooks.

Show Sanskrit

मुनिवदामिषं सूदैर्विशिष्टसंस्कारसंस्कृतं मांसम्। तेन “इदं मेध्यमिदं स्वादु निष्टप्तमिदमग्निना” इति वक्ष्यमाणेन न विरोधः।

munivadaamisham suudairvishishtasamskaarasamskritam maamsam | tena "idam medhyamidam svaadu nishtaptamidamagninaa" iti vakshyamaanena na virodhah |

“‘Giving up amisha like a sage’ refers specifically to meat prepared and specially consecrated by royal cooks. It therefore does not contradict the later passage, ‘This is sacrificial; this is tasty; this is roasted over the fire.’”

—Nageshabhatta:

Show Sanskrit

अत्रामिषशब्देन सूदैः संस्कृतं मांसमुच्यते। केवलं मांसस्वीकारस्योत्तरत्र वक्ष्यमाणत्वात् “इदं मेध्यमिदं स्वादु निष्टप्तमिदमग्निना” इति।

atraamishashabdena suudaih samskritam maamsamuchyate | kevalam maamsasviikaarasyottaratra vakshyamaanatvaat "idam medhyamidam svaadu nishtaptamidamagninaa" iti |

“Here amisha means meat prepared by royal cooks. It cannot mean meat in general, because the later passage says, ‘This is sacrificial; this is tasty; this is roasted over the fire.’”

—Govindaraja:

III. Objection: Hanuman says that Rama does not eat meat.

Show Sanskrit

न मांसं राघवो भुङ्क्ते न चापि मधु सेवते। वन्यं सुविहितं नित्यं भक्तमश्नाति पञ्चमम्॥

na maamsam raaghavo bhungkte na chaapi madhu sevate | vanyam suvihitam nityam bhaktamashnaati panchamam ||

“Rama is not eating meat, nor is he drinking honey. He eats only what is available in the forest, and even that amounts to one fifth of his usual meal.”

—Ramayana 5.36.41

Answer. This is not a description of Rama’s normal habits. The word pancamam—one fifth of a meal—already indicates an abnormal state, and the rest of the passage makes the reason explicit:

Show Sanskrit

न दंशान्न मशकान्न कीटान्न सरीसृपान्। राघवोऽपनयेद्गात्रात् त्वद्गतेनान्तरात्मना॥

na damshaanna mashakaanna kiitaanna sariisripaan | raaghavo'panayedgaatraat tvadgatenaantaraatmanaa ||

“Rama does not even drive flies, mosquitoes, insects, or reptiles from his body, because his heart is fixed upon you.”

Hanuman tells Sita that Rama is behaving this way out of grief at their separation. It is not his usual state—unless one supposes that he always ate only one fifth of a meal and habitually allowed mosquitoes and snakes to remain on his body.

Indeed, this strengthens the argument that Rama did ordinarily eat meat, because Hanuman specifically mentions that he has ceased eating it as an expression of grief. Why mention abstention from meat alongside these other unusual acts if he had never eaten meat at all?

Show Sanskrit

दुष्यन्तेन करूशेन रामालर्कनलैस्तथा। विचकाश्वेन निमिना जनकेन च धीमता॥
एतैश्चान्यैश्च राजेन्द्र पुरा मांसं न भक्षितम्॥

dushyantena karuushena raamaalarkanalaistathaa | vichakaashvena niminaa janakena cha dhiimataa ||

etaishchaanyaishcha raajendra puraa maamsam na bhakshitam ||

“Dushyanta, Karusha, Rama, Alarka, Nala, Vicakashva, Nimi, the wise Janaka, and others, O king, did not eat meat in ancient times.”

—IV. Objection: Mahabharata 13.116 lists Rama among people who never ate meat.

Answer. Nothing in the passage indicates that the Rama named here is Rama, son of Dasharatha. Given the unanimous evidence of the Ramayana itself, the verse may refer to another Rama. The Mahabharata mentions at least three figures of that name: Rama, son of Jamadagni; Rama, son of Vasudeva; and Rama, son of Dasharatha. It was an extremely common name.

V. Objection: These passages are interpolations.

Answer. They are not: all these passages occur in the Critical Edition of the Ramayana.
