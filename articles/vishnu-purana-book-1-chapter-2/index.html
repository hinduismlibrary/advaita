VP 1.2.1

अविकाराय शुद्धाय नित्याय परमात्मने।
सदैकरूपरूपाय विष्णवे सर्वजिष्णवे

Glory to the unchangeable, holy, eternal, supreme Viṣṇu, of one universal nature, the mighty over all:

Śrīdhara translation

After saluting Viṣṇu, whose higher and lower forms are the subject of this Purāṇa, the meaning is explained at length, beginning with nine verses headed by “avikārāya.” At the end of the preceding chapter, the Purāṇa’s meaning was stated in brief, including Viṣṇu’s being the material and efficient cause of the world; this could suggest that he undergoes change, so that implication is removed here. In ordinary experience an agent is changeable because he is the locus of action, and is associated with attachment and other conditions; the words “avikārāya” and the following terms remove those implications. A material cause is also seen to take many forms through transformation, as clay or gold becomes pots and ornaments; “sadaikarūparūpāya” removes that implication, meaning “to him whose form is always one.” “Sada” is to be understood with all the terms. “Sarvajiṣṇu” means one who, through inconceivable sovereignty, surpasses all agents, causes, and the rest. To such Viṣṇu the salutation is offered.

VP 1.2.2

नमो हिरण्यगर्भाय हरये शङ्कराय च।
वासुदेवाय ताराय सर्गस्थित्य् अन्तकारिणी

to him who is Hiraṇyagarbha, Hari, and Śaṅkara, the creator, the preserver, and destroyer of the world:

Śrīdhara translation

Vāsudeva is saluted in the three forms beginning with Hiraṇyagarbha—Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śaṅkara—which perform creation, preservation, and dissolution. Having set worldly existence in motion, Vāsudeva is now saluted as the one who brings it to an end. “Tārāya” means “to the deliverer,” the one who carries devotees across saṃsāra.

VP 1.2.3

एकानेक-स्वरूपाय स्थूल-सूक्ष्मात्मने नमः।
अव्यक्त-व्यक्त-रूपाय विष्णवे मुक्ति-हेतवे

to Vāsudeva, the liberator of his worshippers: to him, whose essence is both single and manifold; who is both subtile and corporeal, indiscrete and discrete: to Viṣṇu, the cause of final emancipation,

Śrīdhara translation

He has one and many forms, and his self is gross and subtle. How can two opposite forms belong to one? The answer is “avyakta-vyakta-rūpāya”: the unmanifest is the cause, the manifest is the effect, and Viṣṇu has both forms. Thus he is one in unmanifest form and many in manifest form; as the Self of all, he is the cause of liberation for worshippers.

VP 1.2.4

सर्ग-स्थिति-विनाशानां जगतो यो जगन्-मयः।
मूल-भूतो नमस् तस्मै विष्णवे परमात्मने

Glory to the supreme Viṣṇu, the cause of the creation, existence, and end of this world; who is the root of the world, and who consists of the world.

Śrīdhara translation

Viṣṇu is both cause and effect. He is the root and essence of the world’s creation, preservation, and destruction; therefore he is all-pervading and is the Supreme Self, transcendent through both cause and effect and untouched by their limitations.

VP 1.2.5

Śrīdhara translation

The Supreme is free from the modifications of being, beginning with decline and destruction. The cited śruti says, in effect, “He becomes as though non-existent if one knows Brahman as non-existent; but if one knows Brahman as existent, they know him as existent,” and another passage says that he is to be apprehended as existence alone. This might seem to introduce the second modification of being, but “he can be described as always existing” means that his existence is not occasional; it is always present and so is not a modification produced by birth.

VP 1.2.16

प्रधान-पुरुष-व्यक्त-कालानां परमं हि यत्।
पश्यन्ति सूरयः शुद्धं तद् विष्णोः परमं पदम्

These four—Pradhāna (primary or crude matter), Puruṣa (spirit), Vyakta (visible substance), and Kāla (time)—the wise consider to be the pure and supreme condition of Viṣṇu.

Śrīdhara translation

The statement that Pradhāna, Puruṣa, Vyakta, and Kāla are forms of Brahman might suggest that they are effects to be accepted as ultimate objects. That implication is avoided: the wise behold the pure supreme station of Viṣṇu itself, the highest reality, not a dependent product among those four.

VP 1.2.23

नाहं न रात्रिर् न नभो न भूमिर् नासीत् तमोज्योतिर् अभूच् च नान्यत्।
श्रोत्रादि-बुद्ध्यानुपलभ्यम् एकं प्राधानिकं ब्रह्म पुमांस् तदासीत्

“There was neither day nor night, nor sky nor earth, nor darkness nor light, nor any other thing, save only One, unapprehensible by intellect, or That which is Brahma and Pumān (spirit) and Pradhāna (matter).”

Śrīdhara translation

At that time there was neither day nor night because the sun and the other illuminators were absent; there was no darkness because night was absent, and no light because day was absent. Nothing else existed because sound and the other qualities were absent. The three alone existed at dissolution: Pradhāna, called “prādhānika,” Brahman, and Puruṣa, none apprehensible through knowledge produced by the ear and the other senses. Thus the śruti says, “Then neither the non-existent nor the existent was present,” and so on.

VP 1.2.24

विष्णोः स्वरूपात् परतो हि तेऽन्ये रूपं प्रधानं पुरुषश् च विप्र।
तस्यैव तेन्ऽन्येन धृते वियुक्ते रूपान्तरं तद् द्विज काल-सञ्ज्ञम्

The two forms which are other than the essence of unmodified Viṣṇu, are Pradhāna (matter) and Puruṣa (spirit); and his other form, by which those two are connected or separated, is called Kāla (time).

Śrīdhara translation

The form called Time is explained in four verses beginning “viṣṇoḥ.” Pradhāna and Puruṣa are two distinct forms other than the unconditioned form of Viṣṇu, produced through Māyā. Through another form of that same Viṣṇu they are held together at creation and separated at dissolution. Alternatively, at dissolution they are established in their separated condition; if they were never separated, dissolution could not occur. That other form is called Time.

The distinction between creator and created, preserver and preserved, and destroyer and destroyed is not ultimately real. Viṣṇu alone is the creator and creates himself; he is both the preserver and the preserved, and both the withdrawer and what is withdrawn.

VP 1.2.67

स एव सर्व-भूतात्मा विश्व-रूपो यतोऽव्ययः।
सर्गादिकं तु तस्यैव भूत-स्थम् उपकारकम्

that also is the lord of all elements, the universal form, and imperishable: hence he is the cause of creation, preservation, and destruction, and the subject of the vicissitudes inherent in elementary nature.

Śrīdhara translation

If Viṣṇu alone is said to perform creation and the rest, what of the apparent creators and preservers such as fathers? He is the Self of all beings, their ruler and impeller, and the universal form. Therefore the creation and the rest that occur in beings are an auxiliary service of Hari, expanding through his own manifestations—just as an act performed by priests is an auxiliary service of the sacrificer.

VP 1.2.68

स एव सृज्यः स च सर्ग-कर्ता स एव पात्य् अत्ति च पाल्यते च।
ब्रह्माद्य्-अवस्थाभिर् अशेष-मूर्तिर् विष्णुर् वरिष्ठो वरदो वरेण्यः

He is the object and author of creation: he preserves, destroys, and is preserved. He, Viṣṇu, as Brahmā, and as all other beings, is infinite form: he is the supreme, the giver of all good, the fountain of all happiness.

Śrīdhara translation

The unity of creator, created, and the rest is thus established. Viṣṇu alone is the created and the creator; through the states beginning with Brahmā he is the agent of creation and the other cosmic functions, and his form includes all. Thus Viṣṇu alone is supreme—the highest reality—and he is the giver of boons and worthy of choice because he is the form of supreme bliss.
