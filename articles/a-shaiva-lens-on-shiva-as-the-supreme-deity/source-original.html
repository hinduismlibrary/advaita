Preface

Introduction

The question of which deity constitutes the supreme Brahman, the ultimate ground of all existence, has animated Hindu philosophical debate for millennia. The preceding Hari-Tattva-Vimarsha presents a careful & learnt Vaishnava case. This response, composed in the spirit of ancient scholarly debate (vada), presents the Shaiva case: that it is Lord Shiva: specifically Sadshiva or Maheshvara, who is the ultimate transcendental ground of all existence, including that of Vishnu himself as a functional manifestation. This paper does not deny the greatness of the Param Shaiva Bhagavan Vishnu, nor is it an attack on the Vaishnava tradition. The purpose is to demonstrate through the same scriptures & methodology, the scriptural corpus, read without sectarian presupposition, that points to Shiva as the Para-Tattva.

Methodology

We accept the same hierarchy of scriptural authority, Shruti, including the Vedas & Upanishads, which take precedence, followed by Smriti such as Itihasa, Purana and Dharmashastra. We commit to the Mimamsa principle of samanvaya, reconciliation, and will not dismiss any pramana as interpolation for the sake of convenience. However, we do dispute one foundational element of the opposing framework before all else: the Guna-based classification of the Puranas. We shall demonstrate this classification as self-contradictory, circular in its application and cannot serve as a neutral between the traditions of Vaishnavism and Shaivism.

On Guṇa-Based Purāṇa Classification

The Hari-Paratva-Vimarsha’s most consequential meta-argument is the classification of Puranas by guna, establishing the Vaishnava Puranas as Sattvika, and the Shaiva Puranas as Tamasa. This classification does enormous work in the document, and pre-emptively discredits any statements made in Shaiva Puranas before any verse is considered from a Shaiva source. Supremacy of Śiva in Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas

In the Padma Puran only, which classifies the Shaiva Puranas as “Tamasic”, in the 33rd Chapter of Svarga Khanda says, in verses 57-59, Narad Muni establishes Shiva’s supremacy. यथानारायणः श्रेष्ठो देवाना ं परुु षोत्तमः । यथेश्वराणा ं ग रशः स्थानानामेतदत्तु मम ् ॥ ५८ ॥

Just as Nārāyaṇa, the Puruṣottama, is the greatest among the Devas, and just as Giriśa (Śiva) is the greatest among the lords (Īśvaras), so is this place (Avimukta) the highest among all sacred places. Note that the identity of Narayan is tied to that of a Devata, while Shiva is identified as Ishvara. Pūrva Mīmāṃsā Sūtras (specifically the Devatā-adhikaraṇa, 9.1.6–10), Maharṣi Jaimini and the commentator Śabara Svāmī define a deity strictly through the lens of ritual grammar. ● The Rule: Tyāgoddheśyatvaṁ devatātvam (त्यागोद्देश्यत्व ं देवतात्वम)् . ● The Meaning: "Deity-hood consists solely in being the intended recipient of a ritual relinquishment." Mīmāṃsā strips deities of physical forms (vigraha) or personal, independent wealth in the empirical sense. Instead, a Devatā is a functional, structural placeholder within a sacrifice (Yajña). They are defined entirely by the fact that a sacrificial offering (Havis) is poured into the fire for them while their name is uttered in the dative case (e.g., Agnaye svāhā—"To Agni, svāhā!"). Thus, a Devatā is inherently bound to a cooperative, cosmic-ritual economy.

In contrast, the word Īśvara does not originate from a ritual-functional category, but from an intrinsic, verbal action in Sanskrit grammar. ● The Root: It is derived from the Pāṇinian Dhātu īś (īśa aiśvarye — ईश ऐश्वय ). ● The Meaning: "To possess absolute lordship, sovereignty, or to rule unconditionally." An Īśvara is an independent master who commands, governs, and exercises unchecked autonomy. While a Devatā’s power is relational and structural (dependent on the Veda, the mantra, and the ritual space), an Īśvara's power is ontological and absolute. In Srimad Bhagavat Puran, the core Vaishnava text of Vaishnavism, Shiva is described as Supreme again: श्मशानचक्रा नलध ू लधम्रू - वकीण वद्योतजटाकलाप: । भस्मावगुण्ठामलरुक्म देहो देविस्त्र भ: पश्य त देवरस्त े ॥ २५ ॥ न यस्य लोके स्वजन: परो वा नात्यादत ृ ो नोत किश्चद् वगह्य: ।

वय ं व्रतयै च् चरणाप वद्धा- माशास्महेऽजा ं बत भक्ु तभोगाम ् ॥ २६ ॥ “Your brother-in-law, God Rudra, whose tawny matted hair is scattered by the winds of the cremation ground, and whose golden body is smeared with ashes--sees everything with his three eyes (the Sun, Moon, and Fire). Double Standards on Relative & Absolute Supremacy

Kṛṣṇa on Śiva's Supremacy in Mahābhārata

Even setting aside the classification framework, the Hari-Paratva-Vimarsha applies a revealing double standard in its interpretation of passages. This methodological inconsistency runs through the entire document and must be named explicitly. When Vedic Brahmana passages praise Vishnu — such as the Shatapatha Brahmana's 'Agni is lowest, Vishnu is highest' — the opposing paper reads these as statements of ontological supremacy. When identical language is used for Rudra — such as the Shvetashvatara's 'Eko hi Rudro na dvitiyaya tasthe' (Rudra is one, there is no second to him) — these are suddenly reinterpreted as relative superiority within a domain. The Shvetashvatara is a PRINCIPAL Upanishad in the jnana-kanda — the section the opposing paper itself designates as 'the actual doctrinally decisive section of the Vedas.' The Shatapatha Brahmana is karma-kanda — the ritual section the same paper admits is 'doctrinally indecisive.' On the opposing paper's own methodology, the Shvetashvatara should outrank the Shatapatha Brahmana. Yet the hermeneutic works in the opposite direction.

The Hari-Paratva-Vimarsha uses a sophisticated move to resolve Vishnu's worship of Shiva: Vishnu, as the antaryamin of Rudra, worships himself when he appears to worship Shiva. This is structurally identical to what Shaiva acharyas say about Vishnu — that he is Sadashiva's Sattvika manifestation, and Sadashiva is his antaratman. The opposing paper itself cites (from the Brahma Samhita) the milk-and-curd analogy: just as milk becomes curd but not vice versa, Vishnu enters Rudra but Rudra is not the source of Vishnu. The Shaiva inverts this: Sadashiva (milk) manifests as the Sattvika Vishnu (curd). Which came first — milk or curd? The Shvetashvatara Upanishad and the Shiva Purana are unambiguous: Sadashiva is the source. Evidence from Śruti on the Supremacy of Śiva

Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad & Ādi Śaṅkarācārya on the Supremacy of Śiva

“Hara” as the Destroyer of the Root of Bondage

The Hari-Paratva-Vimarsha relies heavily on the argument that Vishnu is the Antaryamin (indwelling soul) of Shiva, claiming that "Rudra is not actually the supreme lord" but merely a vessel for Vishnu. However, in his commentary on Verse 1.12 of Shvestahsvara Upanishad, Adi Shankaracharya identifies the "Impeller" (Prerita)—the indwelling ruler of both the individual soul (Bhokta) and the material world (Bhogya)—specifically as the Parameshvara described in this Upanishad. Since the Shvetashvatara identifies this Lord as Rudra (Verse 3.2), the logic of indwelling is flipped: Shiva is the Impeller (Prerita) and Indweller (Antaryamin) of Vishnu. If Vishnu is the "Sattvika manifestation" used for preservation, he is part of the "triad" (Trividham) that is impelled by the supreme Prerita (Shiva).Vishnu is "Tamasic internally" because he is "deluded" into sleep on Shesha, whereas Shiva is the "Sattvic internal" instructor of yogis who "never sleeps".

By identifying Rudra/Shiva as the Prerita (the Ruler and source of the triad), Shankara confirms that the term "Brahman" refers primarily and factually to Shiva. As Krishna himself admits in the Mahabharata, he would rather be an insect by Shiva's command than rule the three worlds by any other's favor, because "there is no higher essence" than the one who is the "primeval cause”.

The Vaiṣṇava Claim: “Relative Superiority” and “Courtesy”

Hari-Paratva-Vimarsha argues that Vedic praises of Rudra, such as "Rudra is one, there is no second to him," must be interpreted as "relative superiority" within a specific domain, similar to praises given to Indra. It further asserts that titles like "Brahman" and "Bhagavan" primarily and factually refer only to Vishnu, while their application to Shiva is a mere "secondary courtesy" or for the purpose of worship. Finally, it claims that the core scholarly work of Adi Shankaracharya is "unequivocally Vaishnava" at the pragmatic (Vyavaharika) level. While the Hari Paratva Vimarsha uses ritualistic Karma-kanda texts (like the Shatapatha Brahmana) to claim Vishnu's "absolute" supremacy, they dismiss the Shvetashvatara Upanishad—a Principal Upanishad in the doctrinally decisive Jnana-kanda—as "relative". On the Vaishnava's own methodological grounds, a principal Upanishad must outrank ritual sections. The Bṛhaddharma Purāṇa (Pūrva 27.25) and the Śaktisaṅgama Tantra explicitly reveal that Lord Śiva took a physical form in Kali Yuga as Adi Śaṅkarācārya to preserve the Vaidika Dharma. Therefore, when analyzing the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad Bhāṣya, we are not merely reading the notes of a human scholar; we are witnessing the transcendent Lord Śiva, operating through his Avataric intellect, defining Himself as the ultimate Paramārtha-Tattva.

On Supposed Supremacy of Vishnu नारायणः परं ब्रह्म तत्त्व ं नारायणः परः । नारायणः परो ज्यो तरात्मा नारायणः परः ॥ यच्च किञ्चज्जगत्यिस्मन ् दृश्यत े श्रूयतऽे प वा । अन्तब हश्च तत्सव व्याप्य नारायणः िस्थतः ॥ English Translation: "Narayana is the Supreme Brahman; Narayana is the Supreme Reality. Narayana is the Supreme Light; Narayana is the Supreme Self. Whatever there is in this world that is seen or heard, Narayana stands pervading all of that, both internally and externally." In Sanskrit grammar, proper nouns can be interpreted either as fixed names (Rūḍhi) or by their root etymological meanings (Yauga). Shaiva scholars argue that in the highest Vedantic contexts, words like "Narayana" describe the qualities of the absolute consciousness, not a specific deity with a discus and conch. Nāra means the collection of all souls/entities, and Ayana means the resting place or refuge. Therefore, Nārāyaṇa literally means "The Ultimate Refuge of all living beings." Who is the ultimate resting place of the universe during cosmic dissolution (Pralaya)?

The Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad explicitly states that during dissolution, only Śiva exists as the absolute substratum. Thus, "Narayana" here is an epithet describing the all-pervasive, restful nature of Lord Śiva, not Lord Vishnu. the Nārāyaṇa Sūktam does not exist in a vacuum; it is part of the larger Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad. Just a few sections later in the exact same text, the Upanishad explicitly identifies this Supreme Reality with names uniquely reserved for Shiva: "ऋतं सत्य ं परं ब्रह्म परुु ष ं कृ ष्ण पङ्गलम ् । ऊध्वर ेतं वरूपाक्ष ं वश्वरूपाय व ै नमो नमः ॥" (Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad 12.1) This verse defines the Supreme Brahman as Virūpākṣa (the Three-Eyed One) and Kṛṣṇapiṅgalam (the dark and tawny-colored ascetic). These physical attributes belong exclusively to Lord Śiva. Therefore, the text itself clarifies that the all-pervading "Narayana" consciousness is identical to Mahādeva.
