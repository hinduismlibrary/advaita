Riddles in Hinduism

An on-site reading edition of B. R. Ambedkar’s work

This page now presents every riddle and appendix directly on this website. It follows the arrangement of Dr. Babasaheb Ambedkar: Writings and Speeches, Volume 4, and replaces the former embedded Internet Archive reader.

The writing below is an original section-by-section guide to Ambedkar’s arguments. Several pieces survive as working drafts or unfinished manuscripts. The official volume remains the authority for his exact wording, notes and pagination.

Part I — Religious

1. The Difficulty of Knowing Why One Is a Hindu

Ambedkar begins by asking what positive set of beliefs defines a Hindu. He compares the diversity of doctrines, deities, rituals and philosophical positions gathered under the same name and argues that ordinary labels do not provide a clear test of religious identity.

2. The Origin of the Vedas

Ambedkar assembles several traditional accounts of the Vedas’ origin. Different texts attribute them to different beings, sacrifices and cosmic processes; he argues that multiplying mythic explanations does not produce a coherent account of authorship.

3. The Testimony of Other Shastras

The inquiry is widened to other Shastras. Their testimony varies in detail and theology, and Ambedkar uses the comparison to challenge the claim that the scriptural tradition speaks with one uncontested voice about the Vedas.

4. Why Were the Vedas Declared Infallible?

Ambedkar asks when and why the Vedas came to be placed beyond criticism, particularly when earlier traditions preserve disagreement and reinterpretation. Infallibility is treated as a developed claim with intellectual and social consequences.

5. Neither Made by Man nor God

This chapter examines the doctrine that the Vedas are apauruṣeya, without a human author. Ambedkar asks why this stronger claim became necessary and argues that authorlessness shields a text from questions about intention, error and responsibility.

6. The Contents of the Vedas

Attention shifts from the status of the Vedas to their contents. Ambedkar surveys hymns, charms, prayers and ritual materials and insists that moral or spiritual worth must be established from what a text teaches, not from its antiquity or designation as sacred.

7. The Turn of the Tide

Ambedkar traces how practical authority often moved from the Vedas toward later texts, customs and sectarian scriptures. The riddle is how the supposedly supreme revelation could become less decisive than authorities formally ranked below it.

8. The Upanishads Declare War on the Vedas

The Upanishads are presented as an intellectual challenge to Vedic ritualism. Ambedkar contrasts inward knowledge and metaphysical inquiry with sacrifice and priestly ritual, emphasizing conflict before later attempts at harmonisation.

9. The Upanishads Made Subordinate

After treating the Upanishads as critical of ritualism, Ambedkar asks how they were incorporated into the Vedic canon. A challenge could be neutralised by being reclassified as the concluding portion of the authority it had opposed.

10. Why Did the Hindu Gods Fight?

Ambedkar surveys narratives in which major gods and their adherents compete for supremacy. Different sectarian texts elevate one deity while diminishing another, and he reads the divine rivalries as reflections of competition among cults and traditions.

11. Why Did the Gods Rise and Fall?

Deities once central become subordinate while later gods acquire new myths and cosmic status. Ambedkar uses these reversals as evidence that Hindu theology underwent substantial historical transformation.

12. Gods Dethroned, Goddesses Enthroned

This riddle examines the rise of powerful goddess traditions and compares them with earlier divine hierarchies. Ambedkar presents the change as a major development that demands historical explanation rather than being assumed timeless.

13. The Riddle of Ahimsa

Ambedkar investigates the place of non-violence in Hindu tradition and questions the claim that it has always been the governing ethic. He compares doctrine with sacrifice, social rules and historical change.

14. From Ahimsa Back to Himsa

The chapter examines how non-violence was qualified or reversed in specific religious and social contexts. Ambedkar highlights the difference between praising an ethical principle and applying it consistently.

15. An Ahimsak God and a Bloodthirsty Goddess

Ambedkar considers theological pairings that combine sharply different ritual and ethical traditions. The chapter explores how distinct cults were synthesised while their contradictions remained visible.

Part I — Appendices

Appendix I. The Riddle of the Vedas

Additional material on Vedic origin, authority and interpretation reinforces the argument that the tradition contains several competing explanations. The working form of the manuscript is preserved in the collected edition.

Appendix II. The Riddle of the Vedanta

Ambedkar examines claims about ultimate reality, the self and liberation, asking whether metaphysical unity can coexist coherently with social hierarchy and exclusion.

Appendix III. The Riddle of the Trimurti

The idea of Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva as a coordinated triad is compared with sectarian texts that repeatedly proclaim one member supreme. Ambedkar presents the Trimurti as an attempted synthesis that never fully removes rivalry.

Appendix IV. Smarta Dharma and Tantrik Dharma

Ambedkar contrasts orthodox household practice with Tantric traditions, focusing on differences of ritual, initiation and authority. The contrast demonstrates how widely differing systems are placed under the single name Hinduism.

Appendix V. The Infallibility of the Vedas

This appendix returns to philosophical defences of Vedic eternality, authorlessness and freedom from error, testing those claims against internal disagreement and changing interpretation.

Part II — Social

16. The Four Varnas

Ambedkar compares competing scriptural accounts of the four varnas’ origin. Variations in genealogy, symbolism and status challenge the certainty claimed for a divinely fixed hereditary order.

17. The Four Ashramas

The stages of student, householder, forest-dweller and renunciant are examined historically. Ambedkar asks how they became an ordered sequence and who was permitted to enter each stage.

18. Manu’s Explanation of Mixed Castes

Ambedkar analyses Manu’s elaborate derivation of mixed castes from unions across varna lines. He argues that the taxonomy is internally strained and rationalises an existing system of graded social prejudice.

19. From Paternity to Maternity

Changing rules for determining a child’s status through the father or mother are connected to caste reproduction, marriage and social control. Ambedkar asks what interests were served by each change.

20. Kali Varjya

Practices once permitted were later declared unsuitable for the Kali age. Ambedkar treats this as a method of changing religious practice while avoiding an open admission that earlier rules had been revised.

Part II — Appendices

Appendix I. The Riddle of Varnashrama Dharma

Varna and ashrama are examined as one social-religious system. Ambedkar tests claims of harmony against the graded rights, exclusions and unequal obligations built into the structure.

Appendix II. Compulsory Matrimony

Marriage is presented not merely as private choice but as an institution maintaining caste continuity, inheritance and prescribed domestic roles, with unequal consequences for men and women.

Part III — Political

21. The Theory of Manvantara

Ambedkar examines the cyclical reigns of successive Manus and asks what explanatory or political function is served by locating law and social order in vast sacred cycles beyond ordinary history.

22. Brahma Is Not Dharma

Metaphysical speculation about ultimate reality is distinguished from the practical demands of morality and social justice. Ambedkar asks whether spiritual insight has value when oppressive institutions remain untouched.

23. Why Was Kali Yuga Made Unending?

Ambedkar investigates the duration of the Kali Yuga and the effect of portraying society as trapped in an extraordinarily long age of decline. Such chronology can encourage resignation and postpone reform.

24. The Riddle of the Kali Yuga

Further accounts of the Kali age are compared for their characteristics, duration and doctrinal use. The section asks whether mythic time explains injustice or merely removes it from historical criticism.

Part III — Appendix

Appendix I. The Riddle of Rama and Krishna

Ambedkar critically examines episodes associated with Rama and Krishna as moral and political exemplars. His central demand is that revered figures remain open to ethical scrutiny rather than being exempted by claims of divinity.
