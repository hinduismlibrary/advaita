MMK 3.9

It should be understood that seeing explains hearing and smelling and tasting and touching, mind, hearer, what is heard, etc.

Original Sanskrit (GRETIL)

vyākhyātaṃ śravaṇaṃ ghrāṇaṃ rasanaṃ sparśanaṃ manaḥ | darśanenaiva jānīyācchrotṛśrotavyakādi ca || 9 ||

Candrakīrti’s Commentary

Candrakīrti extends the analysis from seeing to the remaining sense faculties: hearing, smelling, tasting, touching, and mind, together with the hearer, what is heard, and the corresponding objects. They should be understood through the same examination used for seeing and the seer.

Candrakīrti cites the Buddha: “The eye does not perceive form, and the mind does not know mental objects”—this is the highest truth, where ordinary people do not settle. When seeing is disclosed through a collection of conditions, the wise understand that as the conventional domain through which ultimate truth is spoken of.

He then cites verses saying that eye-consciousness arises in dependence on the eye and form, but form does not reside in the eye and does not pass into it. Phenomena are without self and impure, though self and purity are imagined in them; from such inverted, false conception eye-consciousness arises. The yogin sees the arising and cessation of consciousness and its conditions, and sees them as empty and illusion-like—not as having gone somewhere or arrived from elsewhere.

In the *Ācāryopālipṛcchā*, the eye sees only in conjunction with conditions; without conditions it does not see. The eye does not itself inspect form, so distinctions of conjunction and separation are conceptual constructions. With the support of light, the eye sees beautiful and varied forms; when that support is absent, seeing is not found. A pleasing sound that is heard does not enter inside, and no transfer of sound can be found: the sound is raised up through conceptual construction.

A final cited verse says that songs, dances, and instrumental sounds are not graspable as stable things; pleasures are dreamlike and delude the unwise. Those who chase them through eager imagining come to ruin. The speaker asks why he should become like a child, a servant of affliction. Candrakīrti then closes the third chapter, “Examination of the Eye and Other Faculties.”
