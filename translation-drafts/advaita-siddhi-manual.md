सत्यज्ञानसुखात्मकः श्रुतिशिखोत्थाखण्डधीगोचरः ।  
मिथ्याबन्धविधूननेन परमानन्दैकरतानात्मकं  
मोक्षं प्राप्त इव स्वयं विजयते विष्णुर्विकल्पोज्झितः ॥

**IAST**

māyākalpitamātṛtāmukhamṛṣādvaitaprapañcāśrayaḥ  
satyajñānasukhātmakaḥ śrutiśikhotthākhaṇḍadhīgocaraḥ |  
mithyābandhavidhūnanena paramānandaikatānātmakaṃ  
mokṣaṃ prāpta iva svayaṃ vijayate viṣṇurvikalpojjhitaḥ ||

**Translation**

May Viṣṇu, free from conceptual division, shine forth. He is the ground of the false dual world—beginning with the apparent knower—that Māyā projects. His nature is reality, awareness, and bliss; the undivided understanding born from the Upaniṣads knows him. When false bondage is swept away, he seems to attain liberation, whose nature is an unbroken fullness of supreme bliss.

I bow to the dust that rises from the feet of Śrī Rāma, Viśveśvara, and Mādhava, who directly realized Mādhava through their oneness. By contact with that dust, darkness and passion are swept away.

**Translator's note:** The verse compresses several names and relations into one compound. I have kept its reference to the unity of the named teachers visible rather than silently resolving it into a different reading.

## First pariccheda — opening argument

### Why the statement of disagreement belongs in inquiry

**Source heading:** विप्रतिपत्तिवाक्यस्य विचाराङ्गत्वम्

Since the establishment of non-duality depends on first establishing that duality is false, the falsity of duality must be the first point proved. Proof requires both establishing one's own position and refuting the opposing position. These two tasks must be carried out through one of the recognised forms of philosophical exchange: *vāda* (debate aimed at truth), *jalpa* (contentious disputation), or *vitaṇḍā* (criticism that attacks the opponent's position without establishing one's own). Because doubt arising from disagreement has a role in inquiry, a neutral moderator must begin by stating the disagreement.

That doubt, however, is not what creates *pakṣatā*, the status of a proposition as the subject of inference. The definition being discussed describes *pakṣatā* as the absence of a cognition that would establish the point, conjoined with the absence of a desire to establish it; doubt itself is not part of that definition. Otherwise, a person already certain of the Self on the authority of scripture could not infer it while wishing to do so. Here the text distinguishes the desire to prove a thesis (*sisādhayiṣā*) from the desire to infer (*anumitsā*). Nor can doubt be required on the ground that debaters are certain: if they are certain, doubt is impossible. And if one allows a doubt that they merely assume for the sake of argument, the rule would apply too broadly.

Disagreement is not needed simply to assign the opposing roles. The moderator can do that by saying, “You must prove this,” and, “He must refute that.” Since those instructions already assign the roles, stating the disagreement would be redundant for that purpose.

Even so, a doubt produced by disagreement belongs in the inquiry—not as a component of inferential cognition, but because the inquiry must remove it. In a particular case, certainty or another obstacle may prevent disagreement from producing such a doubt; nevertheless, disagreement is naturally capable of producing it. There is also no rule that disputants must actually be certain. The saying “The certain conduct a debate” refers to the certainty they claim to possess. We see boastful people enter contentious debate while taking up an opponent's position, even though they are certain of the contrary.

Therefore, just as the moderator sets the terms of a debate, a neutral moderator must state the disagreement in order to carry out that role. The disagreement about falsity is this: Is something distinct from consciousness, capable of appearing as existent, and not sublated by any valid cognition other than knowledge of Brahman, the object denied in all three times in the locus where it appears? Or is it not? Is it the object of that negation specifically when the negation is asserted from the standpoint of ultimate reality? Or is it not?

**Translator's note:** “Object of a negation” translates *pratiyogin*: the thing whose absence is being asserted. “Locus” translates *upādhi*, the basis in which something is presented. This formulation is setting up the debate; the argument for the definition continues in the following sections.

## First pariccheda — continued

### Considering what delimits the inferential subject

**Source heading:** पक्षतावच्छेदकविचारः

The passage now considers what precisely counts as the subject of an inference. If the aim is to establish the predicate as applying throughout the range fixed by the subject's delimiting condition, then, on one account, the proof is redundant even when the predicate has already been established for only part of that range. To avoid that result in the shell-and-silver example, the subject is qualified as “not sublated by any knowledge other than knowledge of Brahman.” If, however, the aim is to establish the predicate exactly within the range fixed by that delimiting condition, then proving it for a part of the range does not make the proof redundant. On that account, this extra qualification can be left out. The other two qualifications must still be retained: they prevent the subject from wrongly including sheer nonexistence or Brahman.

Alternatively, the disagreement can be stated one object at a time: “Is space unreal, or not? Is the earth unreal, or not?” If space, earth, and the other elements are each treated as the subject, a pot and similar things do not become cases of a doubtful, inconclusive reason. A pot has the same status as the subject. Doubt about the predicate is appropriate in the subject; it becomes a defect only when it occurs outside the subject. Thus the fallacy of a doubtful, inconclusive reason occurs when the reason is uncertain in a case where the absence of the predicate is certain. “Being a subject” can mean either being a case where the predicate is in doubt or lacking a valid cognition that establishes the predicate. Pots share that status, so their inclusion is not a defect. Calling them “equal to the subject” means only that they are not themselves named in the proposition. But being named in a proposition cannot be what makes something a subject, because inference for oneself need not be expressed as a proposition.

Earlier logicians state the inference in this form: “The disputed world is false, because it is perceptible, inert, and limited, like the silver seen in a shell.” There is no need to insist on every formal limb of the inference. Because the disagreement is already governed by an agreed convention, using a concise way of delimiting the subject is not a problem. The convention may not be present at the moment of inference, yet it can still indicate how the subject is delimited. Or the delimiting condition of the subject may simply be whatever delimits the subject matter of the disagreement. On either reading, the older inference's word “disputed” causes no difficulty.

**Translator's note:** “Predicate” here renders *sādhya*, the point to be established; “reason” renders *hetu*. “Shell-silver” is the standard example of silver mistakenly perceived in a shell.



### First definition of falsity: neither absolutely real nor absolutely unreal

**Source heading:** अथ सदसद्विलक्षणत्वरूपप्रथममिथ्यात्वविचारः

**Objection.** What kind of falsity is to be proved? The *Pañcapādikā* says that *mithyā* means “indefinable,” and describes the indefinable as not belonging to either reality or unreality. But what does this description mean? Does it mean (1) the absence of nonbeing qualified by being; (2) the two properties of absolute absence of being and absolute absence of nonbeing; or (3) an object qualified by both—absolutely lacking being and absolutely lacking nonbeing?

The first account does not work. The world is not accepted as nonbeing qualified by being, when being alone is its basis; and proving the absence of that qualification would merely prove what is already established.

The second account also fails. If being and nonbeing are treated as strict opposites, the absence of one requires the presence of the other, so they cannot both be absent. This account also applies too widely: attribute-free Brahman would lack being as an attribute, yet it remains real by its very nature. It would therefore count as false even though that is not intended. The account also fails to establish the predicate for shell-silver. Although shell-silver is not absolutely real—it is sublated—the proposed definition does not establish the required absence of absolute nonbeing in that case. The proof would therefore fail to cover the intended example.

The third account appears to fail for the same reasons: it brings back the contradiction, the unwanted result concerning Brahman, and the failure to cover shell-silver.

**Reply.** No. Those objections do not apply if the intended account consists of two properties: absolute absence of being and absolute absence of nonbeing. There is no contradiction unless being and nonbeing stand in one of three stronger relations: direct mutual exclusion; each extending throughout the other's absence; or each being included wherever the other's absence occurs.

The first relation is not accepted here. “Being,” in this discussion, means never being sublated at any of the three times. The absence of that kind of being is not absolute nonbeing. Rather, absolute nonbeing is the condition of having no locus at all in which something is cognized as existent. The predicate intended here is the absence of that condition: the thing is not absolutely real, yet it is cognized as existent in some locus. Thus the predicate is that the thing differs from what is never sublated, while still appearing as existent somewhere. Shell-silver meets this description, so the predicate is not missing from the example.

Nor are the absences of being and nonbeing equivalent in the second way. Shell-silver, which is not absolutely real, still appears as existent; its case shows that one absence does not simply extend wherever the other occurs. The third relation does not force a contradiction either. Cowhood and horsehood may be mutually exclusive, yet a camel can lack both. The absence of two mutually exclusive properties can coexist in one thing.

**Objection.** It was also argued that the world should count as real because Brahman, though without attributes, is of the nature of being, and the world too is experienced as being.

**Reply.** That does not follow. One existence, common to all things, is enough to explain why “is” is experienced throughout the world. There is no warrant for supposing that every part of the world has its own independent nature of being, like Brahman; that would also undermine the common use of the word “is.”

Alternatively, the predicate may be stated as two differences: difference from what is real and difference from what is absolutely unreal. If the world were identical with both, or identical with either one, those differences could not be present; this formulation therefore does not create the alleged extra result.

**Objection.** One might say that part of the predicate is already established: the world is already known to be different from absolute nonbeing, so proving it again is redundant.

**Reply.** Not so. In an argument that qualities are both different from and non-different from their qualified objects because they are spoken of in the same grammatical construction, an opponent may already accept difference by itself. Yet the intended point is the combined apprehension of difference and non-difference, so the proof is not redundant. The same applies here. The aim is to establish the combined apprehension of something that appears as existent even though it is not absolutely real. Just as one might aim to establish the combined identity expressed by “the pot is a jar” when that shared reference is not otherwise observed, here the target is the combined status of the world as perceptible yet not absolutely real.

The combined or qualified predicate may therefore be used. One might object that it is never found anywhere and is an unestablished qualification; or that knowing each part separately would allow one to prove something impossible, like a hare's horn. But the particular combination at issue is already found in shell-silver, so the qualification is not unestablished and the hare's-horn comparison does not apply.

Nor does this account wrongly include Brahman because Brahman is without attributes and so lacks being and nonbeing as attributes. Brahman is of the nature of being, so it is not a locus of the absolute absence of being. And because it is without attributes, it is not a locus of attributes consisting in absences either. That is the point.

**Translator's note:** *Sublation* means the correction of an earlier appearance by a later cognition that cancels it, as when supposed silver is recognized as a shell. *Counterpositive* means the thing whose absence is asserted. The example “the pot is a jar” illustrates a single object described under two names; it is not a claim that every pot is a jar in ordinary usage.
