## First pariccheda — opening argument

### Why the statement of disagreement belongs in inquiry

**Source heading:** विप्रतिपत्तिवाक्यस्य विचाराङ्गत्वम्

Since the establishment of non-duality depends on first establishing that duality is false, the falsity of duality must be the first point proved. Proof requires both establishing one's own position and refuting the opposing position. These two tasks must be carried out through one of the recognised forms of philosophical exchange: *vāda* (debate aimed at truth), *jalpa* (contentious disputation), or *vitaṇḍā* (criticism that attacks the opponent's position without establishing one's own). Because doubt arising from disagreement has a role in inquiry, a neutral moderator must begin by stating the disagreement.

That doubt, however, is not what creates *pakṣatā*, the status of a proposition as the subject of inference. The definition being discussed describes *pakṣatā* as the absence of a cognition that would establish the point, conjoined with the absence of a desire to establish it; doubt itself is not part of that definition. Otherwise, a person already certain of the Self on the authority of scripture could not infer it while wishing to do so. Here the text distinguishes the desire to prove a thesis (*sisādhayiṣā*) from the desire to infer (*anumitsā*). Nor can doubt be required on the ground that debaters are certain: if they are certain, doubt is impossible. And if one allows a doubt that they merely assume for the sake of argument, the rule would apply too broadly.

Disagreement is not needed simply to assign the opposing roles. The moderator can do that by saying, “You must prove this,” and, “He must refute that.” Since those instructions already assign the roles, stating the disagreement would be redundant for that purpose.

Even so, a doubt produced by disagreement belongs in the inquiry—not as a component of inferential cognition, but because the inquiry must remove it. In a particular case, certainty or another obstacle may prevent disagreement from producing such a doubt; nevertheless, disagreement is naturally capable of producing it. There is also no rule that disputants must actually be certain. The saying “The certain conduct a debate” refers to the certainty they claim to possess. We see boastful people enter contentious debate while taking up an opponent's position, even though they are certain of the contrary.

Therefore, just as the moderator sets the terms of a debate, a neutral moderator must state the disagreement in order to carry out that role. The disagreement about falsity is this: Is something distinct from consciousness, capable of appearing as existent, and not sublated by any valid cognition other than knowledge of Brahman, the object denied in all three times in the locus where it appears? Or is it not? Is it the object of that negation specifically when the negation is asserted from the standpoint of ultimate reality? Or is it not?

**Translator's note:** “Object of a negation” translates *pratiyogin*: the thing whose absence is being asserted. “Locus” translates *upādhi*, the basis in which something is presented. This formulation is setting up the debate; the argument for the definition continues in the following sections.

## First pariccheda — continued

### Considering what delimits the inferential subject

**Source heading:** पक्षतावच्छेदकविचारः

The passage now considers what precisely counts as the subject of an inference. If the aim is to establish the predicate as applying throughout the range fixed by the subject's delimiting condition, then, on one account, the proof is redundant even when the predicate has already been established for only part of that range. To avoid that result in the shell-and-silver example, the subject is qualified as “not sublated by any knowledge other than knowledge of Brahman.” If, however, the aim is to establish the predicate exactly within the range fixed by that delimiting condition, then proving it for a part of the range does not make the proof redundant. On that account, this extra qualification can be left out. The other two qualifications must still be retained: they prevent the subject from wrongly including sheer nonexistence or Brahman.

Alternatively, the disagreement can be stated one object at a time: “Is space unreal, or not? Is the earth unreal, or not?” If space, earth, and the other elements are each treated as the subject, a pot and similar things do not become cases of a doubtful, inconclusive reason. A pot has the same status as the subject. Doubt about the predicate is appropriate in the subject; it becomes a defect only when it occurs outside the subject. Thus the fallacy of a doubtful, inconclusive reason occurs when the reason is uncertain in a case where the absence of the predicate is certain. “Being a subject” can mean either being a case where the predicate is in doubt or lacking a valid cognition that establishes the predicate. Pots share that status, so their inclusion is not a defect. Calling them “equal to the subject” means only that they are not themselves named in the proposition. But being named in a proposition cannot be what makes something a subject, because inference for oneself need not be expressed as a proposition.

Earlier logicians state the inference in this form: “The disputed world is false, because it is perceptible, inert, and limited, like the silver seen in a shell.” There is no need to insist on every formal limb of the inference. Because the disagreement is already governed by an agreed convention, using a concise way of delimiting the subject is not a problem. The convention may not be present at the moment of inference, yet it can still indicate how the subject is delimited. Or the delimiting condition of the subject may simply be whatever delimits the subject matter of the disagreement. On either reading, the older inference's word “disputed” causes no difficulty.

**Translator's note:** “Predicate” here renders *sādhya*, the point to be established; “reason” renders *hetu*. “Shell-silver” is the standard example of silver mistakenly perceived in a shell.



### First definition of falsity: neither absolutely real nor absolutely unreal

**Source heading:** अथ सदसद्विलक्षणत्वरूपप्रथममिथ्यात्वविचारः

**Objection.** What kind of falsity is to be proved? The *Pañcapādikā* says that *mithyā* means “indefinable,” and describes the indefinable as not belonging to either reality or unreality. But what does this description mean? Does it mean (1) the absence of nonbeing qualified by being; (2) the two properties of absolute absence of being and absolute absence of nonbeing; or (3) an object qualified by both—absolutely lacking being and absolutely lacking nonbeing?

The first account does not work. The world is not accepted as nonbeing qualified by being, when being alone is its basis; and proving the absence of that qualification would merely prove what is already established.

The second account also fails. If being and nonbeing are treated as strict opposites, the absence of one requires the presence of the other, so they cannot both be absent. This account also applies too widely: attribute-free Brahman would lack being as an attribute, yet it remains real by its very nature. It would therefore count as false even though that is not intended. The account also fails to establish the predicate for shell-silver. Although shell-silver is not absolutely real—it is sublated—the proposed definition does not establish the required absence of absolute nonbeing in that case. The proof would therefore fail to cover the intended example.

The third account appears to fail for the same reasons: it brings back the contradiction, the unwanted result concerning Brahman, and the failure to cover shell-silver.

**Reply.** No. Those objections do not apply if the intended account consists of two properties: absolute absence of being and absolute absence of nonbeing. There is no contradiction unless being and nonbeing stand in one of three stronger relations: direct mutual exclusion; each extending throughout the other's absence; or each being included wherever the other's absence occurs.

The first relation is not accepted here. “Being,” in this discussion, means never being sublated at any of the three times. The absence of that kind of being is not absolute nonbeing. Rather, absolute nonbeing is the condition of having no locus at all in which something is cognized as existent. The predicate intended here is the absence of that condition: the thing is not absolutely real, yet it is cognized as existent in some locus. Thus the predicate is that the thing differs from what is never sublated, while still appearing as existent somewhere. Shell-silver meets this description, so the predicate is not missing from the example.

Nor are the absences of being and nonbeing equivalent in the second way. Shell-silver, which is not absolutely real, still appears as existent; its case shows that one absence does not simply extend wherever the other occurs. The third relation does not force a contradiction either. Cowhood and horsehood may be mutually exclusive, yet a camel can lack both. The absence of two mutually exclusive properties can coexist in one thing.

**Objection.** It was also argued that the world should count as real because Brahman, though without attributes, is of the nature of being, and the world too is experienced as being.

**Reply.** That does not follow. One existence, common to all things, is enough to explain why “is” is experienced throughout the world. There is no warrant for supposing that every part of the world has its own independent nature of being, like Brahman; that would also undermine the common use of the word “is.”

Alternatively, the predicate may be stated as two differences: difference from what is real and difference from what is absolutely unreal. If the world were identical with both, or identical with either one, those differences could not be present; this formulation therefore does not create the alleged extra result.

**Objection.** One might say that part of the predicate is already established: the world is already known to be different from absolute nonbeing, so proving it again is redundant.

**Reply.** Not so. In an argument that qualities are both different from and non-different from their qualified objects because they are spoken of in the same grammatical construction, an opponent may already accept difference by itself. Yet the intended point is the combined apprehension of difference and non-difference, so the proof is not redundant. The same applies here. The aim is to establish the combined apprehension of something that appears as existent even though it is not absolutely real. Just as one might aim to establish the combined identity expressed by “the pot is a jar” when that shared reference is not otherwise observed, here the target is the combined status of the world as perceptible yet not absolutely real.

The combined or qualified predicate may therefore be used. One might object that it is never found anywhere and is an unestablished qualification; or that knowing each part separately would allow one to prove something impossible, like a hare's horn. But the particular combination at issue is already found in shell-silver, so the qualification is not unestablished and the hare's-horn comparison does not apply.

Nor does this account wrongly include Brahman because Brahman is without attributes and so lacks being and nonbeing as attributes. Brahman is of the nature of being, so it is not a locus of the absolute absence of being. And because it is without attributes, it is not a locus of attributes consisting in absences either. That is the point.

**Translator's note:** *Sublation* means the correction of an earlier appearance by a later cognition that cancels it, as when supposed silver is recognized as a shell. *Counterpositive* means the thing whose absence is asserted. The example “the pot is a jar” illustrates a single object described under two names; it is not a claim that every pot is a jar in ordinary usage.

### Second account of falsity: being negated in the locus where it appears

**Source heading:** अथ द्वितीयमिथ्यात्वोपपत्तिः

**Definition.** Falsity is being the counterpositive of an absence that is asserted for all three times in the locus where the object is presented.

**Objection.** If this threefold absence is ultimately real, non-duality is lost. If it is merely an appearance, the point has already been proved and the argument is redundant. If it is empirically real, then, because it can itself be sublated, it does not conflict with ultimate reality. That would leave the argument beside the point. It would also make the non-dual Upaniṣads fail to reveal truth, and it would make the world—the counterpositive of their denial, though only an appearance—ultimately real.

**Reply.** No. Even if the negation is ultimate, it does not destroy non-duality, because the Brahman in which the world is denied is not different from that negation. Nor does it follow that the world, as the counterpositive of an ultimate absence, is itself ultimately real. A counterpositive need not be ultimately real: imagined silver in a shell is also presented as the counterpositive of an absence.

Or the negation may be non-ultimate. Even then, it is not merely an illusory negation; it is empirically real. One cannot conclude that it is irrelevant because it is sublatable and therefore cannot oppose ultimate existence. A dream object is sublated by a negation that occurs within the dream, yet that dream-negation does oppose the dream object. The negation is not required to be unsurpassable in order to negate what appears at its own level. Here the negation and the world it negates have the same empirical status, so the negation does oppose the world.

**Objection.** If the negation itself is later denied, does that not establish the reality of its counterpositive?

**Reply.** That follows only when denying the negation is what makes the counterpositive present. For example, after the judgement “this is not silver,” the further judgement “this is not not-silver” can establish silver. But in other cases both the negation and its counterpositive are denied. At the time of destruction, for instance, both the prior absence of a pot and the pot whose absence it was are denied; the pot is not thereby made real. In the same way, a cognition that sublates both the world and its denial does not establish the world as real.

Nor does it follow that scripture is invalid because it communicates a non-ultimate negation. Scripture can authoritatively teach that what is different from Brahman—including the world and its denial—is non-ultimate. Since it teaches this as non-ultimacy, there is no defect in its authority.

**Objection.** Is the counterpositive of this denial the object in its own nature, or is it the object considered under the aspect of ultimate reality? Neither option seems acceptable. On the first option, space and the other elements, whose existence and causal activity are established by scripture, and which arise from ignorance and are destroyed by knowledge, would be negated at all three times even while they are present to cognition. On the second, there is mutual dependence: an object's ultimate status is defined by its not being sublated, but its falsity is now being defined through that very ultimate status. If ultimate reality is itself denied only under the aspect of ultimate reality, the same problem recurs without end.

**Reply.** Do not understand the definition that way. The world and shell-silver are accepted as the counterpositives of threefold negation in their own nature. After the illusion of silver in a shell, direct knowledge of the basis gives the understanding, “The silver is not, was not, and will not be here.” The negation concerns the apparent silver itself; it does not require first deciding whether the silver is ultimate or merely phenomenal. Scripture likewise gives the understanding that the world itself is denied: “Here there is no plurality.”

**Objection.** Perhaps, then, only ordinary, real silver is the counterpositive of the negation, “This is not silver.” In that case, the appearance of silver in the shell is not itself negated, and the correction would not address the very illusion that prompted it.

**Reply.** The earlier teachers' statement that ordinary, real silver is the counterpositive must be understood carefully. What is presented as the counterpositive is the illusory silver that appears to be identical with ordinary silver. Whether one speaks of that appearance in its own nature or under the aspect of ordinary silver is not the point. The statement is intended to reject the view that silver is perceived only as identical with what is before us: the person who wants silver acts toward the appearance as though it were ordinary silver.

The *Tattvapradīpikā* explains the point this way. The earlier teachers' phrase “ordinary, real silver is the counterpositive of the denial ‘This is not silver’” should be understood by accepting that what is immediately perceived as ordinary silver is denied at all three times as ordinary silver: “This is not ordinary, real silver.” The expression “the silver here is not silver” presents a difference between the illusory silver indicated by “this” and ordinary silver named by “silver”; that difference is a kind of falsity. By contrast, “There is no silver here” expresses absolute absence. Because the locus and the counterpositive are expressed in different grammatical cases, this second statement asserts absence rather than mere difference. It takes the silver that appeared before us as the object of the absolute absence of ordinary silver. This is an explicit account of falsity, so it does not abandon the tradition's position, force an alternative theory of error, or contradict the text.

**Objection.** If this is right, everything must be absolutely nonexistent. Whatever is absent from a given locus at all three times would be a counterpositive of such an absence; the definition would not distinguish the world from a hare's horn. Nor can absolute nonexistence simply mean “without any name or description,” since that would only restate the definition. It cannot mean “not perceived,” either: if nonbeing were never perceived, one could not recognize the difference between nonbeing and what is not nonbeing, remove the perception of nonbeing, or even use the word “nonbeing.” And if it means “not perceived directly,” the definition would wrongly include things that are real but eternally beyond the senses.

**Reply.** Absolute nonexistence is not merely being denied in some locus. It is being unfit to be presented as existent in any locus at all. Before it is sublated, shell-silver and the world are presented as existent in a locus; they therefore are not sheer nonexistence. The word “locus” in the definition points to this presentation as existent. Those who deny any real basis for error cannot account for shell-silver or the world as being presented in this way, but that is not the account adopted here.

**Objection.** The definition still seems too broad. It appears to make falsity the condition of being opposed by an absolute absence that has an existent locus. The logicians already accept cases involving all-pervading absences and things such as space, so the proof would be established before the argument began. If instead one says that a thing is false when it is the counterpositive of an absolute absence residing in something real, the result is still overbroad: a pot is a counterpositive of absences in many places. Adding that the locus must be the locus of the pot by conjunction or by inherence does not solve the problem. If the relation itself is not specified, the account overreaches; if it is specified, non-pervasive relations such as conjunction would again make the proof redundant. One might also deny that space is a counterpositive of absolute absence because there is no direct perception that “space is absent here,” no supporting inference, and no favorable reasoning. But the same lack of evidence would then tell against treating space as a counterpositive of prior or posterior absence. Mere generalization from a few observed cases would establish those other absences as well, although no result requires them.

**Reply.** The word “presented” prevents this overreach. A thing is a counterpositive of an absolute absence in a locus only in the respect in which it was presented as that locus's occupant. That respect includes a particular relation and a particular delimiting condition. A floor is not presented as the locus of a pot without the relation of contact; a tree is not presented as the locus of a monkey's contact without specifying the part of the tree that delimits that contact. Thus the definition concerns the very relation and delimiting condition under which something was presented as a locus. Once these are held fixed, there is no redundant proof of what the logicians already accept.

If one supposes that space cannot be a counterpositive of absolute absence because there is no cognition that “space is absent here,” the same argument would also exclude its being a counterpositive of prior absence or destruction: there is no perception, inference, or consequence that establishes those either. Conversely, mere generalization would establish all of them alike. The argument therefore has to be settled by the way the locus and relation are actually presented, not by adding an unsupported relation after the fact.

On this account, falsity is being the counterpositive of an absolute absence whose locus is sheer existence. One might object that being and absence would then fail to conflict, so their cognitions could not sublate one another. But things at different levels of reality can coexist without conflict. What conflicts are two empirically real cognitions of the same locus and the same thing: where the empirical absolute absence of a pot is present, that pot is not empirically present.

**Objection.** In the correction “This is a shell, not silver,” the cognition of absence is itself empirical. Even if it removes ordinary reality from the silver that appeared before us, it leaves the silver's phenomenal existence untouched. Then, even after correction, the cognition “this is silver” should continue.

**Reply.** Direct, immediate knowledge—“This is a shell”—removes the ignorance that supplied the apparent silver. When that ignorance is removed, the phenomenal existence of the silver is removed as well. If the basis is known only indirectly and the ignorance that produced the illusion remains, the appearance may continue despite the loss of ordinary reality. Thus a cognition such as “the jaggery is bitter” may remain in the relevant sense after only an indirect correction. In the same way, before direct realization of non-dual Brahman, indirect knowledge may remove the world's empirical status while the appearance continues; when the ignorance of the basis is removed by direct realization, the appearance will no longer continue.

This also answers the objection that, if “locus” means merely any locus, form would be false because form is absolutely absent in air. There is no overextension once the relation and the particular respect in which the locus was presented are fixed. If, however, “locus” is taken to mean the underlying basis of an illusion, circularity would follow: the cognition would be called erroneous because its object is false, and the object false because the cognition is erroneous. The relation-sensitive account avoids that circularity.

**Objection.** The scripture “That same one is below” presents Brahman in a locus delimited by space and time. Since Brahman is not ultimately absent there, would the definition make Brahman false? And if Brahman is without qualities, does it not also lack the properties of being and nonbeing, so that the definition would apply to it?

**Reply.** No. Brahman is not a locus of the property of being a counterpositive of an absence. Since the definition does not treat absence as a property separate from its locus, Brahman's lack of such a property does not make Brahman false. Nor does this mean Brahman lacks truth. Brahman is itself of the nature of being, so the absence that constitutes falsity is not opposed to its nature. The Upaniṣadic teaching that Brahman is “reality, knowledge, and infinite” remains intact.

The same reasoning applies to self-luminosity and the other descriptions of Brahman. Self-luminosity is the absence of dependence on another cognition; eternality is the absence of limitation by time; all-pervasiveness is the absence of limitation by space; fullness is the absence of limitation by other things. Although Brahman is not a bearer of positive qualities, it can be described through the absence of every limiting property. There is therefore no inconsistency.

**Translator's note:** “Counterpositive” means the object whose absence is asserted. “Sublation” is the correction of an appearance by a cognition that cancels it. “Locus” here means the basis in which an object is presented, with the relevant relation and delimiting condition held fixed. The *Tattvapradīpikā* is quoted by Madhusūdana within the argument; its words are not a later commentary inserted into the base text.

### Third account of falsity: being removable by knowledge

**Source heading:** अथ तृतीयमिथ्यात्वविचारः

**Definition.** Falsity may be defined as being removable by knowledge.

**Objection.** This definition is too broad if it includes anything removed by a later cognition: an earlier cognition is removed by a later one. It is too narrow if it excludes things removed by non-cognitive causes: a pot and similar objects are removed by a hammer. Even if the definition is restricted to what is removed by knowledge, the difficulty remains. Shell-silver is removed by direct realization of the underlying shell, and so the example would not establish that the silver is removable by knowledge in the required sense. If instead “knowledge-removable” means removable by some property that pervades knowledge, the definition extends too far to latent impressions, which are affected by memory, a form of knowledge.

**Reply.** Knowledge-removability means being the counterpositive of a general absence of persistence brought about by knowledge. Persistence has two forms: persistence as the thing itself and persistence in the form of its cause. This follows from the doctrine that an effect exists in its cause before it is manifest. When a pot is struck with a hammer, the pot ceases to exist in its own form, but its material cause remains. Its general persistence has therefore not been removed by knowledge. The definition does not wrongly include a pot destroyed by a hammer.

Nor does it include an earlier cognition merely because a later cognition removes it. The earlier cognition, once it has subsided, remains in the form of its causal impression. It has not undergone a general absence of persistence. For the same reason, although a hare's horn does not persist in any form, its absence is not caused by knowledge; it is not knowledge-removable in the defined sense.

Shell-silver, by contrast, must be accepted as persisting while it is being perceived. Its immediate appearance would otherwise be inexplicable. It ceases to persist only when the cognition that corrects it occurs. The ignorance that supplies the silver and the silver produced by that ignorance are removed together by direct realization of the underlying shell. The text of the *Vivaraṇa* says that sublation is the removal, through knowledge, of ignorance together with its present or already subsided effects. The *Vārttika* likewise says:

> “From the very arising of right understanding produced by sentences such as ‘That thou art,’ ignorance, together with its effects, was not, is not, and will not be.”

The phrase “together with its effects, was not” refers to removal along with effects that have already subsided; “together with its effects, will not be” refers to the prevention of future effects. The ignorance that produces silver in a shell is removed by direct realization of its basis, together with its present or subsided effects. Since the account accepts distinct instances of ignorance as the causes of distinct appearances of silver, the example does show the required knowledge-removability. Everyone accepts the cognition “the pot is gone” after a hammer-strike; by the same reasoning, everyone accepts the cognition “the ignorance of the shell and the silver in it are gone” after realization of the shell. There is therefore no failure of the example.

**Objection.** Could “knowledge-removable” mean removable by a property that pervades knowledge? If so, a subsequent cognition does not remove a prior cognition by such a property. Rather, it removes it through a special quality of the self shared with desire and the like, or through the technical property the text calls *udīcya*. The proof would then be redundant only under a different account.

**Reply.** That qualification is also acceptable. Nor does it extend the definition to latent impressions that are not removed by desire and the like. There is no valid evidence that a memory, simply as memory, removes its impression. Experience instead shows that an impression becomes stronger when a memory arises. Its increased strength is explained by the accumulation of several impressions with the same content.

In fact, what is intended is knowledge-removability by direct realization. This avoids the earlier objections. There is also no unwanted extension to doubt on the ground that doubt is removed by a decisive cognition, where decisiveness is a property that pervades knowledge. The definition is thus sound.

**Translator's note:** *Sublation* is the cancellation of an appearance by a correcting cognition. The passage distinguishes an object's persistence in its own visible form from persistence in its underlying cause. *Udīcya* is retained as a technical term because its precise role belongs to a Nyāya account of the qualities of the self.

### Fourth account of falsity: absence in the object's own locus

**Source heading:** अथ चतुर्थमिथ्यात्वविचारः

**Definition.** Falsity may also be defined as being the counterpositive of an absolute absence located in that very object's own locus. This means that the object is perceived as present in the locus of its own absolute absence. This differs from the preceding definition; the earlier objections and replies apply here as well.

**Objection.** An absolute absence cannot coexist with its counterpositive in a place connected to it by conjunction or inherence. If it could, then the object's role as a material cause and the other relations that depend on its presence would become impossible.

**Reply.** That objection fails. Just as an absence and its counterpositive can be spoken of together in relation to time, their coexistence in a place is not impossible. The existence of an object's prior absence does not prevent that object's cause from being present. Nor can one argue that prior absence is impossible in a locus of absolute absence: time provides a counterexample. We accept, on the strength of ordinary cognition, that prior absence and absolute absence can share a temporal locus—for example, “Now there is no pot at all; now the pot is not yet present.”

**Objection.** That co-location is accepted for time because we have the cognition “now there is an absolute absence of a pot” and “now there is a prior absence of a pot.” But there is no evidence that both absences can share a spatial locus.

**Reply.** That is not so. The inference concerning falsity, scripture, and related means of knowledge supply evidence. As explained earlier, an absence and an existent thing at different levels of reality do not conflict merely because they share a locus. Nor does the definition extend to sheer nonbeing, because it requires that the object be perceived as existent in the locus of its own absolute absence.

**Objection.** A scriptural statement says, “Some say that this was nonbeing alone in the beginning.” If nonbeing is thus presented as existent, the definition would apply to it too.

**Reply.** The negation in the statement “This was being alone in the beginning” denies that meaning; it does not assert that absolute nonbeing exists. Such an assertion would be contradictory. The definition therefore does not extend to absolute nonbeing. The other points have already been answered above, so Madhusūdana concludes this discussion.

### Fifth account of falsity: being distinct from what is established as real

**Source heading:** अथ पञ्चममिथ्यात्वनिरूपणम्

**Definition.** Falsity may be defined as being distinct from what is real. Here “real” means established by a valid means of knowledge, and a means of knowledge is valid when it produces cognition without a defect. On this account, a thing is false when it differs from what a faultless cognition establishes, as a dream object does. Being established by valid knowledge is pervaded by not being sublated.

To prevent the definition from including sheer nonbeing or attribute-free Brahman, it must also specify that the object is presented as existent. Neither of those is the object of a cognition that presents it under the form “existent.”

**Objection.** What does “real” mean in the phrase “distinct from what is real”? Does it mean being the locus of the universal called existence, being unsublatable, or being Brahman? The first reading cannot apply to pots and other things if, as you maintain, their universal existence is produced by ignorance. The second makes falsity amount to sublatability, which merely returns to an earlier definition. The third makes the proof redundant, since the world is already accepted as different from Brahman.

**Reply.** These objections do not apply, because none of those three meanings has been accepted here. The arguments already given for the definition that something is distinct from both real and unreal should also be considered here. The remaining points will be addressed when the example used to establish the definition is discussed.

**Translator's note:** “A valid means of knowledge” translates *pramāṇa*. “Sublated” means cancelled by a later, corrective cognition. The fifth account is stated briefly here; Madhusūdana says that some supporting points remain for a later discussion.

### General defense of the claim that the world is false

**Source heading:** अथ मिथ्यात्वसामान्योपपत्तिः

**Objection.** If the falsity just defined is itself false, the world must be real. When two opposed properties are possible in one thing, the rule is that if one is false, the other is real. If falsity and reality are themselves opposed, then either falsity is false and reality is established, or falsity is both real and false; either way, the reality of the world follows and non-duality is contradicted.

**Reply.** That does not follow. Even if falsity is itself sublated, the world is not thereby established as real. The rule that denying one of two opposed properties establishes the other applies where the condition that delimits what is being denied does not apply to both. For example, in shell-silver, silverhood and the absence of silverhood are mutually exclusive; so too are silverhood and being different from silver. There the properties that delimit the negation are different. In the present case, the same delimiting feature—perceptibility and the like—applies to both reality and falsity. Just as denying both cowhood and horsehood in an elephant does not make either one true, denying one of these two properties here does not make the other true.

Moreover, reality and falsity are neither simply each other's absence nor properties that each extend throughout the other's absence. Even if they were mutually exclusive, properties belonging to different levels of reality would not conflict. An object's empirical falsity can remove its empirical reality without removing its imagined appearance. This is like conjunction and its absence, which logicians allow to be predicated together when the relations differ.

When one property is established by a means of knowledge that sublates the other, the two belong to different levels; shell-silver and its absence are an example. When both are sublated by the same cognition, they belong to the same level; shell-silver and being distinct from the shell are an example. The world and its falsity are both sublated by the same knowledge of Brahman. They therefore have the same level of reality. Since the cognition that sublates falsity also sublates the world, there is no harm to non-duality.

**Translator's note:** The passage distinguishes two ways properties can be related: one may be established while the other is cancelled, or both may be cancelled by the same cognition. That distinction is central to the reply.

### Establishing “perceptibility” as a reason for falsity

**Source heading in Advaita Śāradā:** अथ दृश्यत्वहेतूपपत्तिः  
**Heading in the E-Bharati transcription:** अथ दृश्यत्वहेतुभङ्गः. The two transcriptions give different headings for this discussion; I am retaining both readings here pending collation with the printed edition.

**Objection.** If falsity is to be proved, the “perceptibility” used as the reason also needs to be explained. What does perceptibility mean? It could mean: (1) being pervaded by a mental modification; (2) being pervaded by the result of cognition; (3) a condition common to those two; (4) being an object of consciousness at some time and in some way; (5) a fixed dependence on a cognition other than oneself in one's own practical dealings; or (6) not being self-luminous.

The first definition fails because the Self is an object of a mental modification produced by Vedānta. For the same reason, the third definition fails. The second also fails: it would not apply to eternal objects beyond the senses or to shell-silver, so the reason would be unproved for part of the subject or absent from the example. The fourth fails because we say, “I did not know Brahman before; now I know it through Vedānta.” The fifth fails because even when speaking of Brahman as non-dual and under similar descriptions, we find a regular dependence on another cognition. The sixth definition—being unfit for immediate practical dealings despite not being an object of knowledge—would also fail in the case of shell-silver, which is fit for immediate practical dealings.

**Reply.** No. Every account except being pervaded by the result of cognition can withstand examination. If perceptibility means being pervaded by a mental modification, there is no defect concerning Brahman. Pure Brahman is not perceptible: scripture says, “That which is unseen.” Only Brahman qualified by adjuncts is an object of a mental modification, and that qualified form is itself false. Brahman is therefore not excluded from the teaching of Vedānta.

**Objection.** If that is so, pure Brahman is never established.

**Reply.** That does not follow, because pure Brahman is established by its own luminosity.

**Objection.** One cannot affirm or deny a property of a subject that is wholly unknown. By denying perceptibility of pure Brahman, one must still accept that pure Brahman is knowable. Nor can one say that scripture denies perceptibility while Brahman is established by its own self-luminosity: when the sentence “The pure is self-luminous” produces a cognition of Brahman as qualified, the pure reality does not shine in that cognition. Its self-luminosity would then remain unproved.

**Reply.** No mental modification can take pure Brahman as an object under a property belonging to a mental modification, because pure Brahman cannot be an object of such a modification. The sentence “The pure is self-luminous” must therefore be understood indirectly: impurity is what is pervaded by non-luminosity, and removing impurity leaves pure Brahman as self-luminous. This is like establishing non-difference by denying difference. The word “pure” is not meaningless, whether it is taken directly or indirectly, because the intended meaning is established.

In the same way, the statement “The pure is neither perceptible nor false” means that impurity is pervaded by perceptibility and falsity; by excluding impurity, it establishes that pure Brahman is neither. This also refutes the view that mere manifestation is enough to make something false and that Brahman, because it shines by itself, must therefore be false. Shell-silver does not possess self-luminosity as its nature, and it has not been established that Brahman is an object of manifestation in the relevant sense.

**Objection.** When a qualified object is known, its qualifying subject is also apprehended. If scripture presents qualified Brahman as perceptible, then the subject, pure Brahman, is perceptible too. One might compare this with the scriptural designation “Viṣṇu, the radiant one,” or with Agni and Soma treated together as a single deity. In those cases, it is the combined or qualified entity that is the object, not each element separately. But if neither the subject nor the qualifier is an object, the reason would be unproved in part.

**Reply.** There is no defect even if the subject, as qualified, is an object, since that qualified form is accepted as false. For the same reason, the claim that a conditioned object can be known while the underlying subject remains outside the object-side does not help: the subject in its conditioned form is also accepted as false. Specifying that the subject is an object of another cognition removes the alleged failure of the reason.

**Objection.** When the undivided mental modification produced by Vedānta takes Brahman with an adjunct as its object, there is no other adjunct at that time. The mental modification itself must therefore serve as the adjunct, which would make that mental modification its own object. That cannot be accepted. In verbal understanding, moreover, something not presented by the words does not appear. If the mental modification somehow became an object, the cognition would still fail to remove ignorance and its effects, because only a cognition that does not take ignorance and its effects as its object can remove them. Otherwise, ordinary cognitions such as “I am ignorant” and “This is a pot,” which also have conditioned objects, would remove ignorance.

**Reply.** A mental modification can serve as an adjunct without appearing as an object within the verbal cognition. The *Kalpataru* author states the point this way: “A mental modification that takes pure Brahman as its object arises as the cause of the cessation of every adjunct other than itself, since it too is an adjunct. The unconditioned is therefore not an object. The mental modification's reflection serves to establish existence here; it does not enter the category of objects by being itself something illuminated.”

The meaning is this. Although consciousness is conditioned by ignorance when it is the witness, ignorance itself does not enter the category of what is witnessed, because ignorance is inert; only its object does. Likewise, although consciousness is conditioned by a mental modification when it is an object, the mental modification itself does not enter the object-category, since it cannot be its own object. It nevertheless makes consciousness available as an object. There is no inconsistency.

This also explains how knowledge and ignorance can have the same object. Ignorance presents Brahman as an object only while ignorance serves as its adjunct; without that adjunct, ignorance itself is absent. Thus both knowledge and ignorance can have the same conditioned object. It also explains why knowledge of an adjunct does not always remove ignorance: ignorance is presented through its adjunct, and only a cognition with the same object can remove it.

**Reply continued.** A further defensible definition is being an object of a mental modification that is not produced by words. Otherwise, sheer nonbeing—such as a hare's horn, presented by the verbal thought “A hare's horn is nonexistent”—would also count as perceptible. Pure Brahman can be the object of a modification produced by Vedānta, but neither pure Brahman nor sheer nonbeing is the object of a non-verbal mental modification. There is therefore no overreach.

Perceptibility may instead mean being an object of a mental modification that presents something under a determinate qualification. The qualification must be some describable property. This avoids overreach to pure Brahman, which is known without such a qualification, and to sheer nonbeing, which is known under a property that cannot itself be described as existing. Absence, too, can be the object of cognition under a describable property—its being an absence—so the reason is not unproved in that case.

One can also define perceptibility as being an object in a way that gives rise to practical dealings. This is common to what is pervaded by the mental modification and what is pervaded by its result. The ways of avoiding overreach to Brahman and sheer nonbeing have already been explained.

Or perceptibility can mean being an object of consciousness, that is, being related to consciousness in some way. Consciousness itself is not thereby included: a relation that depends on difference cannot hold between consciousness and itself as one and the same thing. The problem of sheer nonbeing must still be addressed, as above.

Another definition is the fixed dependence of one's own practical dealings on a cognition other than oneself. “Cognition” here can mean consciousness made manifest through an object, consciousness made manifest through a mental modification, or pure consciousness itself. On that account, dealings with pots and other objects—including eternal objects beyond the senses and objects revealed by the witness—depend on a cognition other than the objects themselves. “Practical dealings” includes both manifestation and expression. Brahman's ever-established manifestation, by contrast, does not depend on another cognition. The word “fixed” prevents an unwanted extension: only the dealings concerning the object in question are required to depend on another cognition.

This also supports defining perceptibility as not being self-luminous. Self-luminosity is the capacity to be immediately apparent without depending on anything other than oneself. Its opposite is dependence on something else for immediate appearance. Pots, which are not immediately apparent by themselves but become so through another cognition, meet this condition. One cannot object that Brahman too has an imagined difference from Brahman: that difference is not defined by dependence on another cognition for immediate appearance. It is defined by some other property, such as being an individual self or the Lord.

Perceptibility can also be stated as being unfit for immediate practical dealings while not being an object of knowledge. It is no objection that this condition might occur in Brahman, ignorance, the inner instrument, or shell-silver. Here “fit for immediate practical dealings” means fit to be the object of the mental modification that removes ignorance. Ignorance and shell-silver are not such objects, so neither the reason nor the example fails. Madhusūdana says that the way pots and similar things are pervaded by the result of cognition will be explained later. If the cessation of ignorance is taken as a fifth kind, add that the reason must be present while ignorance is present; this also avoids an extension to sheer nonbeing. Each proposed reason must be qualified in the way needed to prevent a counterexample.

If the point to be proved is only that something is distinct from what is real, however, there is not even the slightest overreach to sheer nonbeing or to this fifth kind of cessation of ignorance. This completes the defense of perceptibility as a reason.

**Translator's note:** The Sanskrit uses several technical accounts of how an object can be “seen” by consciousness. “Mental modification” translates *vṛtti*; “result of cognition” translates *phala*; “adjunct” translates *upādhi*. The heading differs between the two online transcriptions and remains to be checked against the printed edition.

### Establishing “inertness” as a reason for falsity

**Source heading in Advaita Śāradā:** अथ जडत्वहेतूपपत्तिः  
**Heading in the E-Bharati transcription:** जडत्वहेतुभङ्गः. I retain both headings because the online witnesses differ.

Inertness can also serve as a reason for falsity.

**Objection.** What does inertness mean? It might mean not being a knower, not being knowledge, or not being the Self. The first definition fails: on your account, the “I”-sense included in the disputed subject is a knower, so the reason is not established there. And the pure Self, which is not a knower, would be an unwanted counterexample.

The second definition also fails. You say that only consciousness reflected in a mental modification is knowledge. The mental modification by itself and consciousness by itself are therefore each “not knowledge.” Even if that avoids the problem concerning the mental modification, it still makes consciousness an unwanted counterexample.

The third definition fails because you have not explained what “Self” means. It is not a special class or species, since you accept only one Self. Even if qualified selves are distinct, they are included within the disputed subject. Nor can the Self be defined simply as bliss: the proposed distinction from bliss is not established for pleasure that arises through the senses. If that pleasure too is Self, the objections raised against defining inertness as non-knowledge return.

**Reply.** No. The second and third definitions can be used without those defects.

If inertness means being non-knowledge, the Self is not an unwanted counterexample. Knowledge is illumination marked by an object, and that illumination does not cease even in liberation. The Self is therefore not “non-knowledge.”

**Objection.** Object-directedness is natural to desire, knowledge, and similar states, just as being the counterpositive of an absence is natural to an absence. If knowledge's object-directedness is real at the same level as knowledge, the account still fails.

**Reply.** Knowledge's being directed toward an object is a relation to that object. That relation is not ultimate; it is superimposed, since an ultimately real relation cannot be established, as will be explained later. It is no more inherent in knowledge than silver is inherent in a shell. Desire and the other states are accepted as object-directed only because of the adjunct that makes them so; the object-directedness is not their intrinsic nature.

**Objection.** Then desire, like knowledge, could sometimes exist without any relation to an object.

**Reply.** That does not follow. What causes a state to be object-directed has a higher level of reality than the adjunct that is the state itself. Desire and its object have the same level of reality, so desire remains dependent on an object.

**Objection.** You accept that in liberation the Self is without an object. If bliss does not shine then, liberation would be purposeless.

**Reply.** In that state bliss itself is the light; it is not that bliss needs to be illuminated by another light. Awareness marked by an object remains as well, so knowledge is not lost.

**Objection.** Even so, if there is no knower, that state is not knowledge. An act of enjoying cannot occur without someone who enjoys. Nor does it help to say that knowledge is beginningless and therefore not an activity. Other beginningless things still depend on something else: prior absence depends on the thing that is its counterpositive; a universal depends on its individual manifestation; the distinction between living self and Brahman depends on the qualified subject and what is distinguished from it; ignorance depends on a locus and an object; and even the existence of Brahman seems to require an agent, as in the sentence “Brahman exists.”

Even if past and future cognition, or God's cognition, does not depend on an object for its production, it is still defined in relation to an object. Knowledge's dependence on a knower and a known object must therefore be natural. Otherwise, the experience “I know this” would not occur, and the account would conflict with the *Vivaraṇa*, which defines knowledge as the knower's illumination of an object.

**Reply.** Not so. A universal may depend on an individual for its definition and yet, at a particular time, be unconnected with that individual, because the adjunct that produces the connection has a higher level of reality. For the same reason, being accompanied by a known object and being accompanied by a knower are not intrinsic to knowledge.

To be accompanied by an object might mean that knowledge is produced by its object. That does not work for indirect knowledge or for God's knowledge. Or it might mean that whenever knowledge exists, its object exists at the same time. This fails for the same reasons as before; a spatial relation between every cognition and its object is even more remote. One might narrow the claim to immediate cognition and say that whenever it is direct, an object is present. But that too fails for God's and a yogin's knowledge. The rule that everyone accepts is narrower: whenever cognition is sensory, there is an object. That does not apply to knowledge that is the nature of the Self, since that knowledge is not sensory.

**Objection.** If you do not accept that direct cognition requires an object, how can you establish the silver in the cognition “This is silver”? Directness could be explained without a real object, so the cognition would not prove the indefinable silver.

**Reply.** The objecthood recalled in the experience “I know this as silver” cannot be explained without another support; the indefinable silver is established on that basis, as will be shown later. The same reasoning establishes an indefinable object even in an indirect illusion. Alternatively, the object is pervaded by knowledge only when the direct cognition is produced; the account does not accept that scripturally taught knowledge is a produced direct cognition. The existence of indefinable silver is therefore not left unexplained.

Being accompanied by a knower might mean being produced by a knower. That fails for God's knowledge, which is not produced, and for knowledge, whose eternality will be established later. It might mean that knowledge is always pervaded by a knower; that is not established as a necessary rule. Or it might mean that knowledge inheres in a knower. But that does not follow either: knowledge could be associated with knowledge in the same way that it could be produced by knowledge. Since knowledge is not accepted here as either a quality or an action, one cannot infer that it must reside in a substance. Experience and the statement in the *Vivaraṇa* can be explained by a knower–known relation arising on some occasions; that relation need not be knowledge's intrinsic nature.

The verb in “Brahman exists” does not make Brahman the agent that brings its own existence about; that would be impossible for what is eternal. The verbal form is used because it is grammatically correct.

**Objection.** Knowledge must be either valid cognition or error. The knowledge that is the Self cannot be valid cognition, because its object would then be ignorance or something else that must be ultimately real. Nor can it be error, since error is produced by defects.

**Reply.** It can be distinct by its own nature from both. Even logicians accept God's knowledge and indeterminate perception of a pot as examples that do not fit their ordinary account of either validity or error. If God's cognition were valid, it would have to be produced by the proper qualities; if it were error, it would have to be produced by defects. Likewise, in indeterminate perception there is no determinate presentation of a property as belonging to a thing. We have no difficulty allowing knowledge to be neither one nor the other by nature. Although self-knowledge is intrinsically distinct from both valid cognition and error, two different mental modifications can condition it, allowing it to function in ordinary dealings as either.

This also clarifies the third definition. Inertness can be defined as being other than the Self, or as being other than bliss. Sensory pleasure too is Brahman in its nature; it is only the adjunct that limits it that is subject to arising and ceasing.

**Objection.** The imagined difference between knowledge and the Self, or between bliss and the Self, is also present in Brahman, so the reason would overextend.

**Reply.** The relevant difference must be at the same level of reality as the thing being considered. Alternatively, specify that the difference is not due to an adjunct. This avoids overextension to sheer nonbeing and to the fifth kind of cessation of ignorance, as explained earlier.

Inertness may also mean not being self-luminous, a definition already established in the preceding discussion. This completes the defense of inertness as a reason.

**Translator's note:** “Knower” translates *jñātṛ*; “knowledge” here translates *jñāna*, which Madhusūdana analyzes as consciousness marked by an object or mental modification. The *Vivaraṇa* is quoted within the base text's argument; it is not a commentary added to this translation.



### Establishing boundedness as a reason for falsity

**Source heading:** अथ परिच्छिन्नत्वहेतूपपत्तिः  
**Heading variant:** The E-Bharati transcription of the 1937 edition labels this unit परिच्छिन्नत्वहेतुभङ्गः; Advaita Śāradā labels it परिच्छिन्नत्वहेतूपपत्तिः. This is a difference in the heading, not a difference in the argument below.

Boundedness also serves as a reason for the world's falsity. There are three kinds of boundedness: spatial, temporal, and objectual. Spatial boundedness is being the counterpositive of an absolute absence; temporal boundedness is being the counterpositive of destruction; and objectual boundedness is being the counterpositive of mutual absence.

**Objection.** If spatial boundedness means being the counterpositive of an absolute absence related by inherence, the reason applies to the Self too: like space and other things, the Self is not inhered in anything. If the relation is conjunction, the reason is not established for space and similar things, since conjunction applies only to substances with form. If one instead means an absolute absence located in something without form, the reason still applies to the Self. And if one means the absence of every relation, it applies to the supreme Self, which is free from all relations, while it is not established for ignorance, which has relations. Temporal boundedness is not established for space either, since other schools accept it as eternal. Finally, objectual boundedness applies to the Self: the Self is the counterpositive of a mutual absence with inert things, since it is different from them. Otherwise, the Self would itself have to be inert.

**Reply.** No. The reason's application to the Self is prevented by specifying that the absolute absence and mutual absence must be at the same level of reality as their counterpositive. There is no failure to establish the reason for ignorance, space, and similar things, because they are counterpositives of absolute and mutual absences at their own level of reality. When the absence of something empirical—such as ignorance or space—is asserted as ultimately real, add the qualification that its locus must be no less real than the counterpositive. Thus apparent silver in a shell can still be the counterpositive of an empirical absence without making the reason absent from the example. Nor does this make the predicate identical with the thing to be proved: the definitions of falsity stated earlier are not themselves defined in this way.

Space's being subject to destruction is also established. Scripture says, “From this very Self, space arose”; its production is therefore accepted on scriptural authority and may be inferred. The passage that calls the Self “all-pervading and eternal, like space” is to be understood as describing the Self's being all-pervading at the same time as other things and its enduring through the total dissolution of the universe. Scripture says that what is other than the Self is subject to destruction; it is non-Self alone that is perishable.

For this reason, pots and other things are imagined in the reality that appears as common to them all: they are distinguished from one another. A snake, a garland, and similar appearances are distinguished within the “this” that appears in a rope. In the same way, pots and other things are distinguished within Brahman, which pervades them: “The pot exists”; “the cloth exists.” Ānandabodha's account is therefore sound. Here, “distinguished” means objectual limitation through being the counterpositive of a difference at the same level of reality. Brahman and what is absolutely unreal are not counterexamples.

**Objection.** In statements such as “a horned cow” and “a hornless cow,” individual cows are not imagined in cowhood, even though cowhood is what appears in common across the examples. This would be a counterexample.

**Reply.** No separate universal of cowhood, over and above the universal of existence, is accepted. Even if one accepts cowhood and similar universals, one need not accept a further universal that delimits what manifests them. Particular individuals—whether unshared or grouped by incidental features such as having a dewlap—can produce the relevant ordinary usage. The universal of existence, qualified by each particular individual, is enough to account for those usages.

For the same reason, the Brahmasiddhi author's statement is sound: “Pots and the like are imagined in the form of existence, because each is apprehended as pervaded by it—like the moon reflected in each wave of water.” One might object that Brahman, the reality of existence, has no form, never transmigrates, is covered by ignorance, and is knowable only through words; how, then, could it be the object of the cognition “the pot exists”? When someone says “the pot is impermanent,” the cognition concerns impermanence in the pot. Likewise, one might argue, “the pot exists” must concern an existence belonging to the pot itself.

Nor is this like seeing Rāhu only when it is situated next to the moon. If Brahman became perceptible only by being delimited by a pot or another object, then space, when delimited by sound, should be audible too. Rāhu is said to become visible because its blue form, which is otherwise concealed by distance, is made perceptible through its relation to the bright white moon.

**Reply.** Brahman is not covered by root ignorance in its nature as existence; it is covered only by ignorance of the power associated with pots and other objects. When a cognition produced by the senses removes that covering, there is no obstacle to Brahman's shining forth in the cognition “the pot exists.” Nor does Brahman's being without form make visual perception impossible. Form is required only for objects apprehended by a particular sense. Brahman, as existence, is apprehended by every sense. Other schools also accept that existence is perceived by all the senses. As the author of the Vārttika says:

> “Therefore experience alone is the object, whose character is not otherwise known;  
> where the senses and the other means of knowledge are self-established.”

Mīmāṃsakas and others likewise accept that time, although without form, is perceived by every sense. One might again object that space, when delimited by sound, would then be audible. No: a capacity that belongs to something by nature, but is obstructed by a particular cause, can be brought into operation by a delimiting condition. Rāhu's capacity to be seen, obstructed by distance, is made effective through its relation to the moon. Similarly, Brahman's capacity to be perceived is obstructed by a covering and becomes effective when Brahman is delimited by pots and other things. Space, however, is not naturally capable of being heard and then merely obstructed; sound cannot make it audible by delimiting it.

Alternatively, sight requires form when it apprehends a substance, but not when it apprehends other kinds of things. Brahman is not a substance: scripture denies the four kinds of dimension when it describes Brahman as “neither gross nor minute, neither short nor long,” thereby denying that Brahman is a substance. So there is no difficulty. Even if Brahman is taken to be a substance, sight requires form only in a substance whose level of reality is lower than that of its locus. We observe that qualities in which substancehood has been superimposed can be seen without form. Any substancehood in Brahman would likewise have a lower level of reality than Brahman itself, so form is not required to apprehend it. The separate question of what it means for something to be imagined—whether it is apprehended where its own nature is absent, or whether it is removable by knowledge of its own nature—does not affect this argument.

Thus boundedness, too, has been established as a reason for falsity. **This concludes the argument establishing boundedness as a reason.**



### Establishing part-hood as a reason

**Source heading:** The Advaita Śāradā text does not give this unit a separate opening heading; its closing colophon reads इत्यंशित्वहेतूपपत्तिः (“this concludes the argument establishing part-hood as a reason”). E-Bharati labels it अंशित्वादिहेतुभङ्गः (“refutation of part-hood and related reasons”). E-Bharati also splits the compound इतरांशिवत् in its transcription; Advaita Śāradā reads it as one word.

Chitsukha says: “This cloth is the counterpositive of an absolute absence located in these threads, because it has parts, like another composite.” Here “threads” means the cloth's material cause. This would establish falsity as being the counterpositive of an absolute absence located in one's own material cause.

**Objection.** Since the effect is not different from its cause, the cloth's dependence on the threads is already established. The proof would therefore be redundant. Or, if the cloth is not dependent on those threads, the proof would establish a different point.

**Reply.** That does not follow. If effect and cause were wholly identical, the relation of cause and effect would be impossible; some difference between them must be accepted. Nor does the Brahma Sūtra passage “It is non-different from that, because of the words about its arising” contradict this. Its meaning is simply that an effect does not exist apart from its material cause. Alternatively, the subject can be qualified as an effect whose dependence solely on that cause has not been sublated; this does not shift the point to be proved. Nor is the present case already sublated: that objection will be answered later. The question whether absolute absence is established by a valid means of knowledge has also already been settled.

**Objection.** A cloth can be present in these threads through conjunction. The reason would then occur in a case where the cloth is not the counterpositive of an absolute absence located in the threads.

**Reply.** No. If one accepts that something inhering in the threads is the counterpositive of an absolute absence located there, one must accept the same of something conjoined with them. The proposed counterexample is therefore equal to the subject. Nor does this introduce a different point merely because the relation does not extend everywhere. If a cloth and its absence could occupy one and the same locus in the relevant respect, the distinction between the world and its absence would collapse. The opposing view does not accept that conjunction and its absence occupy the same locus in that way. If it did, the absolute absence would have to be qualified as occurring in threads delimited by this particular cloth and at this particular time. An absence at another time would then be no counterexample.

Nor does the direct perception “the cloth is in these threads” defeat the argument. Such a perception is compatible with error, just as the perception that the moon is only a handspan wide is. It therefore cannot by itself sublate the inference. The text will explain the alleged sublation in detail later.

**Objection.** The reason is contradictory: something that does not inhere in these threads cannot be a composite inhering in them.

**Reply.** There is no contradiction. A cloth can be a counterpositive of an absolute absence located in these threads and still inhere in them. Being the counterpositive of that absence is not what makes something inhere in the threads. Otherwise, on the opponent's view, every universally present property would inhere in these threads. What makes something inhere there is, rather, its being the counterpositive of a prior absence located in them, or a related condition; that is compatible with its also being the counterpositive of their absolute absence. And “inhering in these threads” means being their product, not being joined to them by an eternal relation, which the opponent does not accept.

**Objection.** One can counter this inference with another: “This cloth is not the counterpositive of an absolute absence located in these threads, because it is produced from these threads, unlike another cloth.”

**Reply.** This is not an unestablished qualification. In general, being the counterpositive of an absolute absence located in these threads is itself the counterpositive of an absolute absence located somewhere. It is pervaded by being the counterpositive of a relational absence, just as being the counterpositive of a prior absence is. One might object that the absolute absence of space is known to be pervaded by relational absence in a pot, while space, because it is all-pervading, is not the counterpositive of an absolute absence located anywhere. But there is no counterexample: space's all-pervading character belongs to a universal property that is not itself a counterpositive of absence. The absolute absence of space is therefore not pervaded by being the counterpositive of a relational absence.

The proposed limiting condition—that if a cloth is the counterpositive of an absolute absence in these threads, it must not have been produced from those threads—does not block the inference. In the negative pervasion, the opponent's challenge based on the non-counterpositivity of a prior absence is weak: the alleged condition does not occur in the subject, and so does not pervade the uncertain counterpositivity of the absolute absence. The argument will also show later that an opposing line of reasoning cannot establish the impossibility of perceptibility and the other proposed reasons. For the same reason, not being produced from these threads is not a limiting condition. A limiting condition must be removed when one proves the absence of the predicate, as explained above; and if conjunction is accepted as a relation that does not extend everywhere, that condition has counterexamples there. The opponent therefore has no established pervasion between being the counterpositive of the absolute absence in these threads and not being produced from them. The argument is sound.

Accordingly, Vidyāsāgara's inference is sound: “The disputed world does not exist apart from cognition, because it is not apprehended apart from cognition, like dream objects.” Here the predicate, “not existing apart from cognition,” is one of the forms of falsity stated earlier. The reason is that the world is apprehended only when reflected consciousness is present. There is no difficulty in the inference. Other formulations of the argument can be established in the same way, as appropriate. May this be auspicious. **This concludes the argument establishing part-hood as a reason.**



### Refuting the claim that perceptibility and similar reasons have limiting conditions

**Heading variant:** Advaita Śāradā gives the heading अथ सोपाधिकत्वनिरासः (“refutation of the claim that the reasons have limiting conditions”); E-Bharati titles the unit दृश्यत्वादेः सोपाधिकत्वम् (“perceptibility and similar reasons are limited by conditions”).

**Objection.** Reasons such as perceptibility have a limiting condition (*upādhi*) that makes the inference inconclusive. Two such conditions are proposed: either an object appears because of a defect that is not removed by what is taken to be that object's sublator; or the object is something to be denied by a sublating cognition that is itself not removed by what is taken to be the object's sublator. These conditions are absent from the countercases and coextensive with falsity. They would therefore provide a negative pervasion and count as limiting conditions.

**Reply.** No. They do not extend to every false thing. The identity of the body and the Self is false, yet it is removed only by knowledge of Brahman. But when that knowledge arises, it removes both the identity and the ignorance that caused it. The proposed conditions are therefore absent from this false case. Their alleged absence does not extend to falsity as a whole.

The reply also rejects the move of using a condition that merely excludes the subject of the inference, as though that were enough to establish an upādhi. The argument that the proposed conditions are present in the examples but absent in the subject would then amount to nothing more than defining the condition by its difference from the subject. The objection that sublation establishes the condition does not help: the claim that the world is sublated will itself be examined and refuted later.

A condition whose absence establishes the absence of the predicate must extend wherever that predicate is absent. Thus, in an argument that “space and the other elements are not false because they lack appearance caused by a defect that survives what is taken to be their sublator,” the phrase about the defect's surviving its alleged sublator is unnecessary. It is not what makes falsity absent. Appearance caused by a defect, by contrast, does extend to falsity; but it also extends to the reason being used, such as perceptibility. It is therefore not a limiting condition. Falsity is established by perceptibility and the other reasons themselves.

The same point applies to the second proposed condition: the phrase “by a sublator that is not itself removed by what is taken to be the object's sublator” is unnecessary when proving that falsity is absent. The remaining feature is shared by falsity and the reason for proving it, so it is not a limiting condition either.

For this very reason, being an object of a cognition produced by a defective cause at the same level of reality as the supposed substratum is a genuine limiting condition. Brahman, too, can be the object of a cognition—such as the cognition that it is momentary—produced by a cause that Buddhists regard as defective. The qualification “at the same level of reality as the substratum” is therefore needed to make the condition coextensive, not merely to exclude the subject of the inference. There is no defect of making the subject a special case that the proposed condition excludes. The imagined property of momentariness in Brahman is itself false, yet it is not the object of a cognition produced by a defect at the same level of reality as its supposed substratum. The condition therefore does not fail to extend to falsity.

Nor is being outside the purport of scripture a limiting condition. What scripture has as its purport is confined to Brahman; the absence of that property extends to the reason being used. Phenomenality is not a limiting condition either. Phenomenal status means being sublated by something other than knowledge of Brahman. The false identity of body and Self does not have that status, so this condition does not extend to all falsity. And being nothing but an appearance is no limiting condition: on the view that creation occurs only with perception, this property extends to the reason itself; other schools do not accept that view in the first place.

Thus perceptibility and the other proposed reasons have not been shown to depend on a limiting condition. **This concludes the refutation of the claim that those reasons are limited by an upādhi.**

**Translator's note:** In inference, an *upādhi* is a limiting condition alleged to break the necessary connection between a reason and what it is meant to prove. It must extend to the thing being proved while failing to extend to the reason.



### Distinguishing the inference from fallacious look-alikes

**Heading variant:** Advaita Śāradā gives the heading अथ आभाससाम्यभङ्गः (“refutation of resemblance to a fallacious inference”); E-Bharati calls the unit दृश्यत्वादेः आभाससाम्यम् (“the alleged fallacy of perceptibility and similar reasons”).

**Objection.** The proposed inferences resemble fallacies: “The disputed world is merely phenomenal because it is perceptible, like silver seen in a shell”; and “Brahman is false because it is an object of ordinary transactions,” or because it is “different from absolute non-being,” again like shell-silver.

**Reply.** No. Those arguments fail when tested against consequences that run against them. If the world's empirical reality were sublated, ordinary dealings with it would become impossible. And if Brahman were false, the result would be nihilism. Such contrary reasoning defeats the alleged fallacies; the opposing consequence in each case will itself be answered later in the appropriate discussion.

Moreover, “phenomenal” can mean either “sublated by something other than knowledge of Brahman” or “having nothing but appearance as its nature.” If it means the first, the false identity of body and Self is a counterexample: it is false, yet it is not sublated by anything other than knowledge of Brahman. The proposed reason is also not decisive. If it means the second, the inference is already established on the view that things are created only when perceived.

The same analysis applies if the claim to be proved is that Brahman is false. If the claim is that Brahman is false in a qualified or dependent sense, it is already established. If the claim is unqualified, the reason “being an object of ordinary transactions” is not established for Brahman. Even if one accepts that Brahman is an object of a mental cognition produced by Vedānta, that does not make the reason decisive.

Likewise, “being different from absolute non-being” is not established for Brahman. Here, non-being means being unfit to be apprehended as existent in any locus; being different from non-being would mean being fit to be apprehended as existent in some locus. That does not apply to pure Brahman. One cannot instead define non-being as being sublatable and its opposite as being unsublatable: using unsublatability to establish falsity would be self-contradictory, the shell-silver example would fail to establish the reason, and the resulting nihilism will be refuted later.

Thus the inferences from perceptibility and the other proposed reasons are not fallacious look-alikes. **This concludes the refutation of their alleged resemblance to fallacious inferences.**



### Defining reality while answering the objection from ordinary perception

**Source heading:** अथ प्रत्यक्षबाधोद्धारे सत्त्वनिर्वचनम् (“Defining reality while answering the objection from direct perception”).

**Scope note:** This draft translates the first marked argument in the source, through the rejection of “general-characteristic” perception. The original then begins a separate marked discussion, अथ साक्षिबाधोद्धारः, on witness-consciousness, followed by its concluding verses; that part is still to be translated.

**Objection.** Direct perceptions such as “the pot exists” defeat the inference from perceptibility and the other proposed reasons. They show that the world is real.

**Reply.** No. The kind of reality that opposes falsity and is eligible to be directly perceived by the eyes and other senses has not been defined. It cannot simply mean being the object of valid cognition, being fit to be such an object, or not being the object of error. Each of these definitions relies on valid or erroneous cognition that the senses cannot establish, and each faces further problems.

Reality cannot mean being the object of valid cognition. A means of knowledge does not operate on what is absent, so the existence of its object would have to be established before the valid cognition that is supposed to define it. And if valid cognition is itself defined as cognition of something real, defining reality through valid cognition and valid cognition through reality would be mutually dependent. Moreover, even the illusory appearance of silver in a shell is admitted to be the object of valid cognition when it is decisively apprehended as something to be denied.

Nor can reality mean merely being fit to be an object of valid cognition, since that fitness has not been explained. And it cannot mean not being an object of error: that has not been established, because anything can be mistaken to be momentary, for example. For the same reason, reality cannot mean being an object of valid cognition that is not qualified by nonexistence; that definition is mutually dependent on the reality it is meant to define. Nor can it mean being the object of a valid cognition in which existence itself is the qualifier, since that makes the definition depend on itself. Nor is reality the property of being an object of an erroneous cognition qualified by nonexistence; that, too, involves mutual dependence. Finally, reality cannot mean that, in the locus where something is presented, there is no denial of its existence in all three times. That definition also depends on itself.

Reality is not simply the universal property of existence, the ability to produce practical effects, or difference from absolute non-being. None of these is incompatible with falsity; directly perceiving one of them therefore cannot defeat an inference to falsity. Nor is reality the absence of the falsity meant by Vedānta: that would wrongly include what is utterly nonexistent, such as a hare's horn. Nor is it “being different from non-being while not being superimposed.” To be not superimposed would mean not being an object of superimposition, but that is impossible: everything may be presented as momentary and so on.

One might define reality as being, at some time, directly apprehended by a valid cognition in which existence is the qualifier, or as being related to time. But existence in that definition means presentness, not reality, so it does not avoid the earlier circularity. Past things, too, were present at some time, so the definition does not leave them out. And the superimposed is shown through sublation to be unrelated to all three times, so this definition does not overextend in the other direction. Still, being an object of a valid cognition qualified by presentness does not oppose falsity: an appearance of silver in a shell is related to the time when it appears. Sublation denies that it is truly related to all three times, but it does not deny its apparent relation to a time. Nor can reality be defined as a *true* relation to time, since what “true” means has not yet been explained. If it is explained, the rest of the proposed definition becomes unnecessary.

**Objection.** On your own view, the world has the same reality as Brahman. As a verse says: “The sort of reality Brahman has should also belong to the world. If it is indeterminable there, let it be indeterminable here for us as well.” One might add that it is not the world's being unlimited that makes it real, because something utterly nonexistent could also be unlimited. Reality must be something else; and, like Brahman, the world and even a shell can be real because they serve as the substratum of error.

**Reply.** You must be like a bridegroom who, when the bride's father asks for his family lineage at the wedding, answers, “Whatever lineage you have is mine too.” If a bridegroom and his bride's father belonged to the same lineage, the marriage could not go ahead. In the same way, if the world and Brahman had exactly the same reality, the world would have to be unreal.

Brahman's reality is its nature as self-luminous, non-dual consciousness. If the inert world had that same reality, it would be false by its very nature, just as silver is false because its appearance conflicts with the shell that underlies it. The world's inertness conflicts with self-luminous consciousness. And it is consciousness alone that is accepted as the substratum of every error, because both limited and unlimited ignorance have consciousness as their object. The fact that a shell or another thing serves as the basis of an illusion does not establish that thing as ultimately real.

**Objection.** Even so, we say that non-being is being the counterpositive of a denial that holds in every place and at all three times. This applies both to what is utterly nonexistent and to what is indeterminable. Reality is the absence of that non-being, and it belongs to the world just as it belongs to Brahman. There is no under-coverage in the case of conjunction, because conjunction is accepted as not extending throughout its locus; if necessary, qualify the absence as one that does extend throughout its locus. Nor is there under-coverage in the case of space. We do not accept that the absolute absence of space is present everywhere; if we did, we could qualify absence as having a counterpositive that occurs in a locus.

**Reply.** No. A reality defined through these many objects, none of which is accessible to the senses, cannot itself be grasped by the senses. No one directly perceives being the counterpositive of a denial that extends throughout every place and all three times, with a counterpositive that occurs in a locus, and so no one could directly perceive the absence of that relation either. Even if the requirement that the counterpositive occur in a locus is dropped, “everywhere” and “at all three times” are still not suitable objects of sensory perception.

**Objection.** If one perceives the absence of a denial that is located here and now, then one has perceived that the object is not the counterpositive of that denial. Since “here and now” are included among all places and all times, that cognition includes the object's not being the counterpositive of the denial that holds everywhere and in all three times. The falsity defined by being the counterpositive of that all-place, all-time denial could therefore be apprehended without inference.

**Reply.** No. Being the counterpositive of every denial located in one's own place and time is not accessible to the senses; the absence of that relation is even less accessible. And being the counterpositive of some denial in one's own place and time does not conflict with falsity. Nor can one define reality as not sharing a locus with one's own absolute absence: that relation is not established for properties that are present wherever their locus is, or for a pot when the relevant relation is changed. The alternative definitions—whether the object fails to occur in every locus of its own absolute absence or fails to occur in any such locus—run into the same problems already described.

Since no other account can be defined and the accounts rejected so far do not oppose falsity, reality must be defined as not being the counterpositive of any absolute absence that shares its locus. As already explained, this kind of reality is not fit to be apprehended by the senses.

**Objection.** In perceiving a denial that occurs in some particular place and time, we know it as a denial located here and now. That connection can bring all denials of things in one's own place and time to mind. A pot can then be perceived as not being the counterpositive of all those denials; so the counterpositivity of the denial that holds everywhere and at all times is not difficult to perceive.

**Reply.** No. Even if a general-characteristic connection brings all those denials to mind, the senses cannot apprehend the pot's absence of counterpositivity to them. An absence is perceptible only when its counterpositive is perceptible; the counterpositivity of every denial is not an object of the senses.

In fact, a universal is not a connection through the senses; there is no valid means of knowledge for such a connection. One might object that, unless the senses make contact with the universal “smoke,” a person could not learn the pervasion between smoke and fire in a kitchen and then infer fire from smoke on a mountain. If “smokeness” is the sensory connection, however, it should make the mountain smoke available in the kitchen as well.

That does not follow. When the senses make contact with smoke on the mountain, a remembered pervasion understood under the qualifier “smoke” can be combined with that particular perception. The materials for a qualified cognition are complete, as when one recognizes “fragrant sandalwood” by perceiving the sandalwood and already knowing its fragrance. Or the memory of the pervasion, in the form in which it was learned, can serve as the reason for recognizing that the subject has the property. Only the smoke in the kitchen, apprehended as smoke, is the object of the remembered pervasion. The mountain smoke is also known as smoke, but this occurs without a general-characteristic connection. That is enough for the inference.

**Objection.** Without a general-characteristic connection, one could not have the doubt “Is smoke, as smoke, invariably connected with fire, or not?” The smoke whose connection with fire is known is already settled; smoke that is not known cannot be doubted.

**Reply.** Even when the pervasion has been settled for a particular instance of smoke, a doubt can still arise about smoke under the general description “smoke.” As the author of the *Maṇi* says, even when something is certainly distinguished from other things under the description “pot,” a doubt or desire to establish it can still arise when it is considered under the description “earth,” and so forth. A doubt about whether smoke, as smoke, is connected with fire can arise even when the smoke in a particular place is known to be fire-pervaded; uncertainty about the validity of that knowledge can also support such a doubt. The same explanation applies to the doubt “Does air have form?”

**Objection.** Desire arises for something not yet attained, and it is produced by cognition of something in the same class. Such a cognition requires a general-characteristic connection. One might say that knowledge of happiness in an already known object can produce desire for an unknown happiness, since having the same qualifier is enough. But then, when silver is experienced under the description “silver,” there should be desire for silver in a pot and similar things. If the object that bears the qualifier must also be the same, then an illusion of silver in a shell should not produce desire for silver. On the theory that one thing is mistaken for another, the memory of silver alone should prompt the desire.

**Reply.** Desire for silver does not arise toward the shell itself; on our account, it arises toward the indeterminable silver that appears there. This will be explained later. The view that the silver is merely remembered and mistakenly transferred to the shell is also set aside. Error is due to the object being sublated, not to a qualifier being attached to a different locus; that too will be explained later.

**Objection.** Knowledge of an absence is produced by knowledge of its counterpositive. Without a general-characteristic connection, one could not directly perceive the absence of every bright light, since one would have no access to all of its counterpositives.

**Reply.** On our view, darkness is a positive entity, not merely an absence. The absence of bright light is manifested by darkness itself, without requiring prior knowledge of its counterpositive. Even on other views, which require cognition of the counterpositive, it is enough to know the property that delimits counterpositivity; a general-characteristic connection that makes every counterpositive available is not required.

Nor does knowledge of the absence of cowhood require a general-characteristic connection. Any particular cow can supply the relevant instance of cowhood. The same explanation applies to the perception of a prior absence. And if knowledge of a future event is needed to make a doubt possible, that knowledge can arise from inference. The *Nyāyakusumāñjali* says:

> “If there is doubt, inference is certainly possible; if there is no doubt, how could it arise?  
> Doubt extends as far as contradiction; reasoning is held to end where doubt ends.”

For instance, one can infer that present cooking began before the present moment because it is cooking, as with cooking in the past. The final stage of cooking is no counterexample: an awareness of a deviation that depends on the proof of the conclusion does not defeat the proof. Otherwise proving and disproving the same thing would conflict. Cognition concerning all smoke, cooking, and similar things can also arise from words. One cannot insist that a word must be recalled before a doubt arises, since a word previously heard can be remembered later with the meaning it had when it was correctly understood.

**Objection.** Since inference is produced by knowledge of a qualifier, the general-characteristic connection must be accepted. Otherwise, an inference whose subject is a qualified object could not arise from knowledge of the qualifier.

**Reply.** No. An inference about an object qualified by the probandum can arise from knowing the property that delimits the qualifier; a second inference is not needed, and there is no regress. The supposed special connection through a cognition, introduced to explain a judgment such as “fragrant sandalwood,” is also unnecessary: the fragrance can be inferred from sandalwood. If one instead allowed direct perception of an object already qualified by what is to be proved, inference as a whole would be undermined.

One might also argue that, in an inference based only on negative concomitance with absence as the probandum, there is no positive instance establishing that probandum, so the accepted materials for inference must be stronger than those for perception. We do not accept that argument. If one accepts an inference from considering that the smoke on a mountain is pervaded by fire, there is no difficulty in an inference in which the probandum qualifies the mountain. There is no authority requiring the subject of an inference to be its principal object.

A universal such as “smokeness” is not itself a sensory connection. If it were, after mistaking a cloud of dust for smoke, one would immediately apprehend the pervasion between fire and every instance of smoke under the universal “smokeness.” But the cloud does not have the nature of smoke. One cannot simply accept this result: then the later inference would not arise. Nor can knowledge of smokeness itself be the required sensory connection, since it does not arise before one perceives smoke. There is no evidence that a qualified cognition, or knowledge of its qualifier, must be produced in this special way; ordinary dealings can be explained by the causal relations that have already been established.

There is also no evidence that directly perceiving one instance of smoke as smoke produces a second, matching cognition of every unseen instance. Experience is “I directly see the smoke here,” not “I directly see every instance of smoke in the world.” Even those who rely on experience do not accept that conclusion.

If a general-characteristic connection were accepted, it would lead to omniscience. From the pervasion “whatever is knowable is nameable,” and a corresponding pervasion between whatever is an object of knowledge and an object of linguistic expression, one would know every object and its name. But universal knowledge is not accepted. It would also make impossible doubts such as “Is this pot an object of another person's knowledge?” A settled cognition of the pot as a pot does not remove that doubt: the certainty concerns the pot under the qualifier “pot,” whereas the question of whether it is knowable concerns a different qualification. In the cognition “the pot is knowable,” pothood itself also appears as a qualifier, since whatever contributes the distinction that appears in a cognition functions as its qualifier. One cannot explain this by saying that the cognition “the pot is knowable” is generated by prior knowledge of pothood as a qualifier; that special causal role has already been rejected. Accepting a special relation of this sort would also lead to the theory that reality is indefinable, along with the other problems already discussed.

Thus the proposed use of a general-characteristic connection to make the absence perceptible does not work. This removes the claim that either ordinary or extraordinary perception defeats the inference to falsity by directly apprehending the reality opposed to it.

**Closing mark in source:** इति सामान्यप्रत्यासत्तिभङ्गेन लौकिकालौकिकप्रत्यक्षबाधोद्धारः (“This concludes the reply to the objection from ordinary and extraordinary perception by refuting the proposed general-characteristic connection.”)



### Answering the objection from witness-consciousness

**Source heading:** अथ साक्षिबाधोद्धारः (“Next, answering the objection from witness-consciousness”).

**Objection.** If direct perception grasps only what is present, how could the falsity of silver seen in a shell be directly known as its being the counterpositive of a denial that holds in all three times in the locus where the silver appears? One might say that the shell's nature, which contradicts silver, is directly perceived; from that, the absence of silver is necessarily established, and the cognition “the silver appeared falsely” is explained by the witness, which reveals every relation to its object. If so, the witness should also grasp directly, across all times, that the sky is not the counterpositive of a real future denial.

**Reply.** No. The witness reveals what is present; it cannot reveal the absence of a future sublation that does not yet exist. And witness-cognition is common to valid cognition and error, so it cannot by itself defeat a valid means of knowledge.

**Objection.** When the witness apprehends that a cognition is valid, it also apprehends that the pot and similar objects are not sublated. Validity cannot be known without including the fact that the object is not contradicted.

**Reply.** No. An action can be successful when its object is not sublated during ordinary transactions, and that is enough to account for the success. Even if the witness apprehends validity in that sense, there is no contradiction. What marks a cognition of a pot as the sort that leads to a successful transaction is not that its object is never sublated in all three times. It is that, unlike an appearance such as shell-silver, its object is not sublated during the period of ordinary activity. As already explained, that does not conflict with a later, real sublation. A future sublation—or its absence—cannot be known by the witness without a means of knowledge, because the witness reveals only what is present.

**Objection.** Then cognitions such as “I am the body” and “the water is hot” must also be valid, since their objects are not sublated during ordinary activity.

**Reply.** Until knowledge of Brahman arises, those cognitions share the same status in this respect as ordinary cognition of a pot. Their being uncontradicted during ordinary activity does not establish that their objects are ultimately real.

**Objection.** A sublating cognition that occurs later either denies the original object at the very time when the original cognition apprehended it, or denies it at a different time. In the first case, how could the original cognition have been valid? In the second, the later cognition establishes only that the object is impermanent.

**Reply.** We do not accept that the validity of the cognition of the world means that its object can never be sublated. We accept only that, during ordinary activity, no contrary valid cognition has arisen. The object is apprehended as existing at the time when it is apprehended, and it is not contradicted at that time; that is the validity we accept.

**Objection.** What distinguishes your cognition of a pot from a merely phenomenal cognition and establishes the pot's empirical reality should also establish its ultimate reality.

**Reply.** No. The difference between cognition of a pot and a phenomenal cognition consists in features such as the pot's being the object of a determinate cognition that is not contradicted during ordinary activity. That cannot establish ultimate reality. The cognition “I am the body” and an immediate error just before knowledge of Brahman provide counterexamples. The distinction establishes only empirical status.

**Objection.** There is no difference between the experiences “the pot exists” and “the silver is false.” How, then, can one distinguish “the pot is false” from “the falsity of silver is itself false”? If the latter too is false, silver would become ultimately real.

**Reply.** No. It has already been explained why the falsity of falsity does not make silver ultimately real.

**Objection.** If ultimate reality is not an object of direct perception, the scriptural statements that deny it would deny something that has never been presented.

**Reply.** Those statements deny what is presented by the senses. Even when they deny duality as ultimately real, the denied object can be present indirectly or as something one might suppose to be present. Scriptural prohibitions such as “Fire is not to be kindled in the open space” show that a negation need not be pointless simply because the prohibited thing has not actually been attempted.

**Objection.** If the world is not ultimately real, and direct perception does not present it as ultimately real, how can direct perception fail to reveal the truth about it? Everyone directly experiences the world as ultimately real.

**Reply.** We do not define truth-revealing cognition as cognition that apprehends an object under a qualifier that belongs to it as real, or falsehood-revealing cognition as cognition that apprehends something different from such an object. A cognition reveals truth when its object is not sublated; it fails to reveal truth when its object is sublated. Only the cognition of Brahman produced by scripture has an unsublated object, because the Veda has Brahman as its purport. Other cognitions do not have that status. Thus the perception of the world need not apprehend ultimate reality in order to fail to reveal the truth about it. The common belief that the world is ultimately real can be explained indirectly, just as the commonly accepted ability of water to quench thirst can be established through experience without being directly perceived as an ultimate fact.

Therefore:

> “Since the reality that is fit for direct perception has not been defined here,  
> direct perception cannot defeat the inferential sign of falsity.”

And:

> “Neither ordinary perception, nor perception produced by a general characteristic,  
> nor perception in the form of the witness defeats the sign that infers falsity.”

**Closing mark in source:** इति प्रत्यक्षयोग्यसत्त्वानिरुक्त्या प्रत्यक्षबाधोद्धारः (“This concludes the reply to the objection from direct perception, by showing that reality fit for direct perception has not been defined.”)



### Examining claims that perception is superior by kind and priority

**Source heading:** E-Bharati numbers this unit 1-1-20 and gives the heading प्रत्यक्षस्य ज्ञात्या उपक्रमादिन्यायैश्च प्राबल्यम्. Its body and closing colophon read जात्या (“by kind”), so that is the reading translated here. The closing colophon is प्रत्यक्षस्य जात्युपक्रमन्यायादिभिः प्राबल्यनिराकरणम् (“refuting perception's alleged superiority by kind, the rule of opening priority, and related arguments”).

In the cognition “the pot exists,” Brahman—the substratum—shines as “exists,” just as the “this” shines in the cognition “this is silver.” One cannot object that Brahman, which has no form, could not appear in visual or other sense-cognitions. Its appearance despite having no form has already been explained, by the same reasoning used for time and other things.

**Objection.** If so, in statements such as “the blue pot,” “the silver is false,” and “the hare's horn does not exist,” “blue,” “false,” and “nonexistent” should also reveal the substratum. One cannot say that blueness belongs to pots while existence does not. That would depend on first establishing that existence is superimposed, which is mutually dependent on the conclusion. Otherwise, in “reality, knowledge, infinity,” the word “reality” would also reveal a substratum.

**Reply.** No. The apparent co-reference of “existent” and “pot” is defeated by the word “pot.” Such co-reference might be grounded in one of three things: the shining of the universal property of existence; existence in the thing's own nature; or its being unsublated in all three times. But the universal property of existence cannot shine in the common cognition of things such as absences, since even you do not accept that it belongs to absences. Nor can the cognition “exists” be explained by saying that, in one case, existence is related directly and, in another, indirectly. A cognition with the same form cannot arise through relations of different kinds; otherwise the difference between those relations would not be established.

Nor can the cognition of existence in an absence or similar thing arise from that thing's own existence, since that would not provide anything common across the cases. If a feature that is not common could produce a cognition that is common, every universal would be unnecessary. For the same reason, the ordinary use of “exists” is not everywhere due to a thing's own intrinsic existence. When one thing common to all cases can explain the cognition of existence, there is no reason to posit many separate causes for it. And the cognition cannot be grounded in being unsublated in all three times, since that is not accessible to the senses, as already explained; it is also absent in an error such as “this is silver.” It is therefore reasonable that, in every case, the one Brahman that underlies everything is experienced as existence.

There is no such obstacle to “blue” and similar words being co-referential with “pot.” But blueness cannot be the substratum of the pot, because it did not exist before the pot. It would also be needlessly complicated to posit infinitely many distinct substrata for blue, yellow, and other particular qualities. Those supposed substrata would have the same status as the things they underlie, whereas a substratum must have a different, more stable level of reality than what is superimposed on it. Nor can falsity or nonexistence be the substratum in “false silver” or “the hare's horn does not exist”: that would lead to nihilism, whose difficulty has already been stated and will be discussed again. The same objection does not apply to “reality, knowledge, infinity”: there, existence belongs to Brahman in its own nature, not through a relation. But this does not mean that existence belongs to pots in their own nature; that has already been rejected.

Thus the substratum's presence in the direct cognition “the pot exists” has been explained.

Only a perception whose validity is certain can defeat another means of knowledge. But the validity of this perception is not certain: it conflicts with scripture and inference, and whether it will be sublated later is not known.

**Objection.** Perception itself is stronger and defeats inference and scripture. If perception is invalid only because it conflicts with them, while their validity depends on the absence of conflict with perception, their validity and invalidity depend on each other. There is no such mutual dependence when perception is valid, because perception does not depend on another means of knowledge.

**Reply.** No. Perception of the size of the moon, stars, and similar objects is known to be invalid because it conflicts with inference and scripture. Perception, too, requires the absence of a contradiction from other means in order for its validity to be established. Thus the two sides are on a par in this respect. When mutual conflict creates doubt about validity, once the validity of the Veda has been established through its being unauthored and other such considerations, it can defeat a perception that is already open to a defect. Where is the alleged circularity on our view? Otherwise, even the identity of body and Self would prevent scripture and inference from establishing that the Self is distinct from the body.

**Objection.** Perception is stronger by its very kind than inference and the other means. Otherwise, how could the perception of heat prevent an inference that fire is cold? Its power to defeat that inference does not depend on perception's being relied upon by inference: the subject and similar matters are established by sight, not by touch, so touch is not something on which inference depends. Perception is also stronger because it grasps lines and contours that inference does not grasp and removes confusion about direction that inference does not remove.

**Reply.** No. The perception of temperature through touch can block the inference that fire is cold precisely because inference relies on that perception. Even when sight establishes the subject, touch is needed to establish the property to be proved; without it, the probandum is not established. This supplies no evidence that perception is stronger by its kind.

Nor does perception's grasping things that inference has not grasped make it stronger. That would instead make indirect means of knowledge stronger, since they apprehend properties that perception has not apprehended. And the fact that perception removes confusion about direction that inference does not remove establishes only that the two means differ; it does not establish that one is stronger. Otherwise, sight would be stronger than touch because sight removes the mistaken perception of a bamboo as a snake that touch does not remove. Then a visual cognition of hills and hollows in a painting could not be corrected by touch, even when the tactile perception contradicts it. The rule that it is scripture which is superior by its kind over the three means is recalled in the verse: “The superiority by kind of scripture alone over those three is remembered.” One cannot restrict that rule to Vedic subject matter, since non-duality is also a Vedic subject. The statement “Where direct perception has arisen, it is defeated by inference and scripture” concerns perception whose validity has been examined.

**Objection.** Perception is stronger because no contradiction has yet arisen against it. A rule of ritual interpretation says: “When there is no contradiction, an explanatory passage is to be taken as it stands; if it conflicts with an injunction, the injunction is interpreted figuratively.”

**Reply.** That rule applies when the two passages are equally authoritative and depend on each other as parts of one sentence. In such a case, it is possible to decide that words at the opening of the Veda determine the scope of words at its conclusion—for example, that the concluding words referring to hymns are to be understood as referring to the whole Veda. The rule does not apply here, because the two means of knowledge do not have equal authority. A perception whose status as valid knowledge is uncertain is weaker than scripture, whose authority has been established and which is not like an error. Otherwise the illusion “this is silver” would be stronger than a reliable person's statement “this is a shell.”

Nor does perception become stronger merely because it is faster than inference, just as one might claim that scripture is faster than inference. The rule “In the world, perception and inference are strong or weak according to whether they proceed quickly or slowly; likewise scripture and inference” does not settle the issue. Even a means of knowledge that proceeds slowly can be stronger once its authority has been examined.

**Objection.** A particular statement can be stronger than a general one. For example, “He offers in the Āhavanīya fire” is stronger than “He offers at the station,” because the former has a more specific subject. In the same way, perception, which apprehends the existence of a particular pot, should be stronger than scripture, which generally denies duality.

**Reply.** The rule about particular and general statements applies only when the authority of both is certain. Otherwise, the same reasoning would make a particular cognition such as “this is a cow-horse” stronger than the general denial “there is no cow-horse.”

**Objection.** In another ritual rule, the words “whatever precedes the Agniṣomīya rite” refer to a specific ritual and do not apply generally. They are narrowed by the unrestricted instruction “Recite in the Dīkṣaṇīya rite for as many words as one wishes to use.” If an unrestricted but limited statement can be narrowed by a later statement that applies without exception, why could scripture not be narrowed in the same way by perception, which applies without exception?

**Reply.** No. The non-duality scripture's purport has already been established by the indicators of purport, such as its opening and conclusion, and so it applies without exception. Perception, by contrast, has room to apply because it concerns duality at the empirical level. Where two contradictory means of knowledge—such as the perception “I am a human being” and the scriptural statement “the Self is eternal and all-pervading like space”—cannot both be ultimately valid, one must accept that one has empirical authority and the other ultimate authority. Neither needs to be wholly invalid. If the non-duality scripture can be valid at the empirical level, then the perceptions that grasp duality are ultimately authoritative. If it cannot, then the non-duality scripture must be ultimately authoritative, and perception is valid only at the empirical level. Those who understand the argument may decide.

**Objection.** In a fifteen-day ritual, on the first day called Agniṣṭut, the features of the one-day Agniṣṭoma—including the Subrahmaṇyā recitation—are imported by the name. That limited recitation is then defeated by the broader Subrahmaṇyā recitation prescribed for the fourteen-day rite, because it would be unreasonable to defeat a broader rule with one that applies to fewer cases. Why, then, should the apparent meaning of the non-duality text not be defeated by the many means of knowledge that grasp duality—perception, inference that depends on perception, ritual texts, and statements prescribing meditation on Brahman with attributes? It is said: “When one cognition is opposed by many means of knowledge, the one alone is seen to be invalid, as in the cognition of silver in a shell.”

**Reply.** The example is different. There, one cognition is defeated by many means of knowledge with broader scope. Here, the non-duality scripture has a unique purport, and for that reason it is stronger. Although the identity of body and Self may appear through perception, inference, and pseudo-scriptural passages, a statement whose sole purport is to reveal the difference between body and Self is stronger. Likewise, the non-duality scripture has a unique purport and is stronger. There is no contradiction once one distinguishes knowledge from ignorance and the person who knows from the person who does not.

**Closing mark in source:** इति प्रत्यक्षस्य जात्युपक्रमन्यायादिभिः प्राबल्यनिराकरणम् (“This concludes the refutation of perception's alleged superiority by its kind, the rule of opening priority, and related arguments.”)



### Examining perception's alleged priority because other means rely on it

**Source heading:** प्रत्यक्षस्य उपजीव्यत्वेन प्राबल्यम् (“Perception's superiority because other means of knowledge rely on it”).

**Objection.** Even if perception is not superior to inference and scripture by its kind, it is superior because it is relied upon by them. To be relied upon means to grasp every part of the object that inference and scripture require, whether directly or indirectly. It is also seen that a means which grasps only the particular part that another means requires is relied upon by it; when the dependent means grasps something contrary, it is defeated. For example, in an inference that a pot is all-pervading, sight grasps the subject. In an inference about the impurity of a human skull, scripture supplies the predicate. In an inference about the extent of the mind, another inference grasps the reason. If this is true even when a means grasps only the required part, how much more should a means that grasps every required part defeat a means that grasps something contrary to it?

Even if sight does not grasp words or the validity of cognitions produced by words, it is still relied upon, because it belongs to the same class as hearing and the witness, which do grasp them. We also see that a scriptural text conveying the impurity of a human skull is stronger than an inference to its purity, because it belongs to the same class as the scriptural testimony on which that inference relies.

**Reply.** No. There is no conflict with what is relied upon. What is relied upon is not defeated. What is defeated is the object's supposed ultimate reality; that is not relied upon, because it plays no part in producing the cognition. As the verse says:

> “When the prior relation fixes what serves as the cause, our positions are equal.  
> To argue about the reality or unreality outside that causal function is pointless.”

**Objection.** If being relied upon means simply grasping something required by another cognition, and that is what gives it the power to defeat the other cognition, then the error “this is silver” is relied upon by the cognition “this is not silver”: the latter requires apprehending the silver whose absence it asserts. How, then, could the cognition “this is not silver” arise against it?

**Reply.** The cognition of absence is generated by cognition of the counterpositive as an object of cognition; it does not depend on that cognition's being valid. Assuming that validity too must be relied upon would be needlessly complex. We also see cognition of absence arising from erroneous cognition of its counterpositive. Therefore a cognition contrary to the silver-error can arise. The same applies here: inference depends on perception for cognition of the subject, not for the perception's validity.

**Objection.** What is relied upon is simply what cannot arise without it. The silver-error is therefore relied upon by the cognition of its absence, though it is not stronger for that reason. Mere reliance does not give superiority; only a means whose validity has been examined is stronger. Examination consists in successful practical action, the absence of contradictory cognition, and the absence of defects. In the present case, perception has been examined: the cognition “the pot exists” produces another cognition that distinguishes it, it supports practical activity concerning the pot, and no defect such as great distance is present. In the same way, the scripture that denies difference between the individual and the Lord, together with the witness-cognition, has been examined and is free from defect. Those means should not be defeated.

**Reply.** No. Examination consists in agreement with practical action, the absence of contradictory cognition, and the absence of defects. It establishes only that the object is not sublated in the same place and time as the cognition—just as smoke establishes fire in that same place and time. It therefore establishes validity only for the period of ordinary activity, a status shared by the identity of body and Self. Why, then, could scripture and inference not operate to apprehend the absence of absolute unsublatability? The distinction between trust in a valid means and its semblance, and the distinction between the individual and the Lord, are therefore empirical. The whole world is false.

**Objection.** If perception is invalid, the pervasion it establishes is invalid; and because inferable things and the validity of inference have the same status as that pervasion, they too are invalid. Otherwise, smoke superimposed on vapor, together with a merely phenomenal pervasion, could establish a real or empirical fire. Smoke supported by an empirical pervasion could establish an ultimately real fire. In the same way, since the world and the difference between the individual and the Lord are not contradicted in ordinary activity and have opposite properties, their ultimate reality would be established.

**Reply.** That argument would establish only that an inferable thing has the same level of reality as the pervasion and similar parts of the inference. But there is no rule that it must. We see a valid inference to a fire that is present even when the reason is fallacious. Mīmāṃsakas also accept that true verbal cognition can be produced by eternal, all-pervading phonemes even though they are associated with false features such as shortness and length. And in a pot whose locus is qualified by the prior absence of smell, one can infer a false smell from the real pervasion associated with earth. We also infer an original object from its reflection.

One might say that the inference from a reflection concerns only the original object's having come before it, and that the reason is real because the absence of a reflection is connected to the absence of its original. But “connected” here does not mean that one absence causes the other; absences do not cause each other. It means that one is pervaded by the other. On that account, the reason would be the counterpositive of an absence that is pervaded by the absence of the original; that reason would also occur in space and similar cases. So the inference from a reflection to an original proceeds from the reflection itself, not from a rule that the inferable thing must have the same level of reality as its pervasion.

This also answers the objection that a word's meaning must have the same level of reality as the word's fitness to express it, because a sentence's meaning and its fitness are necessarily co-real. In Vedānta, the non-dual meaning of the sentence and its fitness—the absence of contradiction in that meaning—are both unsublated. And the ultimate reality of the word, the cognition it produces, and that cognition's validity is not what determines whether the object is ultimately real. On your own view, an unreliable statement such as “this is silver” and the mistaken cognition it produces may be real as speech and cognition, although their object is not real. The reality of a word and its cognition does not make their object real.

Nor does the falsity of a cognition's validity entail the falsity of its object, as the cognition of shell-silver might seem to suggest. In a non-conceptual cognition that is neither valid knowledge nor error, the object is not thereby sublated. What makes a cognition invalid is something such as its presenting an object as having a property it lacks; that is not the case here. One might say that if validity means being a cognition of an unsublated object, then the falsity of that relation makes the object false as well. But the falsity lies in the relation expressed by “being the object of an unsublated cognition,” not necessarily in the object. A composite can be false in one respect and true in another: when the staff of a staff-bearing man is shown to be absent, the man himself is not thereby sublated.

**Closing mark in source:** इति अद्वैतसिद्धौ प्रत्यक्षस्योपजीव्यत्वभङ्गः (“This concludes the refutation, in the *Advaita Siddhi*, of perception's alleged superiority because it is relied upon by other means.”)

**English translation**

Madhusūdana says: An inference supported by reasoning that rules out the contrary case can also overturn perception.

An opponent objects: “Even so, this would uproot the whole Mīmāṃsā system. Perception apprehends three Vedic statements: ‘After touching the udumbara wood, one should chant’; ‘With the Aindrī hymn, he attends to the household fire’; and ‘The sacred strewing material is made of śara reeds.’ Yet each would be overturned by an inference: the first by the inference from the rule in a smṛti that all the udumbara wood must be wrapped around; the second by the inference, from the force of the mantra ‘You never take a share, Indra, from the giver,’ that the offering belongs to Indra as a remainder; and the third by an inference from the ritual indication that a Vedic injunction prescribes kuśa grass.”

Madhusūdana replies: No. These cases are different. What exactly is being claimed? Is it that the perceptual cognition of the three Vedic statements is overturned by inference, or that the three statements apprehended by perception are themselves overturned? The first claim does not work: there is no contradiction that would make one of these cognitions the sublator and the other the sublated, and we do not accept that such a relation is at issue here. If this is merely an objection to a point that has not been asserted, it amounts to an irrelevant challenge. For the same reason, the second claim also fails. Even if a scriptural discussion treats those three statements as being overturned by an inferential indication, there is no contradiction in saying that perception is overturned by an inferential reason. A verbal statement and the perception of that statement are not the same thing. We have already maintained that verbal testimony is stronger than every other means of knowledge. So the charge that this position contradicts Mīmāṃsā is simply confusion.

The opponent raises a second objection: “If perception can be overturned by an inferential mark, then the perception that fire is hot could not defeat an inference that fire is cold; nor could the recognition that the self endures through time defeat an inference that it is momentary. The inference, instead, would overturn those perceptions.”

Madhusūdana replies: No. In those cases, perception is stronger and defeats the inference because it is confirmed by successful practical activity and supported by scripture. An untested perception is weaker than an inference that has been tested, just as the perception ‘the sky is blue’ is weaker than the inference that the sky has no color. Thus, the mere existence of an inference from a general feature does not make all means of knowledge collapse into conflict.

The opponent continues: “Then one could infer that every animal has horns from the general property of being an animal. The simpler account makes animalhood itself the condition for having horns, rather than a particular kind of animal such as a cow; positing a separate, unshared condition for each kind would be needlessly complex. Since this reasoning supports the inference, it should outweigh perception. If favorable reasoning gives an inference its strength, then anything whatever could be inferred everywhere from any general property, by supporting it with a reasoning from simplicity. Since such an inference would overturn perception, even the ordinary order among means of knowledge would disappear. There is no other fault here besides its conflict with perception.”

Madhusūdana answers: No. An inference cannot establish horns or anything else that is impossible for the subject to possess while overcoming perception. You too must therefore admit that, in such a case, the reasoning meant to establish the universal relation is fallacious. The ordinary order of knowledge is thus protected on either account: no one accepts an inference as valid when it is supported by fallacious reasoning. It is therefore established that an inference accompanied by sound reasoning can overturn perception.

**English translation**

Madhusūdana says: Perception too can be overturned by verbal testimony once that testimony has been established as a valid means of knowledge through examination.

An opponent objects: “If words could overturn perception, then Jaimini would have treated certain Vedic passages as invalid because they contradict what is directly seen. Instead, he explains them figuratively. For example, the passage ‘Therefore, by day only smoke from a fire is seen, not its flame,’ and the mantra ‘Aditi is heaven’ would otherwise be invalid because they conflict with perception. Jaimini therefore discusses figurative use, saying, ‘But it is a figurative statement,’ and ‘A figurative meaning is appropriate where a quality avoids contradiction.’ In the Mīmāṃsā discussion called the *Tatsiddhi-petika*, he likewise explains statements such as ‘The sacrificer is the strewing grass’ figuratively, on grounds such as establishing an intended result, class, resemblance, praise, abundance, an indicative mark, or association. You too would have no reason to interpret the words ‘that’ and ‘you’ in ‘That you are’ indirectly to avoid conflict with perception, since perception could simply be declared invalid when scripture opposes it. Nor can you answer that the non-dual scriptures remain literal because the usual indicators of purport—such as an opening and conclusion—show that they teach non-duality. A single such indicator, such as novelty, also appears in ‘The sacrificer is the strewing grass’; if one indicator alone determines purport, the others merely repeat it, just as, on your view, scripture merely repeats the difference already established by perception. Why, then, invoke a large collection of indicators?”

Madhusūdana replies: No. Perception that has been tested by a concluding passage, corroboration from other means of knowledge, successful practical results, and similar checks is stronger within ordinary experience. In that sphere, statements such as ‘Only smoke from the fire is seen by day,’ ‘Aditi is heaven,’ and ‘The sacrificer is the strewing grass’ can be assigned an indirect meaning because they conflict with such tested perception. Non-dual scripture, however, does not conflict with perception established in that way, so it can retain its primary meaning. Testing establishes only the practical validity of perception; the non-dual teaching does not overturn that practical validity. What it overturns is the claim that perception is ultimately valid, a claim that practical testing never established. If the first statements were taken literally, by contrast, the practical validity of perception would be contradicted. This is the distinction.

The opponent objects again: “It is wrong to say that the words ‘that’ and ‘you’ are interpreted indirectly to avoid conflict with perception. The purport is established as undivided by the six indicators of a text’s purport and by the overall trend of the teaching. Moreover, the attributes of omniscience that belong to the Lord and limited knowledge that belongs to the individual self cannot be combined as though they were one. An indirect interpretation is therefore needed to preserve the undivided cognition that is the teaching’s intended purport. We see such interpretation used everywhere to preserve the intended connection of a sentence. You cannot say that this makes the sentence non-primary. A sentence is non-primary either when it abandons the meaning it presents and intends something else, or when it expresses something impossible. Neither applies here. The identity presented by the words in a common grammatical construction is not abandoned. Nor is the identity impossible: by an interpretation that sets aside one part while retaining another, as in ‘That is this Devadatta,’ the compatible part of the words’ meaning remains connected. Here, ‘interpretation’ names only the setting aside of limiting qualifications while retaining the subject itself. Vācaspati Miśra says that statements such as ‘The sacrificer is the strewing grass’ are non-primary because they serve another purpose, whereas the non-dual sentence is primary because it serves no other purpose. Śabara likewise says that in an injunction a word does not have an external object as its intended meaning. And we will explain later why statements such as ‘The sacrificer is the strewing grass’ do not intend their own literal meaning, even when a single indicator of purport—such as novelty—is present.”

The opponent continues: “Serving another purpose or serving no other purpose does not determine whether a statement is non-primary or primary; conflict or lack of conflict with another means of knowledge does. Even when a statement serves another purpose, if it does not conflict with another means of knowledge, no indirect sense is adopted. In ordinary speech, ‘This cow is for sale and gives much milk’ praises the cow, and the Vedic statement ‘He wept’ does the same, yet no one rejects their direct meaning merely because each serves an injunction. Conversely, even a statement that serves no other purpose may be interpreted indirectly when it conflicts with another means of knowledge. In ‘One should sacrifice with Soma,’ a direct grammatical connection would produce two incompatible constructions. The words are therefore connected by taking the injunction to mean a sacrifice performed with something that has Soma. This interpretation is used to avoid conflict with perception. Likewise, in the sentence that enjoins inquiry into Brahman—‘Now, therefore, the desire to know Brahman’—and in the Vedic statement ‘Seek to know that,’ the word ‘desire to know’ is interpreted as inquiry so that the command can be carried out without conflict with another means of knowledge. And in ‘All this is Brahman; it arises from, dissolves into, and lives by that,’ an indirect meaning is accepted. Since Brahman cannot be directly expressed by words, every sentence must ultimately use an indirect expression for it; so it is impossible to deny every non-primary use of language. The basis for interpretation—an impossibility in the sentence’s connection or in its intended purport—applies both to commands and to non-command statements. Śabara’s sentence only means that a passage of praise is not given a secondary sense merely to make an injunction’s own direct meaning primary.”

Madhusūdana replies: No; this objection confuses a word’s literal meaning with a sentence’s primary meaning. A sentence is primary when it conveys the meaning that the sentence intends to convey. It need not convey only the literal meaning of each word. A sentence is non-primary when its intended purport concerns a different meaning; the mere use of an indirect meaning for a word does not make the sentence non-primary. Thus, even when an interpretation is needed for the non-dual teaching to convey its own intended meaning, that teaching remains primary.

In an injunction such as ‘One should sacrifice with Soma,’ the qualifying expression is not itself the sentence’s principal purport; and because the intended action is retained, the sentence does not become non-primary merely because the words are interpreted as ‘a sacrifice performed with something that has Soma.’ There is a difference of opinion about whether the word ‘desire to know’ should be understood as the knowledge to be produced or as inquiry, but both sides accept an indirect interpretation in the verb form so that the command directs inquiry. Even though the literal meaning of part of the word is set aside, the sentence’s intended injunction is preserved. A sentence does not become non-primary whenever the meaning of one word must be adjusted to make the sentence intelligible. Its perceived meaning remains primary if it serves no further purpose. When, however, the sentence’s apparent meaning is itself made subordinate to another purpose, the sentence is non-primary.

A word’s purport and a sentence’s purport are different. “Bring the *saindhava*” can mean “bring the salt” or “bring the horse”; in “They live on the Ganges,” the word “Ganges” has its own meaning, while the sentence refers to living on its bank. In “Eat poison,” the words can retain their individual meanings while the sentence is intended ironically. So a sentence may have one purport even when its words have a different or more limited purport; and words may keep the same individual meanings while the sentence’s purport changes.

That is why “This cow is for sale and gives much milk” is a non-primary sentence: it is subordinate to the injunction “It must be bought,” and indirectly praises the cow. Likewise, “He wept” is subordinate to the rule, “Silver must not be given on the strewing grass; gold is the sacrificial fee,” and indirectly censures silver by recounting the weeping. The statement “All this is Brahman; it arises from, dissolves into, and lives by that” is subordinate to the injunction “One should meditate calmly”; it indirectly praises that meditation by presenting it as attainable with little effort. These examples show that a sentence can be non-primary because of its role in the larger instruction, even apart from any conflict with another means of knowledge. So it is wrong to say that indirect interpretation is required only when another means of knowledge contradicts a sentence.

Rather, all passages of Vedic praise use indirect interpretation to convey the meaning that serves the larger intended purpose. There is this distinction: when a statement praises an injunction, the reader first understands the statement on its own; if that apparent meaning is contradicted by another means of knowledge, it is overturned, as in “Prajāpati pulled out the fat from his own body.” That is why this is called a *guṇavāda*, a figurative statement of praise. When another means of knowledge has already established the meaning, the Vedic text merely restates it, as in “Fire is the remedy for cold”; it is an *anuvāda*, a restatement. In both cases, the passage lacks the defining validity of an independently informative, uncontradicted cognition: either it is contradicted, or it does not reveal something previously unknown. But where neither a prior cognition nor a contradiction is present, the statement can be a factual passage, as in “Indra raised the thunderbolt against Vṛtra.” This is the rule explained in the Mīmāṃsā discussion of deities.

The opponent asks: “Then ‘The sacrificial post is the sun’ would not be non-primary, since an indirect interpretation is accepted merely to understand its sentence meaning.” Madhusūdana replies: It would not be non-primary if the sentence ended with “The post resembles the sun.” Rather, even after that comparison is understood figuratively, the sentence still serves the injunction “He ties the animal to the post”: it praises the post and the rite. That subordinate praise is what makes the sentence non-primary, not merely the indirect use of the word “sun.” The discussion in the *Tatsiddhi-petika* gives several ways to understand the intermediate sentence meaning in these examples; the praise of the ritual action is common to all of them.

The opponent also claims that Śabara’s statement means there is no indirect interpretation in an injunction when the goal is to keep a passage of praise primary. That is incorrect. In the injunction used for a horse-acceptance rite, the form “He should accept” is understood as “He should cause another to accept,” by taking the verb to refer indirectly to the motivating action that makes the acceptance occur, in line with the passage of praise. Śabara’s point is instead that when a sentence is intended as an injunction, there is no separate purpose beyond the sentence’s apparent meaning to which that meaning is subordinated.

Therefore, although non-dual scripture may use indirect interpretation, it is primary because it conveys its own intended meaning and is not subordinate to another purpose. It can therefore overturn perception. This is established.

**English translation**

Madhusūdana says: Moreover, scripture is strong even by the rule of *apaccheda*, the setting aside of an earlier rule by a later one. In the discussion of the maxim “When there is a sequence, the earlier is weaker, as in the basic rite,” it was established that the later no-fee sacrifice, prompted by the absence of the *udgātṛ* (the lead chanter), overrides the previously established all-wealth fee, prompted by the absence of the *pratihartṛ* (the responding priest). Here too, a later scriptural passage can override earlier perception.

An opponent objects: “The rule prescribing an all-wealth fee when the *pratihartṛ* is absent has scope when only the *pratihartṛ* is absent, when both priests are absent at once, and when they are absent successively but the *pratihartṛ* is absent later. The no-fee sacrifice prompted by the absence of the *udgātṛ* may therefore properly override it in the case where that absence is later. Otherwise the scripture ‘If the *pratihartṛ* is absent, he should give all his wealth’ would be invalid. For this reason, in the discussion of ‘Because of conflict, there should be an option,’ an option is accepted when both absences occur together.

“Moreover, consider the discussion of: ‘If the *udgātṛ* is last to be absent, at the next sacrifice he should give all his wealth as in the other case.’ When the absence of the *udgātṛ* occurs after the absence of the *pratihartṛ*, the first performance of the required *Jyotiṣṭoma* is carried out without a fee, because the *udgātṛ* is absent. At the second performance, the scripture says, ‘He should give what he would have given at the earlier performance.’ Without cancelling the earlier intention to give all his wealth on account of the *pratihartṛ*’s absence in that first performance, the fee prescribed by the scripture is therefore all his wealth, not the ordinary fee of twelve hundred prescribed for the *Jyotiṣṭoma*. So the rule prompted by the *pratihartṛ*’s absence is not completely overridden; it is assigned to another performance. The *Tipṭīkā* says just this: ‘It is assigned to another performance.’

“Further, since the two causes arise in sequence, there can be, in sequence, both a performance without a fee and a performance with all one’s wealth as the fee. There is no conflict, just as a jujube fruit has first one color and then another—dark and red—when the causes for those colors occur in sequence. The discussion of the *Apaccheda* rule says: ‘The meaning of a rule for a conditional rite is this: although before the condition arises the rite would be performed differently, when the condition is present it must be performed in this way.’ Thus the *Apaccheda* rule has a case in which it applies. But when perception is overridden by non-dual scripture, there is no case left in which perception’s validity can apply.”

Madhusūdana replies: No. Just as the rule prescribing all one’s wealth as the fee still has scope at the second *Jyotiṣṭoma* performance when the *udgātṛ* is absent, both priests are absent together, or the *pratihartṛ* is absent later, so perception too has scope through its practical validity. There is a conflict about a single performance in the ritual example; here too there is a conflict, but only with respect to ultimate status. Therefore it has rightly been said that scripture describing Brahman with attributes and a manifested world is overridden by scripture teaching Brahman as without attributes and beyond the manifested world, by the *Apaccheda* rule.

Ānandabodha Ācārya says: “Because the Vedic statement has this purport, is later, and is free from defect, it overturns the earlier one, just as the sentence ‘This is not a snake’ does.”

The opponent objects: “When scripture conflicts with another means of knowledge, its intended purport is not established. And ‘being later’ fails in cases such as a later grammatical rule being defeated by an earlier one. We see the earlier rule ‘After the roots mṛḍ, mṛd, guḍ, kuś, kliś, vad, and vas, the affix *ktvā* takes the augment *iṭ*’ overriding the later rule ‘The affix *ktvā* does not take *iṭ*.’ Freedom from defect, meanwhile, is established in some other way. These three things cannot prove greater strength: purport is not established; laterness is not conclusive; and freedom from defect is established on another basis.”

Madhusūdana replies: No. Perception has practical validity, whereas scripture has ultimate validity; because these concern different levels, there is no conflict, and scripture’s purport is established. By “later,” we mean not overturned by another means of knowledge. There is therefore no counterexample in the case of a perceptual error: that error is overturned by knowledge that comes afterward. Although the grammatical rule “The affix *ktvā* does not take *iṭ*” comes later in the written sequence, it is earlier in its function, because unless it removes the *kit* status that is naturally present, the rule about the roots *mṛḍ*, *mṛd*, and the rest would not operate to restore that status. When an exception to an exception is stated, the general rule remains in force. Freedom from defect is not established on some unrelated basis either, because validity is accepted only for what a statement intends to convey. Thus, the claimed difference between this case and the *Apaccheda* rule comes only from a failure to understand the point.
